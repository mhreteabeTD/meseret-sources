## Introduction

Our planet earth is a home for all living things in general and human beings in particular. It is in a constant motion caused by internal and external forces. Humanity has been ceaselessly affected by these powerful forces of nature such as earthquake, volcanism, tsunamis, fires, and fault ruptures. The location of these forces is frequently associated with plate boundaries where plates diverge, converge and transverse. [review: "transverse" is the book's word; the standard term is that plates slide past each other (transform / conservative boundaries).]

(p.2)

Knowledge of the earth's movement and the forces that work behind is, therefore, highly desirable so as to predict the ground motion and the forces like earthquake and volcanism. Such a knowledge would enable humanity to minimize the damage from moderate forces, and to avoid total collapse during the occurrence of these forces.

We hope that you remember from the previous grades lessons about the geological history of Ethiopia and the formation of the earth's continents. This is a continuation of these lessons on related matters. This unit particularly deals with the theories of continental drift, plate tectonic, geological processes and the landforms caused by these processes.

## 1.1 THE CONTINENTAL DRIFT THEORY

At the end of this section, you will be able to:

- identify the continents that constituted Laurasia and Gondwanaland;
- explain the geological evidences that support continental drift theory;
- explain why Alfred Wegener continental drift theory was not easily accepted; and
- describe how continental drift theory and plate tectonics are related.

### Key terms

Continental Drift, Pangaea, Laurasia, Gondwanaland

The Continental drift theory presupposes that the earth's continents have moved over geologic time relative to each other; thus, appearing to have "drifted" across the ocean bed. The continental drift theory suggests that the present continents were formed from a single continent called PANGAEA. During the Carboniferous period, some 350 million years ago, Pangaea was located around the South Pole. [review: most sources date the assembled Pangaea to the late Carboniferous–Permian (about 335–300 million years ago); at 350 million years ago it was mainly Gondwana that lay over the South Pole. Keep the book's figure for exams but do not stress the exact date.]

In the Triassic period of Mesozoic era, Pangaea started to break into several parts. The northern part of Pangaea is known as LAURASIA. The southern part of Pangaea is known as GONDWANALAND. Both continents are further subdivided into the present continents. Through the process of drifting, the continents reached their present location. This process of continental drift as a whole took place over millions of years.

[note: The section outcome asks to "identify the continents that constituted Laurasia and Gondwanaland", but the text does not list them. Standard content for the lesson: Laurasia = North America, Europe and Asia (except India); Gondwanaland = South America, Africa (with Arabia), India, Australia and Antarctica (plus Madagascar).]

(p.3)

### Geological Evidences Favoring Continental Drift Theory

What evidences are there in support of continental drift theory?

**Answer:** Coastline (jigsaw) fit, similar rock types/ages and continuing mountain belts, fossil evidence (e.g. Mesosaurus), and paleoclimatic evidence.
**Why:**
- These are the four evidences Wegener used, listed on pp.3–5.
- Africa and South America fit together, especially at their continental shelves.
- Same rocks, same ages and same mountain belts continue across the Atlantic when the continents are fitted back.
- Identical fossils on both sides of the ocean and similar ancient climates recorded in rocks show the land was once joined.

The concept was proposed by a German meteorologist Alfred Wegener in 1912. One of the basic concepts that helped Wegener's continental drift theory the idea of a large united landmass consisting of most of the Earth's continental regions. [review: missing verb; read "…that helped Wegener's continental drift theory was the idea of a large united landmass…"] In his theory, Wegener introduced the supercontinent of Pangaea to explain the ancient climate similarities, fossil evidence and similarity of rock structures between Africa and South America, as well as the outlines of the continents, especially the continental shelves, which seem to fit together (Figure 1.2). Thus, Wegener used the following geological evidence, among others, to support his theory:

1. **Coastline Similarity:** The coastlines of Africa and South America have remarkable similarity in opposite side of the Atlantic Ocean. These coastlines fit together like pieces of a jigsaw puzzle.

(p.4)

[figure: Figure 1.2: Fitting of Africa and South America together — outline map with the bulge of north-east Brazil (South America) fitting into the Gulf of Guinea bend of west Africa; the continental shelves (lighter edge) are shown and they fit even more closely than the coastlines.]

2. **Rock type and structural similarities:** When we fit the continents of Africa and South America "back together again", we find that:
   - Similar rock types extend from one continent to the other
   - The rocks are also the same age.
   - Moreover, several mountain belts also extend from one continent to the next.

3. **Fossil Evidence:** cases of several fossil organisms had been found on either side of the Atlantic Ocean, implying that the continents were once joined together. Fossils of Mesosaurus are found on both sides of the Atlantic in South America and Africa (green shaded areas Figure 1.3). Mesosaurus was a small reptile that lived about 250 million years ago. [review: Mesosaurus is usually dated to the early Permian, about 299–270 million years ago; "about 250 million years ago" is a rounded/late figure. It was a small freshwater reptile — it could not have swum across the Atlantic, which is why its fossils are good evidence.]

[figure: Figure 1.3: Distribution of Mesosousarus [review: caption misspelt; should be "Mesosaurus"] — map of the South Atlantic with South America (left) and Africa (right); green shaded areas show where Mesosaurus fossils are found: south-eastern South America (southern Brazil, Uruguay, Paraguay, northern Argentina) and south-western Africa (Namibia, South Africa); a drawn skeleton of the long-snouted, long-tailed reptile is placed over each area.]

(p.5)

4. **Paleoclimatic Evidence:** similarity of climatic data reconstructed from rock structure are similar in the present continents. [review: wording is circular ("similarity … are similar"). Meaning: rocks record the same ancient climates on continents that now have different climates — e.g. traces of the same ancient glaciation (glacial deposits and scratches) in South America, Africa, India and Australia, and coal (from warm swamp forests) in places that are now cold — which makes sense only if these lands were once joined.]

Why was Wegener's continental drift theory not easily accepted? Do you know any other research findings that could be easily accepted by the scientific community?

**Answer:** It was not accepted mainly because Wegener could not explain the mechanism (what force moves continents) and had no direct evidence of movement; he was also not a geologist, the leading geoscientists were in the Northern Hemisphere while his strongest data came from the Southern Hemisphere, and his timing (break-up only in the Cenozoic) looked too short. Findings that were later accepted easily are those with direct evidence and a mechanism — for example sea-floor spreading at mid-ocean ridges and the plate tectonics theory of the 1960s.
**Why:**
- The four reasons are listed by the book directly below the question (p.5).
- Reason 4 — no direct evidence and no mechanism — was "the greatest problem".
- Plate tectonics (late 1960s) supplied the mechanism (plates moving on the asthenosphere, driven by mantle convection, pp.6, 10) and is "now universally accepted" (p.7).
- A theory is accepted when it has measurable evidence and explains how the process works.

Wegener's theory of continental drift was rejected by scientific community of the time. The main reasons for this rejection were:

1. Wegener was not a geologist by profession, which of course was most welcome by his opponents.
2. Most influential geoscientists at that time were based in the Northern Hemisphere, whereas most of the conclusive data came from the Southern Hemisphere.
3. Wegener thought that Pangaea did not break up until Cenozoic era, and scientists found it hard to believe that so much continental drift could have occurred in so short a time.
4. The greatest problem remained the lack of direct evidence for the movements of continents and the needed explanation for the mechanism.

[review: reason 3 seems to contradict p.2, which says Pangaea started to break up in the Triassic (Mesozoic). Both statements can stand — p.2 gives the modern view, reason 3 gives Wegener's own (later-corrected) view — but students should be told which is which.]

Regardless of the controversies mentioned above, perhaps Wegener's most important legacy is to have introduced the idea of lateral mobility of continents, that is, offering a paradigm change from fixism to mobilism to the scientific community and the public. Hence, the theory ignited a new way of viewing the Earth that led some scientists to start searching for an explanation of how continents could move.

Since Alfred Wegener's continental drift theory, geologists have developed a better picture of the Earth. According to plate tectonic theory, the layer, called the asthenosphere, allows the uppermost mantle and crust, together called the lithosphere to slide across the top of it. Earth's lithosphere is broken into about a dozen large pieces (plus some smaller ones), called plates (Figure 1.4). These plates move slowly at a rate of a few centimeters per year and change size. Plates may be entirely made up of continental rocks, both continental and oceanic rocks, or entirely of oceanic rocks.

Plate tectonics is a theory developed in the late 1960s to explain how the outer layers of the earth move and deform. The theory has caused a revolution in the way we think about the earth. Since the development of the plate tectonics theory, geologists have had to re-examine almost every aspect of Geology. The theory states that the earth's lithosphere is divided into plates that move around on the top of the asthenosphere. Continental crust is embodied within the lithospheric plate. The plates move in different directions, and meet each other at plate boundaries. Plate tectonics has been proven to be so useful that it can predict geologic events and explain almost all aspects of what we see on the earth.

The way the plates interact at their margins depends on whether the crust forming the top of the plate (at the point of contact) is oceanic or continental. Continental crust, made largely of granite, is less dense than oceanic crust made largely of basalt.

As the plates move, they may:

1. Converge, or come together; forming convergent (Destructive) boundary;
2. Diverge, or spread apart; forming divergent (Constructive) boundary or
3. Slide past each other along fractures called transform (Conservative) boundary.

### Convergent plate boundaries

These are boundaries where two plates move toward each other. At such boundaries, the denser plate will be forced under, or subducted beneath, the less dense one. The subducted crust is eventually destroyed. This happens when oceanic crust meets continental crust and when oceanic crust meets another oceanic crust. For example, the eastward-moving Nazca Plate, under the southeastern Pacific Ocean, is being subducted under the westward moving South American Plate. The denser oceanic crust is pushed down into the mantle, causing it to melt and produce magma (molten rock). The magma and gases rise to the surface, and are vented through the western crust of South America, forming the volcanoes of the Andes Mountains.

[figure: Figure 1.6: Convergence of plate boundary — 3-D block diagram: a thinner (oceanic) plate on the left moving right (arrow) bends down and slides beneath a thicker (continental) plate moving left (arrow); a red spot marks melting/magma above the sinking slab, with an arrow showing magma rising toward the surface where the overriding plate is raised into mountains.]

(p.9)

If two oceanic plates converge, subduction of one under the other may produce an arc of volcanic islands. The Mariana Islands of the western Pacific Ocean were formed this way.

Two blocks of continental crust may converge as well. The resulting pressure can deform the crust. If one block is denser, that block may slide under the other, mainly lifting the other plate rather than sinking too much itself. A good example of this is the convergence of the northward moving Indo-Australian Plate with the Eurasian Plate. Thus, as India met Asia, the collision produced the highest mountain range on Earth: the Himalayas.

### Divergent plate boundary

This occurs where plates move away from each other at oceanic ridges, such as in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. There, rising magma from underlying asthenosphere intrude and erupt beneath and at oceanic ridge to create a new seafloor. This pushes the plates on either side away from each other in opposite directions.

Undersea volcanoes and crust expanded by heating have produced a long undersea ridge, but with a rift in the middle where the plates are separating. This process is known as seafloor spreading. Shallow earthquakes are common at oceanic ridges.

[figure: Figure 1.7: Diverging Plates — 3-D block diagram: two plates (brown, green top surface) pulled apart by arrows pointing left and right; between them a central rift/valley dropped down, with magma (orange) rising up a vertical line beneath it; curved arcs in the mantle below show convection cells rising under the rift and turning outward.]

Divergence can also happen on continents, producing fractures called rift valleys. A modern example is the East African Rift Valley. Over millions of years, the continental crust may separate completely, with the area between flooding with water to become a new ocean.

### Transform Fault boundaries

In some places, plates slide past each other laterally along fractures in the crust. These places are called transform faults. The plates stick and then occasionally slip, producing earthquakes.

(p.10)

Most transform faults are found on the seafloor along the undersea mountain chains called oceanic ridges, but the faults also occur on continents, such as the San Andreas Fault in California (Figure 1.8). As plates move past each other along transform faults, crust is neither destroyed nor created.

[figure: Figure 1.8: Transform Fault — two panels. Left: map of the California/Pacific north-west coast labelled "Oceanic Ridge" (offshore, short ridge segments), "Oceanic Trench" (offshore north), "Seattle", "Cascade Mountains (Volcanic Arc)", "San Andreas Fault", "San Francisco", "Los Angeles" and "Transform Faults"; two opposite black arrows along the San Andreas Fault show the Pacific side moving north-west and the North American side moving south-east. Right: 3-D block cut by a vertical fault, the two halves offset sideways with a red dot (earthquake focus) on the fault plane.]

Plate tectonics is driven by the internal energy of the Earth. Although there is some debate among geoscientists as to the exact mechanism, most agree that motion of the plates is ultimately driven by convection currents in the mantle.

Recall that convection is a means of heat transfer wherein the heat moves with the material. It occurs when conduction is inefficient at transporting heat, particularly if the material has a low thermal conductivity, like rocks.

Recall also that the Earth's asthenosphere is ductile, and therefore is likely to flow more readily (it will bend or deform if you apply enough force to it) than the overlying lithosphere.

[figure: Figure 1.9: Convection currents in the Asthenosphere — cross-section: on the right an "Oceanic Ridge" with "Magma" rising through a gap in the "Lithosphere"; "Hot rising currents" (upward arrows) beneath the ridge; "Convection Currents in Asthenosphere" (arrows) flowing left under the lithosphere; "Ridge push" written along the lithosphere sloping away from the ridge; on the left an "Oceanic Trench" where the lithosphere bends down into the mantle as a slab labelled "Slab Pull", alongside "Cool descending currents"; a continent and "Sea level" at top left.]

[note: The unit outcome asks for "driving and retarding forces" of plate motion. Figure 1.9 names two driving forces — ridge push (the raised, hot ridge lithosphere slides away downhill from the ridge) and slab pull (the cold, dense sinking slab pulls the rest of the plate after it) — but the text on these pages explains only mantle convection and does not discuss retarding (resisting) forces such as friction at transform faults and collision zones, or mantle drag under thick continents. Lesson builders should add a short explanation if this outcome is taught.]

(p.11)

Thus, if the asthenosphere moves by convection with the rising currents carrying heat towards the surface at the oceanic ridges, and descending currents sinking at subduction zones after losing heat to the surface, the brittle plates riding on top of the convection cell will be forced to move over the surface, being in a sense, dragged along by the moving asthenosphere.

### Reflective Activity 1.3

1. Discuss the three types of plate boundaries.

**Answer:**
- **Convergent (destructive):** plates move toward each other; the denser plate is subducted and destroyed. Oceanic–continental → volcanic mountains (Nazca under South American Plate → Andes); oceanic–oceanic → volcanic island arc (Mariana Islands); continental–continental → crust is deformed and lifted into high fold mountains (Indo-Australian + Eurasian → Himalayas).
- **Divergent (constructive):** plates move apart; rising magma from the asthenosphere creates new crust — seafloor spreading at oceanic ridges (Mid-Atlantic Ridge) with shallow earthquakes; on land it forms rift valleys (East African Rift Valley) that may become a new ocean.
- **Transform (conservative):** plates slide past each other sideways along transform faults; crust is neither created nor destroyed; plates stick and slip, causing earthquakes (San Andreas Fault, California; many offsets along oceanic ridges).
**Why:**
- The three kinds are defined on p.8 and described on pp.8–10.
- Names match what happens to crust: destroyed (destructive), created (constructive), conserved (conservative).
- Which crust meets which (oceanic is denser basalt; continental is lighter granite) decides the landform at a convergent boundary.
- All three are driven by mantle convection that drags the plates (pp.10–11).

### Brainstorming Activity 1.4

1. How would you define a geological process?

**Answer:** A geological process is a natural force or process that shapes the physical make-up of the earth — e.g. plate tectonics, folding, faulting, volcanism, erosion, weathering and sedimentation.
**Why:**
- Book: "The term 'geological processes' describes the natural forces that shape the physical makeup of our planet earth" (p.12).
- Some act slowly and invisibly; others (earthquakes, eruptions) cause great damage (p.12).
- They work from inside the earth (endogenic) or from outside (exogenic).

2. What is the difference between endogenic and exogenic forces?

**Answer:** Endogenic (internal) forces originate inside the earth and build up relief (e.g. by folding, faulting, earthquakes and volcanism); exogenic (external) forces get their strength from the earth's exterior/atmosphere and act on the surface, mainly wearing it down (weathering, erosion, deposition).
**Why:**
- Exogenic forces "derive their strength from the earth's exterior or originate within the earth's atmosphere" (p.12).
- Endogenic forces describe "pressure that originates inside the earth" (p.12).
- Internal forces "create irregularities or unevenness" — ups and downs — by breaking, bending or folding rock (p.12).

#### EARTHQUAKES

The sudden shaking of the ground that occurs when masses of rock change position below Earth's surface is called an earthquake. The shifting of the rock releases a great amount of energy, sending out shock waves that travel through the rock, and cause the ground to shake. These shock waves, called seismic waves by Earth scientists, may be powerful enough to alter the surface, thrusting up cliffs and opening great cracks in the ground. Earthquakes occur most often along geologic faults, which are fractures in the rocks of Earth's crust. Along faults, the rock masses on opposite sides of the fracture strain against each other and sometimes "slip," causing an earthquake.

The major fault lines of the world are located at the fringes of the huge tectonic plates that make up the crust. Earthquakes, called temblors by scientists, occur almost continuously. Fortunately, most of them can be detected only by sensitive instruments called seismographs. A scale is used for reading it. It is called a Richter scale. It gives reading from 0 (no movement) to 9 (extremely severe). The Richter scale measures the effect in a logarithmic scale. The effects of an earthquake increases significantly for each step up the scale. (Figure 1.13).

##### Causes of Earthquakes

Most of the worst earthquakes are associated with changes in the shape of Earth's outermost shell, particularly the crust. These earthquakes are generated by the rapid release of strain energy that is stored within the rocks of the crust. The strongest and the most destructive quakes are associated with ruptures of the crust, which are known as faults. Although faults are present in most regions of the world, earthquakes are not associated with all of them.

(p.16)

##### Shock Waves

The shifting rock in an earthquake causes shock waves called seismic waves to spread through the rock in all directions. In a great earthquake, shocks may be felt by people thousands of miles or kilometers away from the center. Seismographs can pick up the waves on the other side of the world. There are two broad classes of seismic waves:

**Body waves and surface waves.**

Body waves travel within the body of the Earth. They include **P, or primary waves** and **S, or secondary waves.** P waves spread in the crust from the point of rupture, which is called the focus of the earthquake. The point of which the wave originates is what we call the Earthquake focus. The point on the Earth's surface immediately above the focus is termed the epicenter of an earthquake. P waves alternately compress and expand the rock through which they pass, and vibrate in the same direction in which the waves travel.

**S waves** vibrate at right angles to the direction of wave travel. These secondary waves are the location of an earthquake many thousands of miles away. [review: garbled sentence. Intended meaning: S waves arrive after the faster P waves, and the time gap between the P and S arrivals at a seismograph helps scientists locate an earthquake many thousands of miles away. Also useful: P waves are the fastest and pass through solids and liquids; S waves are slower and pass only through solids.] After both P and S waves have moved through the body of the Earth, they are followed by two types of surface waves, which travel along the Earth's surface. These are named Love and Rayleigh waves, after the scientists who identified them. Because of their larger amplitude, surface waves are responsible for much of the destructive shaking that occurs far from the epicenter. Surface waves, which travel more slowly than body waves, are the most powerful shake waves.

##### Effects of Earthquakes

Earthquakes often cause dramatic changes at the Earth's surface. In addition to the ground movements, other surface effects include changes in the flow of groundwater, landslides, and mudflows. Earthquakes can do significant damage to buildings, bridges, pipelines, railways, embankments, dams, and other structures.

Earthquakes can lead to devastating fires. Fire produced the greatest property loss following the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, when 521 blocks in the city center burned uncontrollably for three days. Fire also followed the 1923 Tokyo earthquake, causing much damage and hardship for the citizens.

Underwater earthquakes can cause giant waves called tsunamis (Figure 1.15). Violent shaking of the seafloor produces waves that spread over the ocean surface in ever-widening circles. In deep water a tsunami can travel as fast as 800 kilometers per hour. By the time a tsunami reaches shore, it has gained tremendous size and power, reaching heights as great as 30 meters. Tsunamis can be catastrophic, with the potential to wipe out coastal settlements.

### Key ideas by subsection

- **Unit intro (pp.1–2):** the earth is in constant motion from internal and external forces; earthquakes, volcanism, tsunamis and fault ruptures cluster at plate boundaries; understanding them helps reduce damage.
- **1.1 Continental drift (pp.2–5):** one supercontinent Pangaea → split (Triassic, Mesozoic) into Laurasia (north) and Gondwanaland (south) → present continents drifted to present positions over millions of years (Fig 1.1 stages: 225, 135, 65 Ma, today). Alfred Wegener, German meteorologist, 1912. Four evidences: coastline fit (Africa–South America, best at continental shelves), same rock types/ages/mountain belts, fossils (Mesosaurus in South America and Africa only), paleoclimate. Rejected because: not a geologist; Northern-Hemisphere scientists vs Southern-Hemisphere data; too-short timing (Cenozoic break-up); greatest problem — no direct evidence and no mechanism. Legacy: shift from fixism to mobilism.
- **1.2 Plate tectonics (pp.5–7):** late 1960s; lithosphere (crust + uppermost mantle, rigid, ≈0–100 km) broken into about a dozen large plates plus smaller ones, sliding on the weak, plastic asthenosphere; plates move a few cm per year and change size; plates may be continental, oceanic or both; plate edges ≠ continent edges (North American Plate includes western North Atlantic floor). Explains mountains, earthquakes, volcanoes, fossil distribution, rock ages; influences atmosphere, oceans and long-term climate.
- **1.3 Plate boundaries (pp.8–11):** granite continental crust is less dense than basalt oceanic crust. Convergent/destructive: ocean–continent → subduction, magma, volcanic mountains (Nazca under South American → Andes); ocean–ocean → volcanic island arc (Mariana Islands); continent–continent → crust lifted into fold mountains (Indo-Australian + Eurasian → Himalayas). Divergent/constructive: magma creates new seafloor at ocean ridges (Mid-Atlantic), seafloor spreading, shallow earthquakes; on land → rift valleys (East African Rift), possibly a new ocean. Transform/conservative: plates slide past; crust neither created nor destroyed; stick-slip earthquakes (San Andreas Fault). Driving force: mantle convection (rising hot currents at ridges, sinking cool currents at subduction zones) drags the brittle plates; Fig 1.9 also names ridge push and slab pull.
- **1.4 Geological processes (pp.11–12):** geological processes = natural forces shaping the earth; studied to understand earth history, find resources (metal ores) and predict disasters. Endogenic (internal, pressure from inside, create relief) vs exogenic (external, from exterior/atmosphere — weathering, erosion, sedimentation).
- **Folding (pp.12–13):** sideways compression bends layers into anticlines (upfolds) and synclines (downfolds); continued compression → asymmetrical fold (one limb steeper) → over fold (one limb pushed over the other) → over thrust fold (fold breaks, one limb thrust forward). Three mountain-building periods in 400 million years; first two → old fold mountains (low, weathered: Caledonides/Scandinavian, Appalachians, Urals); last (Alpine orogeny) → young fold mountains (Andes, Rockies, Alps, Himalayas, Atlas).
- **Faulting (p.14):** fault = crack in the crust from tension or compression, rocks displaced up/down; parallel faults → rift valley/graben (middle block sinks; e.g. East African Rift Valley) or block mountain/horst (middle block rises; e.g. Afar/Danakil Horst).
- **Earthquakes (pp.15–17):** sudden shaking when rocks slip along faults, releasing stored strain energy as seismic waves; major faults at plate margins; seismograph records; Richter scale logarithmic. Focus (point of rupture underground) vs epicenter (point on surface directly above). Body waves: P (primary, push-pull, vibrate along travel direction) and S (secondary, vibrate at right angles); surface waves: Love and Rayleigh — slowest, largest amplitude, most destructive. Effects: ground movement, groundwater changes, landslides, mudflows, damage to structures, fires (1906 San Francisco — 521 blocks burned for 3 days; 1923 Tokyo), tsunamis (up to 800 km/h in deep water, up to 30 m high at shore).

### Common student mistakes

- Thinking continental drift and plate tectonics are the same thing; drift (Wegener, 1912) said continents move but had no mechanism; plate tectonics (1960s) explains how — lithospheric plates move on the asthenosphere, driven by convection.
- Thinking plate edges are the coastlines, or that only continents move (whole plates, including ocean floor, move).
- Mixing up lithosphere (rigid, crust + uppermost mantle) and asthenosphere (weak, plastic layer beneath).
- Swapping the boundary nicknames: convergent = destructive, divergent = constructive, transform = conservative.
- Saying the lighter continental plate subducts — it is the denser (oceanic, basalt) plate that sinks.
- Putting Laurasia in the south / Gondwanaland in the north; forgetting India belonged to Gondwanaland.
- Confusing anticline (upfold, ∩) with syncline (downfold, ∪).
- Confusing graben/rift valley (block sinks) with horst/block mountain (block rises); thinking all faulting is compression.
- Calling the Urals or Appalachians young fold mountains (they are old; low and weathered).
- Confusing focus (underground) with epicenter (on the surface above it).
- Thinking magnitude 6 is "a bit stronger" than 5 — on a logarithmic scale it is about 10× the wave amplitude and ≈32× the energy; and thinking the Richter scale stops at 9.
- Thinking P waves are the most destructive — surface waves (Love, Rayleigh) are slowest but do most damage.
- Calling earthquakes, volcanoes and tsunamis exogenic — they are endogenic in origin.

#### Volcanism:

Volcanism is the process by which molten rock or magma, together with gaseous and solid materials is forced out on the surface of the earth. This movement could be very slow or sudden. With slow movement, the materials could spread over the surrounding area gently. If the movement is violent, the materials will be thrown high into the sky and then fall back in the surface of the earth. Magma may reach the surface of the earth through two types of openings:

1. Vents and 2. Fissures

Vents are holes or openings like a pipe throw which magma flows out into the surface of the earth. If lava emerges via vent, it builds up a volcano (a cone shaped mound). [review: "like a pipe throw which" should read "like a pipe through which".]

Fissures are large and narrow cracks or fractures in a rock. Molten materials may move upward along the cracks and spread out over the surroundings. If the lava emerges via a fissure, it builds up a plateau. During the formation of lava plateau, there is little or no explosive activity through the fissures; lava gently spreads over large area.

#### Volcanoes

The word volcano refers to the form or structure, usually conical, produced by accumulations of erupted material. In some volcanic eruptions, the molten rock called magma when it is underground and lava when it reaches the surface, flows slowly out of the vent.

#### Occurrence

Volcanoes occur mainly near the boundaries of tectonic plates. They form along belts of tension, where plates diverge, and along belts of compression, where plates converge. Nearly 1,900 volcanoes are active today or are known to have been active in historical times. Of these, almost 90 percent are situated in the Pacific Ring of Fire (Figure 1.18).

(p.20)

[figure: Figure 1.18 — Pacific Ring of Fire: map of the Pacific Ocean with a shaded horseshoe band labelled "RING OF FIRE" running from New Zealand (Kermadec and Tonga trenches) north through Indonesia (Java/Sunda trench, Krakatau), the Philippines (Mt Pinatubo, Mt Mayon, Philippine trench), the Mariana trench with Challenger Deep, Izu-Ogasawara, Japan, Kuril and Ryukyu trenches, across the Aleutian trench to Alaska, then south along western North America (Mt Garibaldi, Mt St Helens), the Middle America trench and the Peru-Chile trench to southern South America. Also marked: Puerto Rico trench, South Sandwich trench, Bougainville trench, Equator.]

The Mediterranean-Asian belt, which accounts for most of the world's earthquakes outside the Ring of Fire, has few volcanoes except the Indonesia and Mediterranean which have more numerous ones.

Oceanic volcanoes are strung along the world's oceanic ridges, while the remaining active volcanoes are associated with the Great Rift Valley of East Africa.

Volcanic activity typically alternates between short active periods and much longer dormant periods.

1. An extinct volcano is one that is not erupting, and is not likely to erupt in the future. Example, Mount Zuqualla, Ethiopia.
2. A dormant volcano is currently inactive but has erupted within historic times, and is likely to do so in the future. Example: Mount Kilimanjaro, Tanzania
3. An active volcano is one that has erupted or thought to have erupted during the last 500 years. Example: Erta Ale, Ethiopia.

(p.21)

Volcanoes are usually classified by shape and size. These are determined by such factors as the volume and type of volcanic material ejected, the sequence and variety of eruptions, and the environment. Among the most common types are

1. Shield volcanoes,
2. Strato volcanoes
3. Cinder cones.
4. Craters, and
5. Calderas

**Shield volcanoes** are volcanoes that have a low, but broad profile created by highly fluid lava flows that spread over wide areas. The lava, usually composed of basalt, cannot build up a cone with sides much steeper than 7 degrees (Figure 1.19). The Hawaiian Islands are composed of shield volcanoes that have built up from the seafloor to the surface some 5 kilometers above.

#### Volcanic eruptions

Styles of eruption and types of lava are associated with different kinds of plate boundaries. Most lava that comes from vents in oceanic divergence zones and from mid oceanic volcanoes are basaltic. Where ocean plates collide, the rock types basalt and andesite predominate. Near the zone where an ocean plate and a continental plate converge, consolidated ash flows are found. The eruption of Krakatoa (1883) unleashed a tsunami, a large seismic sea wave that swept the coasts of Java and Sumatra and drowned more than 36,000 people.

#### Mass wasting:

Mass Wasting refers to the down slope movements of rock, regolith, and soil under the influence of gravity. Actual gravity is the controlling factor for mass wasting. There are also other factors. The major ones are:

- Water;
- The angle of the slope, and
- The activities of people.

(p.31)

How do water and the angle of slope cause mass wasting? Let us examine them briefly.

**Water:** We know that a portion of rain water that falls on land soaks into the ground. Part of the sacked water collects into pores of the sediments. Such water could reduce the cohesion among the particles which results in lack of internal resistance. Subsequently, the materials are set in motion by the force of gravity. [review: "sacked water" should read "soaked water" (water that has soaked into the ground).]

**The Angle of the Slope:** The steepest slope at which materials remain firm varies from 25 to 40 degrees. This depends on the size and shape of the particles. For example, the larger and more angular particles maintain the steepest slopes. If the angle is increased, the rock debris will adjust by moving down slope.

**Activities of people:** People often create favorable conditions for mass wasting along steep and unstable slopes. This happens due to farming and overgrazing of the areas. In different parts of Ethiopia, mass wasting occurred several times. This phenomenon resulted in the destruction of human life and properties.

[note: the 25–40° "steepest slope at which materials remain firm" is the same idea as the angle of repose met under cinder cones (p.22) — a good link for lessons.]

How do water and the angle of slope cause mass wasting?

**Answer:** Water soaks into the ground, fills the pores between particles and reduces their cohesion (internal resistance), so gravity can move them; if a slope becomes steeper than about 25–40° (the steepest stable angle for that material), the debris moves down slope until it is stable again.
**Why:**
- Water: rain → soaks in → fills pores → less cohesion/internal resistance → gravity sets material in motion.
- Slope: each material has a steepest stable angle of 25–40°, depending on particle size and shape (large, angular particles hold the steepest slopes); increase the angle beyond it and material slides.
- Gravity is the controlling force in both cases; water and slope only make it easier for gravity to act.

#### I. Chooses the best answer for the following questions

1. Which one of the following is part of the old Gondwanaland?
   A. North America  B. Asia  C. Europe  D. Australia

**Answer:** D. Australia
**Why:**
- Gondwanaland was the southern part of Pangaea (p.2 of the book); it included South America, Africa, Australia, Antarctica and India.
- A. North America — part of Laurasia, the northern part of Pangaea.
- B. Asia — (apart from India) part of Laurasia.
- C. Europe — part of Laurasia.

2. Which of the following was proposed by Alfred Wegener?
   A. Plate Tectonics  B. Continental Drift  C. Ocean floor spreading  D. All

**Answer:** B. Continental Drift
**Why:**
- The book: "The concept was proposed by a German meteorologist Alfred Wegener in 1912."
- A. Plate Tectonics — developed by other scientists in the late 1960s, after Wegener.
- C. Ocean floor spreading — explained in the 1960s (Harry Hess), long after Wegener; it later supplied the mechanism Wegener lacked.
- D. All — wrong because A and C were not his.

3. What is the name of the huge sea that existed around Pangea?
   A. Atlantic Ocean  B. Tethys  C. Amazon  D. Antarctica  E. None

**Answer:** B. Tethys (the answer the book most likely expects).
**Why:**
- Tethys is the only option that is an ancient sea associated with Pangaea; it lay between Laurasia and Gondwanaland.
- A. Atlantic Ocean — formed later, when Pangaea broke apart (the Americas drifted away from Europe/Africa).
- C. Amazon — a river and rainforest in South America, not a sea.
- D. Antarctica — a continent, not a sea.
- E. None — see review note.
[review: strictly, the single huge ocean that surrounded Pangaea was Panthalassa; Tethys was the smaller sea between Laurasia and Gondwanaland (in the "mouth" of Pangaea). Since Panthalassa is not offered, "E. None" is also defensible. The unit text never mentions Tethys or Panthalassa, so students cannot answer from the book. Suggest rewording: "What was the name of the sea between Laurasia and Gondwanaland?" (answer Tethys), or add Panthalassa as an option.]

4. The sudden shaking of the ground that occurs when masses of rock changes its position below the Earth's surface is called:
   A. An earthquake  B. Volcano  C. Magma  D. None

**Answer:** A. An earthquake
**Why:**
- This is the book's exact definition of an earthquake (p.15).
- B. Volcano — an opening/mountain through which magma, gases and ash erupt, not ground shaking.
- C. Magma — molten rock below the surface, not a movement.
- D. None — wrong because A fits exactly.

5. Which of the features given below is associated with the work of wind action in different areas?
   A. Sand dunes  B. Barkhan  C. Loess deposits  D. All  E. None

**Answer:** D. All
**Why:**
- The book lists sand dunes, barkhans and loess deposits as "the most common features" of wind action (p.30).
- A, B, C — each is correct alone, but choosing one would leave out the others, so "All" is the best answer.
- E. None — wrong because all three are wind features.

6. A crack on the earth's crust formed by the forces of tension and compression is:
   A. A fault  B. A fold  C. Volcano  D. All

**Answer:** A. A fault
**Why:**

- B. A fold — rock layers bent (not cracked) by sideways compression.
- C. Volcano — an opening where magma erupts, not a crack formed by tension/compression.

- A. Thermographs — record temperature.
- C. Wind vane — shows wind direction.

#### II. Give Short answers to the following questions

1. Explain the continental drift and plate tectonic theories.

**Answer:** Continental drift (Alfred Wegener, 1912): the present continents were once joined in one supercontinent, Pangaea, which broke (from the Triassic) into Laurasia in the north and Gondwanaland in the south and then into today's continents, which slowly drifted to their present positions. Evidence: matching coastlines of Africa and South America, similar rock types/ages and mountain belts across the Atlantic, the same fossils (Mesosaurus) on both sides, and similar ancient climates. It was rejected at first mainly because Wegener could not explain the mechanism. Plate tectonics (late 1960s): the lithosphere (crust + uppermost mantle) is broken into about a dozen large plates that move a few centimetres per year over the ductile asthenosphere, driven mainly by mantle convection currents. Plates meet at convergent (destructive), divergent (constructive) and transform (conservative) boundaries, where most earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain building happen.
**Why:**
- Definition and date of continental drift, Pangaea, Laurasia and Gondwanaland come from section 1.1.
- The four evidences (coastline fit, rock/structure similarity, fossil, palaeoclimate) are the book's list.
- Plate tectonics is the book's section 1.2–1.3: lithosphere on asthenosphere, ~12 plates, convection currents, three boundary types.
- Link: plate tectonics supplies the mechanism continental drift lacked.

2. Identify the major geological processes and explain associated land features.

**Answer:** (a) Internal/endogenic processes: folding → anticlines, synclines, fold mountains (young: Alps, Himalayas, Andes, Rockies, Atlas; old: Appalachians, Urals); faulting → rift valleys/grabens (East African Rift) and block mountains/horsts (Afar Horst); earthquakes → cracks, cliffs, landslides, tsunamis; volcanism → shield, strato and cinder-cone volcanoes, craters, calderas, lava plateaus (fissure eruptions) and intrusive features (batholith, sill, dyke). (b) External/exogenic processes: weathering (exfoliation, frost action → screes, root wedging; chemical solution → limestone caves, stalactites, stalagmites, pillars, e.g. Sof Omar); erosion and deposition by running water (sheet, rill, gully erosion; V-shaped valley, gorge, waterfall, rapids, interlocking spurs; meanders, ox-bow lakes; flood plain, delta), wind (sand dunes, barchans, loess), the sea (beach, spit, lagoon); and mass wasting (landslides on steep, wet, over-farmed slopes).
**Why:**
- The book divides geological processes into internal (endogenic) forces (1.4.1) and external (exogenic) forces (1.4.2).
- Internal forces raise and break the crust ("ups and downs"); external forces level the surface by denudation (weathering + erosion) and deposition.
- Each landform listed is one the book names and illustrates (Figures 1.10–1.32).

## Notes for lesson design

### Key ideas by subsection (book pp.18–33)
- **Earthquake occurrence (pp.18–19):** Circum-Pacific belt / Ring of Fire ≈ 80 % of earthquake energy; Mediterranean–Asian belt ≈ 15 %; plus mid-ocean ridges and the East African Rift. Focus depth up to ~700 km; shallow (< 60 km) > 75 % of energy, intermediate (60–300 km) ≈ 12 %, deep ≈ 3 %, mostly in Benioff zones along a subducting plate.
- **Volcanism (p.19):** magma reaches the surface through vents (→ volcano, cone) or fissures (→ lava plateau, quiet eruption). Magma underground, lava at the surface.
- **Volcano occurrence and activity (pp.19–20):** ~1,900 active/historically active volcanoes, ~90 % in the Ring of Fire; others on mid-ocean ridges, in Indonesia/Mediterranean and the East African Rift. Active (erupted in last 500 years, Erta Ale), dormant (Kilimanjaro), extinct (Zuqualla).
- **Volcano forms (pp.21–23):** shield (basaltic, fluid lava, slopes ≤ ~7°, Hawaii); strato (alternating lava + pyroclastic layers, viscous lava, steep, explosive, most common); cinder cone (cinders, slope = angle of repose); crater (small summit bowl); caldera (large basin after magma chamber empties and cone collapses, Rinjani). Intrusive features: batholith (huge, deep, dome), sill (horizontal, between layers), dyke (vertical, cuts across layers).
- **Volcanic eruptions (p.24):** lava type follows plate boundary — basalt at divergent ridges, basalt + andesite where oceanic plates meet, ash flows at ocean–continent convergence. Krakatoa 1883 tsunami: > 36,000 dead.
- **External forces (p.24):** running water, wind, ice, waves, gravity; they level the land by denudation (weathering + erosion) and deposition.
- **Weathering (pp.24–27):** physical (same minerals, smaller pieces): exfoliation by heating/cooling in hot deserts, frost action → screes, roots and burrowing animals. Chemical (minerals changed): rain with O₂ (rusting) and CO₂ (carbonic acid → solution/carbonation of limestone; hydrolysis → clay); caves, stalactites (ceiling), stalagmites (floor), pillars — Sof Omar/Weyb river in Bale; bacteria and organic acids.
- **Erosion and deposition (pp.27–30):** running water is the strongest agent; power rises with speed/slope and falls with vegetation cover. Runoff: sheet → rill → gully. River course: upper (V-valley, gorge, interlocking spurs, rapids, waterfalls), middle (confluence, meanders, ox-bow lakes), lower (flood plain, delta). Wind: dunes, barchans, loess. Sea: beach, spit, lagoon.
- **Mass wasting (pp.30–31):** gravity-driven down-slope movement; helped by water (less cohesion), slopes steeper than 25–40°, and people (farming, overgrazing on steep slopes) — a real hazard in Ethiopia.

### Common student mistakes
- Mixing up magma (underground) and lava (at the surface).
- Thinking craters and calderas are the same, or that they are "types of volcano" (the book's list invites this).
- Mixing up stalactite (hangs from the ceiling) and stalagmite (grows from the ground/floor).
- Confusing weathering (breaking in place) with erosion (moving the material away); and physical (same chemistry) with chemical (new substances).
- Placing meanders/ox-bows in the upper course, or V-valleys/waterfalls in the lower course.
- Believing loess is found in deserts (it is deposited beyond them).
- Thinking only Ring-of-Fire areas have earthquakes — Ethiopia's Rift Valley and Afar are seismically and volcanically active (Erta Ale).
- Calling Tethys the ocean "around" Pangaea (it was Panthalassa; Tethys lay between Laurasia and Gondwanaland).
- Expecting the percentages of earthquake energy by depth to add to 100 % (book gives >75 + 12 + 3).
