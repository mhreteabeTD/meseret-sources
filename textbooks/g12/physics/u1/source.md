## Introduction

Science is a collection of different scientific fields, or disciplines. It is the union of these fields that helps us to understand the world in which we live. Although there are many scientific disciplines with a special character and history of their own, each discipline is dependent on and reinforces the other disciplines. Scientific disciplines do not have fixed borders. Each discipline benefits from advances in other areas of science. Understanding the contribution that a scientific discipline can make to others is important for the collaborative development all scientific disciplines, and their contribution to society and the environment.

### Physics and chemistry

### Brainstorming question 1.2

(i) Identify some relationships between physics and chemistry.

**Answer:** Both study matter and its interaction with energy; atomic structure, chemical bonding, energy changes in reactions and spectroscopy all come from physics.
**Why:**
- Atomic and subatomic physics explains the electron structure that decides how atoms bond.
- Heat (thermodynamics) tells whether a reaction can go and what the equilibrium mixture is.
- Spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (physics tools) reveal composition and structure of substances.
- Radioactivity and the periodic properties of elements are explained by nuclear and atomic physics.

(ii) What physics concept is applied in separation of dissolved salt from water by evaporation method?

**Answer:** Heat transfer and change of state (evaporation/boiling), using the difference in boiling points (volatility) of water and salt.
**Why:**
- Heat supplied gives water molecules enough kinetic energy (latent heat of vaporisation) to escape as vapour.
- Salt has a far higher boiling point, so it does not evaporate and is left behind as crystals.

Physics and chemistry may overlap when the system under study involves matter composed of electrons and nuclei. Fundamental laws that govern the behavior of matter apply to both chemistry and physics. Both physics and chemistry are concerned with matter and its interaction with energy. The theory and various rules about atoms which are important in chemistry are ultimately explained in principle by atomic and subatomic particle physics. Chemists and physicists use similar method to study the interaction of large number of particles which have complicated interaction beyond the capacity of any computer, and the capacity of the human mind.

The physics of atoms and subatomic particles is critically important for understanding how individual atoms are joined by covalent bonds to form molecules. The physics of atoms and subatomic particles can also provide quantitative insight into ionic and covalent bonding processes by explicitly showing which molecules are energetically favorable to which others and the magnitudes of the energies involved.

### Discussion question 1.1

Dear students, chemistry studies minute entities like elements, atoms, molecules and ions which cannot be seen with your naked eyes. How do chemists collect information, like composition, structure, properties and the changes they undergo during a reaction with other substances, from these minute invisible particles?

**Answer:** They use instruments built on physics, mainly the interaction of radiation and fields with matter.
**Why:**
- Spectroscopy: each element/molecule absorbs or emits particular wavelengths, giving composition and bond information.
- X-ray diffraction: the pattern of scattered X-rays gives the arrangement of atoms in crystals.
- Mass spectrometry: electric and magnetic fields separate ions by mass, giving molecular mass and composition.
- Measuring heat, temperature, pressure, conductivity and colour change during a reaction shows the changes taking place.

The physics of energy related to heat tells chemists whether a particular reaction is energetically possible in the direction in which it is written, and it gives the composition of the reaction system at equilibrium. The physics of heat energy provides a bridge between the macroscopic properties of a substance and the individual properties of its constituent molecules and atoms.

Spectroscopy is the study of the interaction between matter and electromagnetic radiation as a function of the wavelength or frequency of the radiation.

(p.3)

Spectroscopy is a fundamental examining tool in many fields including physics and chemistry. Most of what we know about the structure of atoms and molecules comes from spectroscopic study. The spectroscopic techniques are developed by collaborative work of physicists and chemists.

In general, the study of matter and electricity in physics is fundamental towards the understanding of concepts in chemistry, such as atomic structure, molecular structure, X-ray diffractions, radioactivity, periodic properties of elements, nature of valency, chemical bonds in molecules, crystal structure of solids and others. This shows that chemistry is rooted in atomic and molecular physics.

#### Physics of Newtonian mechanics and biology

The Newtonian mechanics tells us how different animals and their body parts move. The combination of knowledge of physics and biology help to explain how athletes run fast and why the fastest animal in the world, cheetah, runs fast. Newtonian mechanics tells us that a body is in stable equilibrium under the action of gravity if its center of mass is directly over its base of support. Under this condition, the reaction force at the base of support cancels the force of gravity and the torque produced by it. If the center of mass is outside the base, the torque produced by the weight tends to fall the body. A person falls when his center of gravity is displaced beyond the position of the feet. The wider the base on which the body rests and the closer the center of gravity of it to the base, the more stable it is; that is, the more difficult it is to fall it. How we can use our limbs to do different jobs can also be explained by Newtonian mechanics.

### Discussion question 1.2

(i) Why it is difficult to stand on one foot than the two feet? Why we are more stable when we sleep than we are standing?

**Answer:** On one foot the base of support is small, so the centre of gravity easily moves outside it; lying down the base is very wide and the centre of gravity is very low, so it is hard to topple.
**Why:**
- Stability rule (book): a body is stable while its centre of mass is directly over its base of support.
- Two feet apart make a wide base; one foot makes a tiny base, so a small sway moves the centre of gravity beyond the edge and the weight produces a toppling torque.
- Lying down: the base is the whole length of the body (widest) and the centre of gravity is only a few cm above it (lowest), both of which increase stability.

(ii) Figure 1.1 (a) shows freely standing person and Figure 1.1 (b) shows the way a person carrying a load stands. Why the person carrying the load stands bending in Figure 1.1(b) than the person in Figure 1.1(a)?

**Answer:** The load shifts the combined centre of gravity towards the loaded side; the person leans the body the other way to bring the combined centre of gravity back over the feet.
**Why:**
- Body + load have one combined centre of gravity, which moves towards the heavy bag.
- If it moved outside the feet, the weight would make a torque that tips the person over.
- Bending the trunk away from the load moves body mass to the other side, so the total torque about the feet is zero and the combined centre of gravity lies again over the base of support.

### Discussion question 1.3

(i) Have you observed when the earthworm moves? How do earthworms locomote without limbs?

**Answer:** The earthworm uses its fluid-filled body (hydrostatic skeleton) and Pascal's principle: muscles squeeze the fluid, and the pressure is passed equally through it to lengthen or shorten body segments.
**Why:**
- Pascal's principle: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of it.
- Circular muscles contract → segment becomes thin and long, pushing the front forward.
- Longitudinal muscles contract → segment becomes short and fat; tiny bristles (setae) grip the soil as an anchor.
- Waves of these contractions pass along the body (peristalsis), so the worm crawls forward.

(ii) How a blood moves against gravity from the feet to the heart or from the heart to the brain when a person stands erect?

**Answer:** The heart's pumping produces a pressure larger than the pressure of the blood column (ρgh), and in the veins muscle squeezing plus one-way valves push blood upward.
**Why:**
- Pressure needed to lift blood a height h: ΔP = ρgh. Heart to brain h ≈ 0.4 m: ΔP ≈ 1060 kg/m³ × 9.8 m/s² × 0.4 m ≈ 4.2 × 10³ Pa ≈ 31 mmHg, less than arterial pressure (~100 mmHg), so the heart can push blood to the brain.
- Veins from the feet: leg muscles contract and squeeze veins (the "muscle pump"); valves stop back-flow, so blood can only go up.
- Breathing lowers the pressure in the chest, which also helps draw blood towards the heart.

#### Physics of sound wave and biology

Sound is a mechanical wave produced by vibrating bodies. The vocal cords produce sound when they come together and then vibrate as air passes through them during exhalation of air from the lungs. This vibration produces the sound wave for your voice. When the human vocal cords (Figure 1.2) set into vibrational motion, the surrounding air molecules are disturbed and are forced to follow the motion of the vibrating body. The vibrating molecules in turn transfer their motion to adjacent molecules causing the vibrational disturbance to propagate away from the source. When the air vibrations reach the ear, they cause the

(p.5)

eardrum to vibrate; this produces nerve impulses that are interpreted by the brain.

#### Physics of electricity and biology

Many life processes involve electrical phenomena. The nervous system of animals and the control of muscle movement, for example, are both governed by electrical interactions. The very important electrical phenomena in living organisms are found in the nervous system of animals. Specialized cells called neurons form a complex network within the body which receives, processes, and transmits information from one part of the body to another. The center of this network is located in the brain, which has the ability to store and analyze information. Based on this information, the nervous system controls various parts of the body. The messages are electrical pulses transmitted by the neurons. When a neuron receives an appropriate stimulus, it produces electrical pulses that are propagated along its cablelike structure.

#### Optical physics and biology

Light is the electromagnetic radiation in the wavelength region between about 400 nm and 700 nm. Although light is only a tiny part of the electromagnetic spectrum, it is very important in both physics and biology. Light has fundamental roles in living system because of its paramount importance. Most of the electromagnetic radiation from the sun that reaches the Earth's surface is in this region of the spectrum, and life has evolved to utilize it. In photosynthesis, plants use light to convert carbon dioxide and water into organic materials, which are the building blocks of living organisms. Animals have evolved light-sensitive organs, like the eyes, which are their main source of information about the surroundings. Some bacteria and insects can even produce light through chemical reactions. Optical physics, which is the study of light, includes topics such as microscopes, telescopes, vision, color, pigments, illumination, spectroscopy, and lasers, all of which have applications in the life sciences.

### Discussion question 1.4

Discuss how our eyes use light to see objects?

**Answer:** Light reflected from (or emitted by) an object enters the eye, is refracted by the cornea and lens to form a real, inverted image on the retina; retina cells change the light into electrical nerve signals that the brain interprets.
**Why:**
- Light passes through the cornea and pupil; the iris adjusts the pupil size to control the amount of light.
- The cornea and the convex eye lens refract (bend) the light and focus it; ciliary muscles change the lens shape to focus near or far objects (accommodation).
- The image on the retina is real and inverted; rods (dim light) and cones (colour) convert light energy into electrical impulses.
- The optic nerve carries the impulses to the brain, which interprets them as an upright picture.

### Exercise 1.1

What is the distance that light can travel in one year in kilometer?

**Answer:** About 9.46 × 10¹² km (one light year).
**Why:**
- Formula: distance = speed × time, d = c × t.
- Speed of light c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s = 3 × 10⁵ km/s.
- One year t = 365 days × 24 h/day × 3600 s/h = 365 × 86 400 s = 31 536 000 s = 3.1536 × 10⁷ s.
- d = (3 × 10⁵ km/s) × (3.1536 × 10⁷ s) = 9.4608 × 10¹² km.
- Rounded: d ≈ 9.46 × 10¹² km (≈ 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m).
- Check: 10¹³ km ÷ (3 × 10⁵ km/s) = 3.33 × 10⁷ s ≈ 1.06 years, so 9.46 × 10¹² km for exactly 1 year is consistent. (With c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s and 365.25 days: 9.461 × 10¹² km — same to 3 figures.)

#### Physics of atoms and astronomy

### Brainstorming question 1.6

What do emission and absorption of light by an object tell us?

**Answer:** Which elements the object contains, how hot it is, and how it moves; they also show the energy levels of its atoms.
**Why:**
- An electron falling from a higher to a lower orbit emits a photon whose energy equals the energy difference (E = hf); absorption happens for the jump upward.
- Each element has its own set of energy levels, so its emission/absorption lines are a unique "fingerprint" → composition.
- Which levels are excited depends on temperature → temperature.
- Shift of the lines from their laboratory wavelengths (Doppler effect) → motion towards or away from us.

Atomic astrophysics is concerned with performing atomic physics calculations that will be useful to astronomers and using atomic data to interpret astronomical observations. The astronomers' only information about a particular object comes through the light that it emits, and this light arises through atomic transitions.

The physics that explains emission and absorption of radiation is closely related to the structure and energies of individual atoms that form the astronomical objects. When electrons of atoms jump from higher to a lower atomic orbit, photon is emitted. Photon is absorbed if electron jumps from lower electron orbit to higher electron orbit (Figure 1.5). The emission and absorption of radiation depends on the characteristics of individual atoms, and helps to measure something about the compositions, temperatures, and motions of stars by studying their spectra. You can visit the link: https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/hydrogen-atom to observe the Bohr atomic model for hydrogen atom.

In the interstellar matter, atoms are heated by nearby stars. This results in knocking of electrons to higher energy orbits. These electrons fall down to lower energy orbits emitting light of precisely the wavelength that corresponds to the energy change between the two orbits. The nature of the emitted light depends on the temperature. Thus, emitted light can be used to determine both compositions and temperatures of astronomical objects.

(p.8)

### Discussion question 1.5

How can magnetic vibration be used to form MRI image of the body part and used for diagnosis?

**Answer:** Hydrogen nuclei (protons) in the body line up in the scanner's strong magnetic field; a short radio-frequency pulse knocks them out of line; when the pulse stops they flip back and give off radio energy, which is detected and turned into an image by a computer. **Why:**

- Step 2: pulse applied → alignment disturbed (protons absorb energy, "flip").
- Step 3: pulse off → protons return to alignment, releasing the absorbed energy.
- Step 4: receiver coil detects the energy as an electric current; computer builds the image.
- Step 5: grey matter, white matter, blood, tumour etc. release different amounts of energy → contrast for diagnosis.

How do we get an image from these oscillation of flipped hydrogen protons in the brain? MRI uses the movement of these small magnets within a magnetic field to generate an image. Within the constant magnetic field of an MRI scanner, these small magnets arrange themselves parallel to constant magnetic field. When a current pulse (a current that flows for very short period of time) is applied to the patient's tissue, this parallel arrangement of the small magnets is disturbed. When the current pulse is off the small magnets back to their parallel arrangement releasing energy that they absorbed from the pulse. Different tissues in the body give off different amounts of energy. A special device detects the released energy as an electrical current. The electrical current is transformed in to an image via a

[review: in MRI the disturbing pulse is a short radio-frequency electromagnetic pulse sent by a coil (produced by a current pulse in the coil); no current is passed through the patient's tissue.]

### Radiation therapy

### Brainstorming question 1.12

Have you ever heard a medical treatment curing internal body cancer without surgery and medicine? How can be possible to do this?

**Answer:** Yes — radiation therapy (radiotherapy). High-energy X-rays or gamma rays (or an implanted radioactive source) are aimed at the tumour; the radiation ionises and damages the cancer cells' molecules (DNA) so they die, while the dose to healthy tissue is kept low.
**Why:**
- X-ray/gamma photons have energies far greater than the binding energies of electrons in molecules, so they rip off electrons (ionisation) and change molecular structure.
- An external beam is aimed from many directions so the tumour always gets the full dose but each part of healthy tissue gets only a small dose.
- Or a small sealed source (e.g. cobalt-60, radium) is placed near the tumour (brachytherapy).

The photons of X-rays and gamma-rays and the particles emitted by radioactive nuclei all have energies far greater than the energies that bind electrons to atoms and molecules. As a result, when such radiation penetrates into biological materials, it can rip off electrons from the biological molecules and produce substantial alterations in their structure.

In controlled doses radiation can be used therapeutically. In the treatment of certain types of cancer, an ampul containing radioactive material such as radium or cobalt 60 is implanted near the cancerous growth. By careful placement of the radioactive material and by controlling the dose, the cancer cell can be destroyed without greatly damaging the healthy tissue.

## End of unit questions

**1.** Explain the following biological process in terms of some physics knowledge.

- How our brain receives information from the whole body and send information to other body?

**Answer:** Through the nervous system: neurons carry information as electrical pulses (nerve impulses) from sense organs to the brain and from the brain to muscles and organs.
**Why:**
- A stimulus makes a neuron produce an electrical pulse (book: "electrical pulses that are propagated along its cablelike structure").
- The pulse is due to movement of charged ions (Na⁺, K⁺) across the cell membrane, changing the voltage across it.
- Sensory neurons carry signals to the brain; the brain analyses them; motor neurons carry pulses to muscles, which contract.

- Which part of our body tells us the temperature of our environment? What instrument is used to measure the exact value of our body temperature? What is the physics in this instrument?

**Answer:** The skin (its thermoreceptor nerve endings) senses the temperature of the surroundings. Body temperature is measured with a clinical thermometer (liquid-in-glass or digital). Physics: thermal expansion of a liquid (mercury/alcohol) in a narrow tube with a constriction, or, in a digital thermometer, change of electrical resistance with temperature (thermistor); heat flows from body to thermometer until thermal equilibrium.
**Why:**
- Skin receptors respond to heat flow into or out of the body.
- Liquid-in-glass: liquid expands in proportion to temperature rise; the kink (constriction) stops it falling back so the reading can be read; range about 35 °C–42 °C.
- Digital: the thermistor's resistance changes with temperature; the circuit converts this to a number.
- Thermal equilibrium (zeroth law): the thermometer reads the body temperature only after they reach the same temperature (normal ≈ 37 °C).

- What physics knowledge is needed to understand how a sound is created and transmitted to the listener?

**Answer:** Vibrations and waves: sound is a mechanical, longitudinal wave produced by vibrating bodies (vocal cords), carried by compressions and rarefactions of air molecules, and detected when it makes the eardrum vibrate.
**Why:**
- Source: vibration (frequency → pitch, amplitude → loudness).
- Medium: molecules pass the vibration on; sound needs a medium (cannot travel in vacuum); speed in air ≈ 340 m/s; v = fλ.
- Receiver: eardrum vibrates → small bones amplify → inner ear changes it to nerve impulses → brain.

- What energy transformation occurs in human body?

**Answer:** Chemical energy in food → mechanical (kinetic) energy of muscles, heat (thermal) energy, electrical energy of nerve signals, and sound energy of the voice; some is stored as chemical/potential energy (fat, glycogen).
**Why:**
- Metabolism (respiration) releases the chemical energy of food.
- Muscles convert it to kinetic energy; much becomes heat that keeps the body at ≈ 37 °C.
- Nerves use it to produce electrical pulses; vocal cords turn kinetic energy of vibration into sound energy.

- What energy transformation occurs in photosynthesis?

**Answer:** Light (radiant/electromagnetic) energy from the Sun → chemical energy stored in glucose (and other organic materials).
**Why:**
- Chlorophyll absorbs light of wavelength ~400–700 nm.
- This energy converts carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.
- The energy is stored in the chemical bonds of glucose.

**2.** List as many concepts of physics that can be used in designing modern vehicles.

**Answer:** Newton's laws of motion, friction, momentum and impulse (crash safety, airbags, crumple zones), work–energy and power, thermodynamics (engines, cooling), fluid mechanics and aerodynamics (drag, streamlining), centre of gravity and stability, torque and gears, circular motion (cornering), pressure (tyres, hydraulic brakes – Pascal's principle), electricity and electromagnetism (battery, motor, alternator, sensors), optics (mirrors, headlights), sound (horn, noise reduction), materials science (strength, elasticity), electronics (control units, GPS).
**Why:**
- Each part of a vehicle is a physics device: engine = heat engine, brakes = friction + Pascal's principle, body shape = aerodynamics, seat belts/airbags = impulse (FΔt = Δp, longer time → smaller force).
- Electric vehicles add electromagnetism (motors) and electrochemistry (batteries).

**3.** How physics of light is important to study the astronomical objects?

**Answer:** Light (EM radiation) is almost the only information astronomers receive from distant objects; physics of light lets them build telescopes and decode the light to find distance, temperature, composition and motion.
**Why:**
- Optics: telescopes (lenses/mirrors) collect and focus light; other telescopes detect radio, IR, UV, X-rays.
- Inverse square law: apparent brightness ∝ true brightness / distance² → distance.
- Atomic spectra: emission/absorption lines → composition and temperature.
- Doppler shift of lines → speed towards/away; light year as distance unit (≈ 9.46 × 10¹² km).

**4.** As physics is the basis for the development of many technologies, how technologies contribute for the development of science particularly physics?

**Answer:** Technology provides the tools to make new observations and precise measurements, which test theories and reveal new phenomena.
**Why:**
- Rockets and satellites → measurements in space.
- X-ray technology → atomic and crystal structure studies.
- Spectrometers and lasers → spectral analysis.
- Accelerators and detectors → new particles; computers → simulation and data analysis.
- Book: "The technology that was developed using science can help to do even more science."

**5.** What major physics knowledge is used in defense radar system to detect the enemy target?

**Answer:** Electromagnetic waves (radio/microwaves): their generation, transmission by an antenna, reflection from the target, speed c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s, and the echo-ranging relation R = ct/2; also the Doppler effect for the target's speed.
**Why:**
- Transmitter → EM pulse → target reflects echo → receiver.
- Time delay t: 2R = ct → R = ct/2. Example: echo after 1.0 ms → R = (3 × 10⁸ × 1.0 × 10⁻³)/2 = 1.5 × 10⁵ m = 150 km.
- Antenna direction gives the target's bearing; frequency shift of the echo gives its speed.

**8.** Identify the difference between the diagnostic and therapeutic medical device.

**Answer:** A diagnostic device collects information (images, sounds, measurements) to find out what is wrong; a therapeutic device treats or cures the condition.

## Notes for lesson design

**Key ideas by subsection**
- Introduction: sciences depend on and reinforce each other; physics ↔ technology ↔ engineering are interlocking gears.
- 1.1 Chemistry: atomic/subatomic physics explains bonding; thermodynamics says if a reaction can go; spectroscopy is a shared tool.
- 1.1 Biology: stability = centre of gravity over base of support; Pascal's principle (earthworm); blood flow (viscosity, continuity); sound from vocal cords during exhalation; nerve signals are electrical pulses; light 400–700 nm drives photosynthesis and vision.
- 1.1 Astronomy: gravity provides centripetal force for orbits; each telescope detects one band of the EM spectrum; false colours; inverse square law of brightness; light year; emission (down) and absorption (up) of photons give composition and temperature.
- 1.1 Geology: rocks studied through density, magnetism, radioactivity, elasticity, electrical properties; waves probe Earth without digging.
- 1.2 Engineering: civil (forces, pressure, gravity), mechanical (mechanics, thermodynamics, aerodynamics), electrical (electromagnetism), chemical (molecular physics, thermodynamics); technology also produces new physics.
- 1.3 Medical: X-ray shadowgram (bone white, lung dark); CT = rotating source and detectors + computer slices; MRI = hydrogen nuclei aligned, pulse, relax, emit energy; ultrasound > 20 kHz (3.5–10 MHz), anechoic/hypoechoic/hyperechoic; radiotherapy ionises cancer cells, beam direction changed to spare healthy tissue.
- 1.4 Defense: radar R = ct/2; cruise (jet, powered all the way) vs ballistic (rocket only at start, then projectile); IR night vision – hotter = brighter.
- 1.5 Communication: wired (wire, optical fibre) vs wireless (radio, microwave, infrared); EM theory is essential.

**Common student mistakes**
- Forgetting the factor 2 in radar: using R = ct instead of R = ct/2.
- Light year is a distance, not a time; arithmetic slips with 365 × 24 × 3600 and powers of ten (m vs km).
- Thinking the inverse square law is 1/d instead of 1/d² (doubling distance → ¼ brightness, not ½).
- Mixing emission and absorption direction (emission = electron falls to lower orbit).
- Believing "centrifugal force" holds satellites up (gravity is the centripetal force).
- Thinking CT and X-ray use different radiation (both use X-rays; CT rotates and uses a computer); thinking MRI uses X-rays or ionising radiation.
- Thinking ultrasound is a high-frequency EM wave (it is a mechanical sound wave above 20 kHz); mixing up anechoic (black, fluid) and hyperechoic (bright).
- Saying sound is produced while breathing in; confusing jets (take oxygen from air) with rockets (carry oxidiser).
- Taking the book's "IR proportional to temperature" or "protons are free hydrogen atoms" literally (see review flags).

**Ideas for interactive practice and pictures**
- Radar range generator: random echo time t (10 μs – 5 ms) → find R = ct/2; or give R and ask for t. E.g. t = 400 μs → R = 60 km.
- Light-travel distance: light-seconds/minutes/years in km (light minute = 1.8 × 10⁷ km; Sun ≈ 8.3 light-minutes = 1.5 × 10⁸ km).
- Inverse-square brightness slider: move a star 2×, 3×, 10× farther → brightness ¼, 1/9, 1/100; or find the distance ratio from a brightness ratio.
- Ultrasound classifier: given a frequency (e.g. 15 Hz, 2 kHz, 25 kHz, 5 MHz) decide infrasound / audible / ultrasound / medical range (3.5–10 MHz). Optional: echo depth d = vt/2 with v ≈ 1540 m/s in tissue.
- Bohr atom picture: click an arrow n3→n1, n2→n1, n1→n3 etc. and say "emission" or "absorption"; count lines from level n (n(n−1)/2).
- Centre-of-gravity balance figure: drag a load to one hand and tilt the body until the c.g. line falls inside the feet; widen/narrow base of support.
- EM spectrum strip (Figure 1.4): drag each telescope type (radio, IR, optical, UV, X-ray, gamma) to its band and to "ground" or "space".
- CT animation: rotating source and detector around a patient slice, building up an image; compare to a single X-ray shadow.
- MRI steps ordering game: align → pulse → flip → relax → emit → detect → image.
- Sorting tasks: physics concept ↔ field (chemistry/biology/astronomy/geology/engineering); diagnostic vs therapeutic devices; cruise vs ballistic missile; wired vs wireless communication.
