## Introduction

Kinematics is the study of motion without considering its causes. For example, studying the motion of a football without considering what forces cause or change its motion. Two-dimensional kinematics are simple extensions of the one-dimensional kinematics developed for motion in a straight line in Grade 11. This simple extension will allow us to apply physics to many more situations, and it will also yield unexpected insights about nature.

A ball kicked by a football player, the orbital motion of planets, a bicycle rounding a curve, the rotation of wheels of a car are a few examples of two-dimension motion. In fact, most motions in nature follow curved paths rather than straight lines. Such types of motion along a curved plane are described by two-dimensional kinematics.

**At the end of this unit, you will be able to:**
- Understand the basic ideas of two-dimensional motions.
- Describe the motion of objects in horizontal and inclined projectiles;
- Describe uniform rotational motion, rotational dynamics and Kepler's laws
- Describe Newton's law of Universal gravitation.
- Develop pertinent problem-solving skills.

### Brain storming question 2.1

1. Consider a ball shot horizontally from a very high building at a high speed. Assume that there is no force of gravity acting on the ball. What would the motion of the ball be like? Explain its motion?

**Answer:** It would move in a straight horizontal line at constant speed forever (ignoring air resistance).
**Why:**
- With no gravity (and no air resistance) the net force on the ball is zero.
- By Newton's first law, an object with zero net force keeps a constant velocity.
- The ball leaves the building with a horizontal velocity, so it keeps exactly that velocity: straight line, same speed, never falls.

2. The ball is projected horizontally from the top of the same building. This time, the force of gravity is acting on the ball. What will the motion of the ball be like? Will gravity affect the ball's horizontal motion? Will the ball travel a greater (or shorter) horizontal distance due to the influence of gravity?

**Answer:** The ball follows a curved (parabolic) path down to the ground. Gravity does not change its horizontal motion, but it limits the horizontal distance: the ball only travels horizontally for as long as it is in the air.
**Why:**
- Gravity acts only vertically (downward), so it gives a vertical acceleration g but no horizontal acceleration; the horizontal velocity stays constant.
- Vertically the ball starts from rest and speeds up downward, so the path bends into a parabola.
- The ball hits the ground after t = √(2h/g); its horizontal distance is v_0x × t, a finite value. Without gravity it would never land, so with gravity the horizontal distance travelled is shorter (finite).
- During the time it is in the air, it covers exactly the same horizontal distance per second as it would without gravity.

(p.25)

## 2.1 Projectile motion

**At the end of this section, you will be able to:**
- Explain the motion of the projectile with respect to the horizontal and vertical components of its motion.
- Derive equations related to projectile motion.
- Apply equations to solve problems related to projectile motion

### Discussion question 2.1

Which motion is different from the others? Explain Why?
a) A ball thrown horizontally into the air.
b) A bullet fired from a gun.
c) A javelin thrown by an athlete.
d) A bird flying in the air.

**Answer:** d) A bird flying in the air.
**Why:**
- (a), (b) and (c): once released or fired, each object moves only under gravity (air resistance ignored), so each is a projectile following a parabolic path.
- (d): a bird uses its wings to produce lift and thrust; forces other than gravity act on it and it controls its own path, so it is not a projectile.

A projectile is a thrown, fired, or released object that moves only under the influence of gravitational force. The projectile acceleration is g = 9.8 m/s². Anyone who has observed the motion of a ball kicked by a football player (Figure 2.1b) has observed projectile motion. The ball moves in a curved path and returns to the ground. Other examples of projectile motion include a cannonball fired from a cannon, a bullet fired from a gun, the flight of a golf ball and a jet of water escaping a hose.

[figure: Figure 2.1 a) A ball thrown horizontally — ball leaves the top of a height H with horizontal velocity V_0 and curves down, landing a horizontal distance R from the base. b) A football kicked in a game — ball launched from the ground with velocity V_0 at angle θ above the horizontal, follows a symmetric arch and lands at horizontal distance R on the same level.]

Projectile motion of an object is simple to analyze if we make three assumptions:
1. The free-fall acceleration is constant over the range of motion, and it is always directed downward. It is the acceleration due to gravity (g) = 9.8 m/s².
2. The effect of air resistance is negligible.
3. The horizontal velocity is constant because the acceleration of the object does not have vertical component. With these assumptions, we find that the path of a projectile, which we call its trajectory, is a parabola as shown in Figure ??.

[review: "Figure ??" is a broken cross-reference in the book; the parabola is shown in Figure 2.1 / Figure 2.4. Also assumption 3 is worded backwards: the horizontal velocity is constant because the acceleration has no HORIZONTAL component (the acceleration is purely vertical).]

The horizontal and vertical components of a projectile's motion are completely independent of each other and can be handled separately, with time t as a (p.26) common variable for both components.

### Horizontal Projection

In this type of motion the projectile is projected horizontally from a certain height as shown in Figure 2.2. Its initial velocity along the vertical direction is zero and it possesses only horizontal velocity at the beginning. As the time progresses, due to the impact of gravity, it acquires the vertical component of velocity (Figure 2.2).

[figure: Figure 2.2 The motion of a ball projected horizontally — ball starts at top left with horizontal velocity v_0x; at two later points the velocity v is drawn with its components: horizontal v_0x (same length each time) and vertical v_y1, then a longer v_y2 (growing downward). Total height Δy is marked on the left, horizontal distance Δx along the bottom.]

#### Equations for the horizontal component of motion

The projectile has zero acceleration along x direction. Therefore, the initial velocity v_0x remains constant throughout the motion. We use constant acceleration motion equations. The final horizontal velocity, v_x after a time t is:

v_x = v_0x (constant)

The horizontal distance traveled by the projectile at a time t is given by the equation

Δx = v_0x t  (2.1)

### Discussion question 2.2

Assume that an airplane flying horizontally drops a package to a remote village.
What kind of motion is performed by the package?
Draw the trajectory of the package.
As the package hits the ground at the village, where is the aircraft?

**Answer:** The package performs horizontal projectile motion; its path is half a parabola curving downward and forward; when it lands, the aircraft is directly above it (ignoring air resistance).
**Why:**
- When released, the package has the plane's horizontal velocity and zero vertical velocity, and then only gravity acts on it → horizontal projection.
- Trajectory: starts horizontal under the plane, then bends more and more steeply downward (a half-parabola), like Figure 2.2.
- Horizontally, both plane and package move at the same constant speed, so they cover the same horizontal distance in the same time; the package stays vertically below the plane all the way down and lands directly beneath it.

#### Equations of vertical motion

The vertical motion is a constant accelerated motion. We use the kinematic equations of motion for constant accelerated motion. The final vertical velocity v_y after time t is:

v_y = v_0y + g t  (2.2)

Where v_0y is the initial vertical velocity.

The initial vertical velocity has no downward component (v_0y = 0). Therefore

v_y = g t

From the kinematics equations, the vertical displacement, Δy has a form:

Δy = v_0y t + ½ g t²  (2.3)

But v_0y = 0, therefore

(p.27)

Δy = ½ g t²

**Remember:** When you use equations to answer questions on vertical motion, upwards motion is positive (+) and downwards motion is negative (-).

[review: In equations 2.2–2.6 the book treats g as a signed quantity, g = −9.8 m/s² (downward negative), even though the text above says g = 9.8 m/s². Students should either use g = −9.8 m/s² in these equations, or use the magnitude and write v_y = v_0y − g t, Δy = v_0y t − ½ g t². The examples use g = −10 m/s² with Δy negative.]

#### Time of flight

The time of flight is the time taken by the projectile to hit the ground.
We know that:

Δy = ½ g t²

Then

t = √(2Δy / g)

#### Range

The range is the maximum horizontal distance traveled by the projectile.
Once we find the time of flight t, we can solve for the horizontal displacement using:

Δx = v_0x t

In projectile motion, the time to cover both the x and y displacement is the same. By substituting the total time flight, we get:

R = v_0x √(2Δy / g)

### Activity 2.1

Place two tennis balls at the edge of a tabletop. Sharply snap one ball horizontally off the table with one hand while gently tapping the second ball off with your other hand. Measure the height (y) of the table and the horizontal distance between the table's edge and the balls landing location (R). Determine the following from your measurements:

a) The time of flight of both tennis balls. Explain your result.

**Answer:** Both balls have the same time of flight, t = √(2y/g). For a typical table of height 0.80 m, t = √(2 × 0.80 / 9.8) ≈ 0.40 s.
**Why:**
- Both balls leave the table with zero vertical velocity, and both fall the same height y with the same acceleration g.
- The vertical motion does not depend on the horizontal speed (independence of components), so t = √(2y/g) for both.
- Example arithmetic: 2 × 0.80 m = 1.6 m; 1.6 m ÷ 9.8 m/s² = 0.163 s²; √0.163 ≈ 0.40 s.
- They land at the same moment (you hear one "thud"), but the snapped ball lands farther away.

b) The initial horizontal velocity of the balls when they leave the edge of the table.

**Answer:** v_0x = R / t = R / √(2y/g) for each ball. Example: if the snapped ball lands R = 1.2 m away from a 0.80 m table, v_0x = 1.2 m ÷ 0.40 s = 3.0 m/s.
**Why:**
- Horizontally there is no acceleration, so R = v_0x t.
- Rearrange: v_0x = R / t, using t from part (a).
- Example: 1.2 m ÷ 0.404 s ≈ 2.97 m/s ≈ 3.0 m/s. The gently tapped ball has a smaller R, so a smaller v_0x.

### Example 2.1

A rifle is aimed horizontally at a target 30 m away as shown in Figure 2.3. The bullet hits the target 2 cm below the aiming point.
(a) What is the bullet's time of flight?
(b) What is the initial velocity of the bullet?
Assume gravity (g) = 10 m/s².

**Solution:**
The givens in this question are: ΔX = 30 m, ΔY = 2 cm = 0.02 m, g = 10 m/s².

(a) The equation for the vertical displacement is:

(p.28)

Δy = ½ g t²

−0.02 m = ½ (−10) t²

[added step: t² = (2 × 0.02 m) / (10 m/s²) = 0.004 s², so t = √0.004 s ≈ 0.063 s]

The vertical displacement is in the negative direction, which gives:

t = 0.06 s

Since this is the time of impact with the target, the time of flight of the bullet is also the same.

(b) The equation for x−motion is:

Δx = v_0x t

v_0x = Δx / t = 30 m / 0.06 s

[added step: 30 ÷ 0.06 = 500]

The initial velocity of the bullet is 500 m/s.

[review: t = √0.004 = 0.0632 s, which the book rounds to 0.06 s. Using the unrounded time gives v_0x = 30 m ÷ 0.0632 s ≈ 474 m/s (≈ 470 m/s), not 500 m/s. The 500 m/s comes from rounding t too early. (With g = 9.8 m/s²: t ≈ 0.0639 s, v_0x ≈ 469 m/s.)]

### Inclined projectile motion

This is a type of motion in which an object is projected with an initial velocity v_0 which makes an angle θ with the horizontal (Figure ??). The initial velocity can be resolved into two components, vertical and horizontal component. The vertical component of the velocity changes with time as a result there is acceleration due (p.30) to gravity.

[review: "Figure ??" is a broken cross-reference; it should be Figure 2.4.]

The horizontal component of the velocity is constant throughout the flight; this is because there is no force acting along the horizontal direction of the projectile as a result there is no acceleration along x-axis. The analysis of the motion involves dealing with the two motions.

As shown in Figure 2.4, the projectile has velocity components at different positions. At the top where it reaches its maximum height the vertical component of the velocity becomes zero. After V_y becomes zero the projectile changes its direction and make free fall.

### Discussion question 2.3

Balls A and B are kicked at an angle of 37° and 53° with the horizontal respectively, with the same initial velocity v_0. Which ball has:
a) the maximum horizontal displacement?
b) the maximum height?

**Answer:** a) Neither — both have the same horizontal displacement (range), because 37° + 53° = 90°. b) Ball B (53°) reaches the greater height.
**Why:**
- R = v_0² sin 2θ / g. Ball A: sin 74° ≈ 0.96. Ball B: sin 106° = sin(180° − 74°) = sin 74° ≈ 0.96. Same v_0 and g → same R.
- H = v_0² sin²θ / (2g). sin²37° ≈ 0.6² = 0.36; sin²53° ≈ 0.8² = 0.64. So H_B / H_A = 0.64 / 0.36 ≈ 1.78 → ball B goes higher.
- Example with v_0 = 20 m/s, g = 10 m/s²: R = 400 × 0.96 / 10 = 38.4 m for both; H_A = 400 × 0.36 / 20 = 7.2 m, H_B = 400 × 0.64 / 20 = 12.8 m.

[figure: Figure 2.4 Inclined projectile motion — x–y axes; projectile launched from origin O with velocity V_0 at angle θ, components V_ox (horizontal) and V_oy (vertical). Dashed green parabola. On the way up, velocity points up-right with V_x = V_ox; at the top point A, V_x = V_ox and V_y = 0, the height H is marked from the x-axis to A; on the way down velocity points down-right with V_x = V_ox; at landing point B on the x-axis, V_x = V_ox and V_y = −V_oy. Horizontal range R is marked from O to B.]

#### Equations of inclined projectile motion

The initial velocity can be expressed as x component and y component:

v_0x = v_0 cos θ
v_0y = v_0 sin θ

The horizontal velocity at any time t is:

v_x = v_0 cos θ (constant)

The vertical velocity at any time t is:

v_y = v_0 sin θ + g t  (2.4)

(p.31)

#### Displacements of the projectile

There are two different types of displacement of the projectile motion:
Horizontal displacement at any time t:

Δx = v_0 cos θ t  (2.5)

Vertical displacement at any time t:

Δy = v_0 sin θ t + ½ g t²  (2.6)

The time to reach the maximum height is:

v_y = v_0 sin θ + g t

Since v_y = 0 at maximum height and g is negative:

t = v_0 sin θ / g

[review: with g negative, solving 0 = v_0 sin θ + g t gives t = −v_0 sin θ / g. The book's t = v_0 sin θ / g is correct only if g here means the magnitude 9.8 m/s². Same for t_total and H below: in those final formulas g is the positive magnitude.]

#### Time of flight

The time of flight is the total time for which the projectile remains in flight. The time of flight depends on the initial velocity of the object and the angle of the projection, θ.

Δy = v_0 sin θ t + ½ g t²

When the point of projection and point of return are on the same horizontal level, the net vertical displacement of the object is zero, ΔY = 0.

0 = v_0 sin θ t + ½ g t²

Apply factorization, we have:

0 = t (v_0 sin θ + ½ g t)

Since t cannot be zero and g is negative, solving for t gives us:

t_total = 2 v_0 sin θ / g

[added step: t = 0 is the launch moment, so the landing time comes from v_0 sin θ + ½ g t = 0 → t = −2 v_0 sin θ / g = 2 v_0 sin θ / |g|. Note t_total = 2 × (time to maximum height).]

This last equation does not apply when the projectile lands at a different elevation from the one at which it was launched.

(p.32)

### Horizontal range and maximum height of a Projectile

Let us now consider a special case of projectile motion. Assume a projectile is launched from the origin at O, as shown in Figure 2.4, and returns to the same horizontal level. This situation is common in sports, where baseballs, footballs and golf balls often land at the same level from which they were launched. Two points in this motion are especially interesting to analyze: the peak point A, which has Cartesian coordinates (R/2, H), and the point B, which has coordinates (R, 0). The distance R is called the horizontal range of the projectile, and the distance H is its maximum height. Let us find R and H mathematically in terms of v_0, θ, and g.

#### Range (R)

The range of the projectile is the maximum displacement in the horizontal direction. There is no acceleration in this direction since gravity only acts vertically.

Δx = v_0 cos θ t

When Δx is maximum, Δx = R.
Since the time to cover the range is the total time of flight:

t_total = 2 v_0 sin θ / g

R = v_0 cos θ t_total

[added step: R = v_0 cos θ × (2 v_0 sin θ / g) = v_0² (2 sin θ cos θ) / g, and 2 sin θ cos θ = sin 2θ]

R = v_0² sin 2θ / g

This equation is valid for launch and impact on a horizontal surface, as shown in Figure 2.5. We can see in Figure 2.5a the range is directly proportional to the square of the initial speed v_0 and sin 2θ. Furthermore, we can see from the factor sin 2θ that the range is maximum at 45°.

[added step: sin 2θ has its largest value 1 when 2θ = 90°, i.e. θ = 45°, so R_max = v_0² / g.]

In Figure 2.5 (a) we can see that the greater the initial velocity, the greater the range. In Figure (b) the range is maximum at 45°. This is true only for conditions ignoring air resistance. It is interesting that the same range is found for two initial launch angles that add up to 90°. The projectile launched with the smaller angle has a lower peak than the higher angle, but they both have the same range.

(p.33)

[figure: Figure 2.5 Trajectories of projectiles on leveled ground. (a) The effect of initial velocity v_0 on the range of a projectile with a given initial angle — all launched at 45°: 30 m/s gives R = 91.8 m, 40 m/s gives R = 163 m, 50 m/s gives R = 255 m. (b) The effect of initial angle θ on the range of a projectile with a given initial speed — v_0 = 50 m/s at 15°, 45° and 75°: 15° and 75° both give R = 128 m (75° goes much higher), 45° gives R = 255 m. (Values match R = v_0² sin 2θ / g with g = 9.8 m/s².)]

#### Maximum height (H)

The maximum height of a projectile trajectory occurs when the vertical component of velocity, v_y equals zero. As the projectile moves upwards it goes against gravity, and therefore the velocity begins to decrease. Eventually the vertical velocity will reach zero, and the projectile is immediately accelerated downward under gravity. Thus, once the projectile reaches its maximum height, it begins to accelerate downward.

Δy = v_0 sin θ t + ½ g t²

The time to cover the maximum height is: t = v_0 sin θ / g

When Δy is maximum, Δy = H

[added step: substitute t = v_0 sin θ / g (g as magnitude, gravity term negative): H = v_0 sin θ (v_0 sin θ / g) − ½ g (v_0 sin θ / g)² = v_0² sin²θ / g − v_0² sin²θ / (2g) = v_0² sin²θ / (2g)]

H = v_0² sin²θ / (2g)

[review: the book's text says "once the projectile reaches its maximum height, it begins to accelerate downward". In fact the acceleration is downward (g) during the whole flight, including on the way up; what changes at the top is the direction of the vertical velocity.]

### Discussion question 2.4

1. A projectile is fired in such a way that its horizontal range is equal to three times its maximum height. What is the angle of projection?

**Answer:** θ ≈ 53° (tan θ = 4/3).
**Why:**
- R = 3H.
- Use H = R tan θ / 4 (derived on p.34), or divide: H/R = [v_0² sin²θ / (2g)] ÷ [v_0² · 2 sin θ cos θ / g] = tan θ / 4.
- R = 3H → H/R = 1/3 → tan θ / 4 = 1/3 → tan θ = 4/3 = 1.333.
- θ = tan⁻¹(1.333) = 53.1° ≈ 53°.
- Check: sin 53° ≈ 0.8, cos 53° ≈ 0.6: H/R = 0.8 / (4 × 0.6) = 0.8/2.4 = 1/3 ✓.

2. A ball is kicked into the air from the ground at an angle θ with the horizontal. When the ball reaches its highest point, which statement is true? (a) Both the velocity and acceleration of the ball are zero. (p.34) (b) Its velocity is not zero, but its acceleration is zero. (c) Its velocity is perpendicular to its acceleration. (d) Its acceleration depends on the angle at which the ball was thrown.

**Answer:** (c) Its velocity is perpendicular to its acceleration.
**Why:**
- At the top, v_y = 0 but v_x = v_0 cos θ ≠ 0, so the velocity is horizontal; the acceleration g is vertically downward → they are at 90°.
- (a) wrong: velocity is not zero (v_x remains) and acceleration is g, not zero.
- (b) wrong: acceleration is g = 9.8 m/s² downward all the time, never zero.
- (d) wrong: the acceleration is always g, whatever the launch angle.

3. One ball is thrown horizontally. At the same time, a second ball is dropped from the same height. Ignoring air resistance and assuming the ground is level, which ball hits the ground first? Explain why.

**Answer:** Both hit the ground at the same time.
**Why:**
- Both start with zero vertical velocity from the same height h.
- Both have the same vertical acceleration g; horizontal motion does not affect vertical motion.
- So both fall for t = √(2h/g); the thrown ball just lands farther away.

### Relation between range and maximum height

Consider a projectile motion as shown in Figure 2.4. The initial velocity of the projectile is v_0, H is the maximum height and R is its horizontal range. We know that the maximum height of the projectile H is given by the equation:

H = v_0² sin²θ / (2g)

And horizontal range is given by the equation:

R = v_0² sin 2θ / g

Divide the maximum height of the projectile by the horizontal range.
(In the equation, sin²θ can be written as sin θ sin θ, and sin 2θ can be written as 2 sin θ cos θ).

[added step: H/R = [v_0² sin θ sin θ / (2g)] × [g / (v_0² · 2 sin θ cos θ)] = sin θ / (4 cos θ)]

H/R = sin θ / (4 cos θ)

H = R tan θ / 4

## 2.2 Rotational Motion

**At the end of this section, you will be able to:**
- Describe the motion of a rigid object around a fixed axis.
- Derive equations of motion with constant angular acceleration.
- Apply equations to solve problems related to rotational motion.

[figure: Figure 2.7 Rotation of a disc of mass M around a fixed axis — flat disc of radius R on a vertical rod (axis of rotation), curved arrow at the top showing the spin direction, a dashed circle on the disc showing a particle's circular path.]

Rotational motion is the motion of an object in a circle around a fixed axis. For example, the rotation of Earth around its axis, the rotation of the flywheel of a sewing machine, rotation of a ceiling fan, rotation of wheels of a car, and so on.

The disc in Figure 2.7 is performing rotational motion because all of its particles are rotating around a fixed axis, called its axis of rotation. An object can rotate around a fixed point in two directions: a clockwise or an anticlockwise direction (also known as counterclockwise).

Rigid body is an object with a perfectly defined and unchanging shape. No matter the size of the force, the distance between any two particles within the object remains constant.

### Angular displacement and angular velocity

#### Angular displacement (Δθ)

Figure 2.8 is a view from above of a rotating compact disc, or CD. The disc rotates around a fixed axis perpendicular to the plane of the figure, passing through the center of the disc at O. One particle of the disc P, is kept at a fixed distance r from the origin and rotates around O in a circle of radius r.

[figure: Figure 2.8 A CD rotating about a fixed axis through O perpendicular to the plane of the figure — point P on the disc at distance r from centre O; the radius OP makes angle θ with a horizontal reference line; the arc length s from the reference line to P is marked; a curved arrow shows counterclockwise rotation.]

Because the disc is a rigid object, as the particle moves through an angle θ from the reference line, every other particle on the object rotates through the same angle. Therefore, we can associate the angle θ with the entire rigid object as well as with an individual particle.

[figure: Figure 2.9 A particle P on a rotating disc moves from A to B along the arc of a circle — x–y axes with origin O; at time t_0 the particle is at A at angle θ_0 from the x-axis; at time t_f it is at B at angle θ_f; both at radius r; the arc from A to B is dashed, rotation counterclockwise.]

As the particle travels from position A to position B in a time interval Δt, as shown in Figure 2.9, the line joining the particle to the center sweeps out an angle Δθ. This quantity Δθ is defined as the angular displacement of the rigid object.

(p.40)

Δθ = θ_f − θ_0  (2.7)

Because rotational motion involves studying circular paths, rather than using meters to describe the angular displacement of an object, physicists use radians or degrees. A radian is convenient because it naturally expresses angles in terms of π since one complete turn of a circle (360 degrees) equals 2π radians.

1 revolution = 2π rad = 360°

#### Angular velocity (ω)

How fast an object is rotating can be calculated using the concept of angular velocity. If the disc spins rapidly, the angular displacement can occur in a short time interval. If it rotates slowly, the angular displacement occurs in a longer time interval. The rate at which angular displacement occurs can vary. These different rotation rates can be quantified by defining the average angular velocity ω_av (Greek letter omega) as the ratio of the angular displacement of a disc to the time interval Δt during which the displacement occurs.

ω_av = (θ_f − θ_0) / (t_f − t_0) = Δθ / Δt  (2.8)

Angular velocity has units of radians per second (rad/s).

#### Angular acceleration

If the angular velocity of an object changes from ω_0 to ω_f in the time interval Δt, the object has an angular acceleration. The angular acceleration α (Greek letter alpha) of a rotating rigid object is defined as the ratio of the change in the angular speed to the time interval Δt during which the change in the angular speed occurs:

α = (ω_f − ω_0) / (t_f − t_0) = Δω / Δt  (2.9)

Angular acceleration has units of radians per second squared (rad/s²).

(p.41)

### Direction of angular velocity and angular acceleration

### Discussion question 2.6

In small group, discuss how to find the direction of angular velocity and angular acceleration.

**Answer:** Use the right-hand rule for ω; α points the same way as ω when the rotation speeds up and opposite to ω when it slows down.
**Why:**
- Both ω and α lie along the axis of rotation.
- Right-hand rule: curl the four fingers of the right hand in the direction of rotation; the thumb points along ω. Counterclockwise seen from above → ω points up (toward the viewer); clockwise → ω points down (away).
- α = Δω/Δt: if the spin rate increases, Δω is along ω, so α is parallel to ω; if it decreases, α is antiparallel to ω.

Angular velocity and angular accelerations can be treated as vectors, so we must include magnitude and direction. For rotation around a fixed axis, the direction of rotational motion is specified in relation to the direction along the axis of rotation. Therefore, the directions of ω and α are along this axis.

To illustrate this convention, it is convenient to use the *right-hand rule* demonstrated in Figure 2.10. When the four fingers of the right hand are wrapped in the direction of rotation, the extended right thumb points in the direction of ω. The direction of α follows from its definition α = Δω/Δt. It is in the same direction as ω if the angular speed is increasing in time, and it is antiparallel (parallel but moving in the opposite direction) to ω if the angular speed is decreasing in time.

[figure: Figure 2.10 The right-hand rule for determining the direction of the angular velocity vector — left: right hand with fingers curled around a vertical green arrow, thumb up along the arrow labelled "Angular Velocity Vector ω"; a curved arrow under the hand is labelled "Clockwise Rotation". a) Rotation rate counterclockwise and increasing: ω up, α (red) also up. b) Rotation rate counterclockwise and decreasing: ω up, α (red) down.]

[review: in Figure 2.10 the curled fingers with the thumb pointing up correspond to COUNTERCLOCKWISE rotation as seen from above, but the label under the hand reads "Clockwise Rotation". Seen from above, ω pointing up means counterclockwise rotation (as in panels a and b).]

### Equation of motion for constant angular acceleration

Consider a rigid object such as the CD rotating around a fixed axis with a constant angular acceleration. A set of kinematic equations exist for rotational motion just as they do for translational motion. They have a similar form and are derived in a similar fashion.

α = (ω_f − ω_0) / (t_f − t_0)  (constant angular acceleration)  (2.10)

Then, by rearranging, we get an equation

(p.42)

ω_f = ω_0 + α Δt  (2.11)

where ω_0 is the angular speed of the rigid object at time t = 0. This equation allows us to find the angular speed ω_f of the object at any later time t.

If the angular acceleration is constant, the average angular velocity is obtained by:

ω_av = (ω_0 + ω_f) / 2

ω_av = Δθ / Δt

Combining these two equations, we get:

Δθ / Δt = (ω_0 + ω_f) / 2

When we substitute ω_f = ω_0 + α Δt

[added step: Δθ = ½ (ω_0 + ω_0 + α Δt) Δt = ½ (2ω_0 + α Δt) Δt = ω_0 Δt + ½ α Δt²]

Δθ = ω_0 Δt + ½ α Δt²  (2.12)

This equation allows us to find the angular displacement of the object at any later time t.

We know that: Δθ = ((ω_0 + ω_f) / 2) Δt  and  Δt = (ω_f − ω_0) / α

Combining these two equations, we get:

Δθ = ((ω_0 + ω_f) / 2) ((ω_f − ω_0) / α)

[added step: Δθ = (ω_f² − ω_0²) / (2α), so 2α Δθ = ω_f² − ω_0²]

ω_f² = ω_0² + 2α Δθ  (2.13)

This equation allows us to find the angular speed ω_f of the rigid object for any value of its angular position Δθ.

### Kinematic equations for rotational and linear motion

The kinematics for rotational motion is completely analogous to linear (or translational) kinematics. Many of the equations for the mechanics of rotating objects are similar to the motion equations for linear motion. When solving problems involving rotational motion, we use variables that are similar to linear variables (distance, velocity and acceleration) but take into account the curvature or rotation of the motion. We defined:
- the angular rotation Δθ, which is the angular equivalence of distance, Δs;
- the angular velocity ω, which is the angular equivalence of linear velocity v;
- the angular acceleration α, which is the angular equivalence of linear acceleration, a.

## Notes for lesson design

**Key ideas by subsection**
- Unit intro: 2-D kinematics extends 1-D kinematics; most real motion is curved (projectiles, orbits, rotating wheels).
- 2.1 Projectile basics: a projectile moves under gravity only; assumptions (constant g downward, no air resistance, constant horizontal velocity); horizontal and vertical motions are independent and share the time t; trajectory is a parabola.
- Horizontal projection: v_0y = 0; x: v_x = v_0x, Δx = v_0x t; y: v_y = g t, Δy = ½ g t²; time of flight t = √(2h/g) depends only on height; range R = v_0x √(2h/g).
- Inclined projection: v_0x = v_0 cos θ, v_0y = v_0 sin θ; v_y = v_0 sin θ + g t (g negative); at the top v_y = 0 but v_x ≠ 0; time to top v_0 sin θ / g; t_total = 2 v_0 sin θ / g (same-level landing only).
- Range and height: R = v_0² sin 2θ / g (max at 45°, R_max = v_0²/g; complementary angles give equal R); H = v_0² sin²θ / (2g); H = R tan θ / 4.
- 2.2 Rotational motion: rigid body; every particle turns through the same angle; Δθ = θ_f − θ_0; 1 rev = 2π rad = 360°; ω_av = Δθ/Δt (rad/s); α = Δω/Δt (rad/s²); right-hand rule for ω; α parallel to ω when speeding up, antiparallel when slowing down.
- Rotational kinematics: ω_f = ω_0 + αt, Δθ = ω_0 t + ½ α t², ω_f² = ω_0² + 2αΔθ, ω_av = (ω_0 + ω_f)/2 — a one-to-one analogy with linear kinematics (Table 2.1); link a_t = α r.

**Common student mistakes**
- Sign of g: mixing g = +9.8 and −9.8 in the same equation; getting negative or imaginary times. Pick "up = +" and keep g = −9.8 m/s² (or use magnitudes with explicit minus signs).

#### Relationship between angular motion and translational motion quantities

In this section, we derive some useful relationships between the angular quantities θ, ω and α of a rotating rigid object and the corresponding linear quantities s, v, and a of a point, p in the object. To do so, we must keep in mind that when a rigid object rotates around a fixed axis as in Figure 2.11, every particle of the object moves in a circle whose center is on the axis of rotation.

As the particle moves along the circle through an angular displacement of θ, it moves through an arc length s.

The arc length s is related to the angle θ through the equation:

s = rθ    (2.14)

Note that in this equation the angular displacement must be expressed in rad (not degrees or revolutions).

(p.47)

Because point p, in the figure moves in a circle, the translational velocity vector v⃗ is always tangent to the circular path, and hence is called tangential velocity.

[figure: Figure 2.11 — As a rigid object rotates around the fixed axis (the z axis) through O, the point P has a tangential velocity v⃗ that is always a tangent to the circular path of radius r. Drawing: an irregular blob-shaped rigid body on x–y axes with origin O; a radius line r from O to point P at angle θ above the x axis; a red arrow v at P tangent to the circle; dashed arc s from the x axis to P; curved arrow showing rotation counterclockwise.]

The magnitude of the tangential velocity of the point P is by definition the tangential speed v = Δs/Δt, where s is the distance traveled by this point measured along the circular path. Recalling that Δs = rΔθ and noting that r is constant, we get:

v = Δs/Δt = r Δθ/Δt    (2.15)

Because ω = Δθ/Δt, it follows that:

v = ωr    (2.16)

Therefore, the tangential speed of a point on a rotating rigid object equals the radius multiplied by the angular speed. Although every point on the rigid object has the same angular speed, not every point has the same tangential speed because r is not the same for all points on the object. The tangential speed of a point on the rotating object increases as it moves outward from the center of rotation.

We can relate the angular acceleration of the rotating rigid object to the tangential acceleration of the point P by taking the rate of change v.

a = Δv/Δt = r Δω/Δt    (2.17)

Because α = Δω/Δt, it follows that

a = rα    (2.18)

That is, the tangential component of the translational acceleration of a point on a rotating rigid object equals the radius multiplied by the angular acceleration.

## 2.3 Rotational Dynamics

**At the end of this section, you will be able to:**
- Define the physical concept of torque in terms of force and distance from axis of rotation.
- Define the physical concept of moment of inertia in terms of point mass and distance from the axis of rotation.
- Express torque in terms of moment of inertia and angular acceleration.
- Solve problems involving torque and rotational kinematics.

Having developed the kinematics of rotational motion, we now turn to the dynamics of rotational motion. Just as force played a big role in linear dynamics, we have a torque in rotational dynamics. We begin by defining this quantity and showing how it acts on objects in rotational motion. Next, we relate torque to our study of kinematics through an equation very similar to Newton's second law.

#### Torque

Torque is the rotational effect of force. Torque is what causes an object to acquire angular acceleration. If F is the force acting on an object and r is the distance from the axis of rotation to the point of application of the force, as shown in Figure 2.14, the magnitude of the torque is given by:

τ = rF sinθ    (2.19)

where θ is the angle between r and F when they are drawn from the same origin.

[figure: Figure 2.14 — Counterclockwise rotation by F around the pivot point. A rod pivoted at O (left end) rising to the right; the r direction is a dashed arrow along the rod. Three forces: F₁ pointing straight down from a point near the middle, angle 120° marked between r and F₁; F₂ pointing up-left from the same region, angle 150° marked; F₃ at the far end pointing up, 45° marked between the r extension and F₃. (Same drawing as Figure 2.15.)]

Torque is a vector quantity, meaning it has both a direction and a magnitude. Its SI unit is Nm. The direction of the torque is along the axis of rotation. It is (p.51) determined by a right-hand-rule: when you curl the fingers of your right hand in the direction of the rotation, your thumb points in the direction of the torque.

#### Moment of inertia (I)

The moment of inertia of an object is the quantitative measure of rotational inertia, just as mass is the quantitative measure of linear inertia inertia in translational motion. The greater the moment of inertia of a rigid object or system of particles, the greater is its resistance to change in angular velocity about a fixed axis of rotation.

The moment of inertia depends on the mass and axis of rotation of the body. The moment of inertia is given the symbol I. For a single point mass, as shown in (p.52) Figure 2.16, rotating at radius r from the axis of rotation the moment of inertia is:

[figure: Figure 2.16 — Point mass rotating about O. A green dot m at distance r from centre O, on a dotted circle, with a small arrow showing the direction of motion.]

I = mr²    (2.20)

From the formula, the SI unit of moment of inertia is kg m². Moment of inertia is a scalar quantity.

The moment of inertia for more than one particle around a fixed axis is:

I = m₁r₁² + m₂r₂² + m₃r₃² …

#### Torque and angular acceleration

When a number of individual forces act on a rotating object, we can calculate the net torque:

τ_net = τ₁ + τ₂ + τ₃ …

We can relate the net torque to angular acceleration α, by analogy with Newton's second law of motion (F = ma). We replace m by I and a by α.

τ = Iα    (2.21)

The angular acceleration of a rotating object is proportional to the net torque on the object.

## 2.4 Planetary motion and Kepler's laws

**At the end of this section, you will be able to:**
- Describe the motion of the planets around the Sun.
- State Kepler's three laws.
- Apply equations to solve problems related to orbital motion.

[figure: Figure 2.19 — Earth with its Moon revolving around the Sun. Sun in the middle of an elliptical orbit; several Earth positions on the orbit with red arrows showing direction; a small loop around one Earth showing the Moon's orbit.]

The planets orbit the Sun. They maintain their respective distances from the Sun. They do not cross each other as they revolve around the Sun. Kepler's laws describe how planetary bodies orbit around the Sun.

#### Kepler's laws

Humans have observed the movements of the planets, stars, and other celestial objects for thousands of years. In early history, these observations led scientists to regard Earth as the center of the Universe. This geocentric model was elaborated and formalized by the Greek astronomer Claudius Ptolemy (c.100–c.170) in the second century and was accepted for the next 1400 years.

In 1543, Polish astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543) suggested that Earth and the other planets revolved in circular orbits around the Sun (the (p.55) heliocentric model).

Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe (1546–1601) wanted to determine how the universe was constructed and pursued a project to determine the positions of both stars and planets. His observations of the planets and stars visible from Earth were carried out using only a large sextant and a compass. (The telescope had not yet been invented.)

German astronomer Johannes Kepler was Brahe's assistant for a short while before Brahe's death, where upon he acquired his mentor's astronomical data. Kepler spent 16 years trying to deduce a mathematical model for the motion of the planets. Such data are difficult to sort out because the moving planets are observed from a moving Earth. After many lengthy calculations, Kepler found that Brahe's data on the revolution of Mars around the Sun led to a successful model. Kepler's complete analysis of planetary motion is summarized in three statements known as Kepler's laws.

**Why:**
- Circle: all points at the same distance r from one centre.
- Ellipse: r₁ + r₂ = constant for every point (Figure 2.20b); it has a long (major) and short (minor) axis.
- A circle is the special ellipse whose two foci coincide.

#### Kepler's first law

Kepler's first law is sometimes referred to as the law of ellipses. It states that the orbit of a planet around the Sun is an ellipse (near circular, oval) with the Sun at one focus (Figure 2.20a).

The planet follows the ellipse in its orbit, meaning that the planet-to-Sun distance is constantly changing as the planet goes around its orbit. An ellipse is a closed curve such that the sum of the distances from a point on the curve (r₁ + r₂) to the two foci, f₁ and f₂ is constant, as shown in Figure 2.20b.

[figure: Figure 2.20 — (a) The motion of a planet about the Sun: an ellipse with the Sun (mass M) at the left focus; planet m on the orbit above the Sun; "perihelion" labelled at the left end of the long axis (nearest the Sun) and "aphelion" at the right end; the empty second focus f₂ marked. (b) Any distance drawn from f₁ and f₂ to a point on the curve add up to a constant: ellipse on x–y axes with foci f₁ (Sun, M) and f₂ on the x axis; lines r₁ from f₁ and r₂ from f₂ meet at planet m on the curve.]

#### Kepler's second law

[figure: Figure 2.21 — The shaded regions shown have equal areas and represent the same time interval. Ellipse with the Sun (M) at the left focus; three shaded wedges from the Sun: A₁ between orbit points A and B (short, wide wedge near the Sun), A₂ between C and D (bottom), A₃ between E and F (long, thin wedge at the far right end); planet m with velocity arrow v at the top left.]

Kepler's second law is sometimes referred to as the law of equal areas. It describes the speed at which any given planet will move while orbiting the Sun. Basically, it states that planets do not move with constant speed along their orbits. Instead, their speed varies so that the line joining the centers of the Sun and the planet sweeps out equal area in equal times. The point at which a planet is nearest the Sun is called perihelion. The point of greatest separation is aphelion. Hence Kepler's second Law, a planet is moving fastest when it is at perihelion and slowest at aphelion.

Kepler's second law states that each planet moves so that an imaginary line drawn from the sun to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times interval.

Consider Figure 2.21. The time it takes a planet to move from position A to B, sweeping out area A₁, is exactly the time taken to move from position C to D, sweeping area A₂ and to move from E to F, sweeping out area A₃. These areas are the same: A₁ = A₂ = A₃

Comparing the areas in the Figure 2.21 and the distance traveled along the ellipse in each case, we can see that in order for the areas to be equal, the planet must speed up as it gets closer to the Sun and slow down as it moves away.

Planets orbit the Sun. If we look more closely at the Solar System, we see almost unimaginable numbers of stars, galaxies, and other celestial objects orbiting one another and interacting through gravity. All these motions are governed by gravitational force.

### Discussion Question 2.11

What keeps the planets in orbit? Explain your answer.

**Answer:** The gravitational attraction of the Sun, acting as the centripetal force.
**Why:**
- Gravity pulls each planet toward the Sun's centre, i.e. toward the centre of its (nearly circular) path.
- The planet also has a large tangential velocity; the inward pull continually changes its direction (centripetal acceleration v²/r) without pulling it in.
- Balance: GM_s m/r² = mv²/r. Too slow → it would spiral in; with no gravity → straight line.

Newton proved that the force that causes, for example, an apple to fall toward the ground is the same force that causes the Moon to fall around, or orbit, Earth. This universal force also acts between the Earth and the Sun, or any other star and its satellites. Each attracts the other.

Newton defined this attraction mathematically. The force of attraction between two masses is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between their centers.

[figure: Figure 2.22 — Gravitational attraction is along a line joining the centers of mass of the two bodies. Orange sphere m₁ (lower left) and blue sphere m₂ (upper right) joined by a dashed line labelled r; an arrow near m₁ points toward m₂ and an arrow near m₂ points toward m₁ (both labelled F₂₁ in the book; one should be F₁₂).]

F_g = Gm₁m₂/r²    (2.23)

where G is a constant, called the universal gravitational constant, m₁ is the first mass, m₂ is the second mass, and r is the distance between the two masses. G is a universal constant, meaning that it is thought to be the same everywhere in the Universe. The value G in SI units is G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ Nm²/kg².

(p.60)

The gravitational force is always attractive, and it depends only on the masses involved and the distance between them. The force is directed along the line joining the two masses, as shown in Figure 2.22. The magnitude of the force on each body is the same but the direction is opposite, consistent with Newton's third law, action-reaction (F₁₂ = −F₂₁.)

#### Centripetal Force

It is possible to derive Kepler's third law from Newton's law of universal gravitation. A force that pulls an object towards the centre of a circle is called centripetal force as shown in Figure 2.23. The source for the centripetal force in the Solar System is the gravitational force of the Sun. Without the centripetal force from the Sun the planets would travel in a straight line. The velocity of the planets is high enough so that they continuously accelerate towards the Sun without ever leaving their orbits. It is for this reason that the planets do not fall into the sun from its strong gravitational force of attraction. Applying Newton's second law of motion to circular motion gives an expression for centripetal force.

[figure: Figure 2.23 — Centripetal force constantly pulls the object towards the center of the circle. A dotted circle with an object on it (lower left); a green arrow ("Cetripetal force" [sic]) from the object toward the centre; a purple arrow ("Tangential Velocity") tangent to the circle; a curved arrow at the centre shows rotation.]

F_c = mv²/r    (2.26)

where v is the tangential speed and r is the radius of the orbit and m is mass of the planet.

The gravitational attraction of the Sun provides the centripetal force needed to keep planets in orbit around the Sun. Earth's gravity keeps the Moon and all types of satellite in orbit around Earth. Because the gravitational force provides the centripetal acceleration of the planet, it follows that:

m_p v²/r = GM_s m_p / r²    (2.27)

M_p is mass of the planet, M_s is mass of the sun (M_s ≈ 1.989×10³⁰ kg) and v is the (p.62) speed of the plane about the sun.
[review: "plane" is a typo for "planet"; M_p and m_p are the same quantity.]

v² = GM_s/r    (2.28)

The orbital speed of the planet is

v = 2πr/T    (2.29)

where T is the period of the planet about the Sun.

Thus

(2πr)²/T² = GM_s/r    (2.30)

T²/r³ = 4π²/(GM_s) = 2.97×10⁻¹⁹

[added step: rearrange (2.30): 4π²r²/T² = GM_s/r → 4π²r³ = GM_s T² → T²/r³ = 4π²/(GM_s)]
[added step: 4π² = 39.48; GM_s = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 1.989×10³⁰ = 1.327×10²⁰; 39.48 / 1.327×10²⁰ = 2.97×10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³]

This equation is Kepler's third law: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the distance of the planet from the Sun. The proportionality constant K takes the value:

K = 4π²/(GM_s) ≈ 2.97×10⁻¹⁹
[review: unit missing — K ≈ 2.97×10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³.]

The above equation is therefore valid for any planet. If we were to consider the orbit of a satellite such as the Moon about the Earth, the constant would have a different value, with the Sun's mass replaced by the Earth's mass; that is, 4π²/(GM_E).

- θ = cos⁻¹(0.2) = 78.46° ≈ 78.5°.

4. During volcanic eruptions, pieces of solid rock can be blasted out of a volcano; these projectiles are called volcanic bombs (Figure 2.24).
(a) At what initial speed would the bomb have to be ejected, at 35° to the horizontal, from the hole at A in order to fall at the foot of the volcano at B? (Ignore the effects of air resistance on the bomb's travel.)
(b) What would be the time of flight?

[figure: Figure 2.24 — Volcanic bombs away. Volcano outline; vent A at the summit; a red arrow from A at 35° above the horizontal dashed line; a grey parabolic path lands at B at the foot of the volcano. Vertical drop from A to the level of B = 3.30 km; horizontal distance from below A to B = 9.40 km.]

**Answer:** (a) v₀ ≈ 256 m/s (2.56 × 10² m/s); (b) t ≈ 44.9 s
**Why:**
- Take A as origin, up positive: B is at x = 9400 m, y = −3300 m; θ = 35°, g = 9.8 m/s².
- Horizontal: 9400 = v₀ cos35° · t → v₀t = 9400 / 0.8192 = 11 475 m.
- Vertical: −3300 = v₀ sin35° · t − 4.9t² = (11 475 × 0.5736) − 4.9t² = 6582 − 4.9t².
- 4.9t² = 6582 + 3300 = 9882 → t² = 2016.7 → t = 44.9 s.
- v₀ = 11 475 / 44.9 = 255.5 m/s ≈ 256 m/s.
- Check: y = 255.5 × 0.5736 × 44.9 − 4.9 × 44.9² = 6580 − 9878 = −3298 m ≈ −3300 m ✓.

(p.67)

Firefighter at left aims the hose up at angle θ_i above the horizontal with initial velocity v⃗_i (red arrow); a blue water arc travels a horizontal distance d to a burning building on the right and strikes it at height h above the ground (hose height taken as ground level).]

5. A firefighter, a distance d from a burning building, directs a stream of water from a fire hose at angle θ_i above the horizontal, as shown in Figure 2.25. If the initial speed of the stream is v_i, at what height h does the water strike the building?

**Answer:** h = d tanθ_i − g d² / (2 v_i² cos²θ_i)
**Why:**
- Horizontal: d = v_i cosθ_i · t → t = d / (v_i cosθ_i).
- Vertical: h = v_i sinθ_i · t − ½ g t².
- Substitute t: h = v_i sinθ_i · d/(v_i cosθ_i) − ½ g · d²/(v_i² cos²θ_i).
- Simplify: h = d tanθ_i − g d² / (2 v_i² cos²θ_i).
- Numerical check (example values d = 20 m, v_i = 20 m/s, θ_i = 45°): h = 20 − 9.8 × 400 / (2 × 400 × 0.5) = 20 − 9.8 = 10.2 m.

6. A rotating fan completes 1200 revolutions every minute. Consider a point on the tip of a blade, at a radius of 0.15 m.
(a) Through what linear distance does the point move in one revolution?
(b) What is the linear speed of the point?

**Answer:** (a) 0.942 m; (b) 18.8 m/s
**Why:**
- (a) One revolution = circumference: s = 2πr = 2π × 0.15 = 0.942 m.
- (b) 1200 rev/min ÷ 60 = 20 rev/s. v = 20 rev/s × 0.942 m/rev = 18.8 m/s.
- Check: ω = 20 × 2π = 125.7 rad/s; v = ωr = 125.7 × 0.15 = 18.8 m/s ✓.

7. A car traveling at 80 km/h has wheels with a 40 cm radius. If the car can be brought to stop within 30 revolutions, what is the angular acceleration of the wheels?

**Answer:** α ≈ −8.19 rad/s² (magnitude 8.19 rad/s²)
**Why:**
- v = 80 km/h = 80 × 1000/3600 = 22.2 m/s; r = 0.40 m.
- ω_o = v/r = 22.22 / 0.40 = 55.6 rad/s; ω_f = 0.
- Δθ = 30 rev × 2π = 188.5 rad.
- ω_f² = ω_o² + 2αΔθ → α = −ω_o² / (2Δθ) = −(55.56)² / (2 × 188.5) = −3086 / 377.0 = −8.19 rad/s².

8. The angular velocity of the belt of a grindstone is 40 rad/s. To what angle does the belt rotate in one minute. Given your answer a) in rad b) in rev and c) in degree.

**Answer:** (a) 2400 rad; (b) ≈ 382 rev; (c) ≈ 1.38 × 10⁵ degrees (137 510°)
**Why:**
- Δθ = ωt = 40 rad/s × 60 s = 2400 rad.
- In rev: 2400 / 2π = 2400 / 6.283 = 382 rev.
- In degrees: 2400 × (180/π) = 2400 × 57.30 = 1.375 × 10⁵ ° (or 382 × 360 = 1.375 × 10⁵ ° ✓).

9. A cyclist rides a bicycle with a wheel radius of 0.50 m across campus. If the wheel completes 320 rotations between her apartment and the cafeteria, how far has she traveled?

**Answer:** ≈ 1.0 × 10³ m (1005 m)
**Why:**
- Distance per rotation = 2πr = 2π × 0.50 = 3.142 m.
- s = 320 × 3.142 = 1005 m ≈ 1.01 km.
- (Equivalently Δθ = 320 × 2π = 2011 rad; s = rΔθ = 0.5 × 2011 = 1005 m ✓.)

(p.68)

10. A spinning wheel is slowed down by a brake, giving it a constant angular acceleration of −5.60 rad/s². During a 4.20 s time interval, the wheel rotates through 62.4 rad. What is the angular speed of the wheel at the end of the 4.20 s interval?

**Answer:** ω ≈ 3.10 rad/s
**Why:**
- Δθ = ω_o t + ½αt² → 62.4 = ω_o (4.20) + ½(−5.60)(4.20)².
- ½ × 5.60 × 17.64 = 49.39 → 62.4 = 4.20 ω_o − 49.39 → ω_o = 111.79 / 4.20 = 26.62 rad/s.
- ω = ω_o + αt = 26.62 − 5.60 × 4.20 = 26.62 − 23.52 = 3.10 rad/s.
- Check another way: Δθ = ωt − ½αt² → ω = 62.4/4.20 + ½(−5.60)(4.20) = 14.86 − 11.76 = 3.10 rad/s ✓.

11. Titan, the largest moon of Saturn, has a mean orbital radius of 1.22×10⁹ m. The orbital period of Titan is 15.95 days. Hyperion, another moon of Saturn, orbits at a mean radius of 1.48×10⁹ m. Use Kepler's third law of planetary motion to predict the orbital period of Hyperion in days.

**Answer:** T_H ≈ 21.3 days
**Why:**
- Both orbit Saturn, so T²/r³ is the same: T_H = T_T (r_H/r_T)^(3/2).
- r_H/r_T = 1.48 / 1.22 = 1.2131.
- (1.2131)^(3/2) = 1.2131 × √1.2131 = 1.2131 × 1.1014 = 1.3361.
- T_H = 15.95 × 1.3361 = 21.3 days.

12. The planet Mercury travels around the Sun with a mean orbital radius of 5.8×10¹⁰ m. The mass of the Sun is 1.99×10³⁰ kg. How long does it take Mercury to orbit the Sun. Give your answer in Earth days.

**Answer:** T ≈ 7.6 × 10⁶ s ≈ 88 days
**Why:**
- T² = 4π² r³ / (GM_s).
- r³ = (5.8×10¹⁰)³ = 1.951×10³² m³.
- GM_s = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 1.99×10³⁰ = 1.327×10²⁰ m³/s².
- T² = 39.48 × 1.951×10³² / 1.327×10²⁰ = 5.80×10¹³ s² → T = 7.62×10⁶ s.
- In days: 7.62×10⁶ / 86 400 = 88.2 days ≈ 88 days (agrees with the 88 days stated on p.56).

13. Two identical isolated particles, each of mass 2.00 kg, are separated by a distance of 30.0 cm. What is the magnitude of the gravitational force exerted by one particle on the other?

**Answer:** F ≈ 2.96 × 10⁻⁹ N
**Why:**
- r = 30.0 cm = 0.300 m; m₁ = m₂ = 2.00 kg.
- F = Gm₁m₂/r² = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ × 2.00 × 2.00 / (0.300)².
- = 2.668×10⁻¹⁰ / 0.0900 = 2.96 × 10⁻⁹ N.

## Notes for lesson design

**Key ideas by subsection (pp. 46–68)**
- 2.2 (rest): s = rθ (θ in rad only); v = ωr; a_t = rα. All points of a rigid body share θ, ω, α; v and a_t grow with r. Rope on a pulley / wheel rolling: length of rope or road = rΔθ. Convert rev ↔ rad (1 rev = 2π rad) and rpm → rad/s (× 2π/60).
- 2.3: Torque τ = rF sinθ (N m), sign by sense of rotation (CCW +, CW −), direction by right-hand rule. Moment of inertia I = Σmr² (kg m², scalar) depends on mass and axis. τ_net = Iα (rotational Newton's 2nd law), then use rotational kinematics.
- 2.4: Geocentric (Ptolemy) → heliocentric (Copernicus) → Brahe's data → Kepler. 1st law: ellipse, Sun at one focus (r₁ + r₂ = const). 2nd law: equal areas in equal times → fastest at perihelion, slowest at aphelion. 3rd law: T²/R³ = K, same for all bodies orbiting the same central mass (K_Sun ≈ 2.97×10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³); T ∝ R^(3/2).
- 2.5: F = Gm₁m₂/r², G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N m²/kg², always attractive, equal and opposite (3rd law). g = GM_E/R_E² ≈ 9.8 m/s². Gravity supplies the centripetal force mv²/r → v² = GM/r, v = 2πr/T → T²/r³ = 4π²/(GM): derivation of Kepler's 3rd law; constant changes with the central mass.

**Common student mistakes**
- Using degrees or revolutions in s = rθ, v = ωr (must be rad); forgetting rpm → rad/s conversion (÷60, ×2π).
- Thinking all points on a wheel have the same linear speed.
- Using the force's distance without the sin θ factor in torque; using cos instead of sin; forgetting to assign signs CW/CCW when summing torques.
- Moment of inertia: forgetting to square r; counting the mass on the axis; mixing cm and m (as the book itself does in Example 2.15).
- Kepler's 3rd law: forgetting to square T (book Example 2.18 does this) or to cube R; using altitude instead of distance from the planet's centre (Exercise 2.4 Q2); applying the Sun's K to Earth satellites.
- Believing Earth pulls harder on a person than the person on Earth; doubling distance halves force (it quarters it).
- Thinking the Sun is at the centre of the ellipse; thinking a planet moves at constant speed.
- Projectile summary formulas use "+g" (g negative); students plug g = +9.8 and get wrong signs.
