### 2.1.1 Characteristics of Bigdata

**Bigdata** is characterized by 5V, namely Volume, Variety, Velocity, Veracity, and Value as stated by tectarget.com. It should also be noted that characteristics of Bigdata are subjected to additional features as scientific research and trend continues in Bigdata analytics. The most common characteristics of Bigdata are discussed by considering the 5 V's (Figure 2.3).

- **Variety:** Variety of Bigdata refers to the nature of data i.e whether it is structured, unstructured, and semi-structured data. In the past, data could only be collected from spreadsheets and database. Today, data comes in an array of forms such as emails, PDFs, photos, videos, audio, social media posts, and others. - **Value:** Value is the other major issue that we need to concentrate on. It is not just the amount of data that we store or process. It is the amount of valuable, reliable, and trustworthy data that needs to be stored, processed, and analyzed to find insights.
- **Veracity:** Veracity means the degree of reliability that the data has to offer. Bigdata focuses to find an alternatives way to filter the huge unstructured data to use in business developments.

### 2.2.3 Limitations of Cloud Computing

Cloud computing are not without their limitations. However, It's critical to weigh both the benefits and limitations of a new service before choosing if it is the best fit for us. Below are some of the limitations of cloud computing:

- **Server downtime** – No cloud provider can guarantee that you will not have service interruptions, which will prevent you from accessing your data or files. Because cloud computing is dependent on an Internet connection, if it goes down, so will your cloud services.
- **Security and privacy issues** – When it comes to handling sensitive data, companies are always concerned about how safe and private it is. There is still a risk of intruders, even though a cloud provider would have security processes in place to ensure that your data is maintained and protected properly. Intruders are constantly attempting to find new ways to break into cloud providers' systems in today's digital world. Therefore, cloud computing services are not completely safe.
- **Data ownership and transparency** – Companies may think that there is a lack of transparency concerning data ownership. Does data that is uploaded to a cloud computing system become the cloud provider's property? Another concern is lack of data openness. Companies may be concerned about how their data is managed and whether it is being compromised or not.
- **Inflexibility** – Some cloud computing providers may take a corporation off guard by requiring the usage of their applications and formats. For example, they can limit that a corporation can only upload data developed

(p.42)

  in a specific application program. Because the cloud provider has complete control over your cloud service infrastructure, they have ultimate authority over how your data is handled.
- **Lack of support** – Cloud-based services, in comparison to other hosting services and providers, provide minimal to no customer assistance. Customers who are unable to contact cloud providers by phone or email frequently turn to Internet forums for answers to their questions.
- **Cost** – Another disadvantage of this service is the unknown fees. Although cloud hosting eliminates the need for on-site infrastructure, it is often necessary to ensure that you are paying for all of the features that your organization needs to get the most out of the service. A cloud computing service can become costly in this regard.

### 2.2.4 Types of Cloud Computing

There are mainly four types of cloud computing available currently, namely:

1. public cloud
2. private cloud
3. hybrid cloud
4. community cloud

**Public cloud** – It is a standard cloud computing model where a service provider's resources are shared across different clients (See Figure 2.19). For example, if you rent 10GB of public cloud storage, you will get the requested capacity of a disk from a bigger chunk of a single storage device that is logically divided into small parts and distributed to multiple users isolated to make it private across different clients.

(p.43)

[figure: Figure 2.19. Public cloud — one "Public Cloud" at the top with two-way arrows down to three desktop computers labelled Customer 1, Customer 2, Customer 3 (one provider's resources shared by many clients).]

**Private cloud** – As the name indicates, the private cloud has all resources dedicated to your application. It can be either from a cloud service provider or your company's data center. It gives complete control over data security and privacy. The private cloud provides an additional layer of protection to process sensitive and confidential information (Figure2.20).

## 2.3. Fog Computing

### Brainstorming 2.3

- What are the difference and similarities between Cloud Computing and Fog Computing?

**Answer:** Similarities: both provide computing, storage and networking services over a network; fog is an extension of the cloud and works together with it; both support IoT and Bigdata. Differences: cloud computing is centralised in large, distant data centers, so data must travel through the network backbone, which can cause congestion and delay (latency). Fog computing uses "fog nodes" placed close to the data source (at the edge), so data can be processed locally and quickly, with lower latency, better mobility and better real-time response; only the data that needs it is pushed on to the cloud.
**Why:**
- Text: "Fog computing is an extension of the cloud"; fog nodes "are located closer to the data source"; fog decides "what data needs to be pushed to the cloud, and that can be analyzed locally, at the edge".
- Text: what distinguishes fog is "its closer proximity to small end-users, its wider consumer reach, and better mobility"; it "reduces latency".
- Figure 2.27: Cloud at the top, Fog nodes in the middle, Devices at the bottom.

*Fog computing* is an extension of the cloud. Cloud Computing relies heavily on the bandwidth made available, which depends on the capacity of the network service provider. With billions of users processing, sending, and receiving data in and out of the cloud, the system becomes increasingly congested.

Fog computing uses the concept of 'fog nodes' which are located closer to the data source and have a higher processing and storage capability. Fog provides the

(p.47)

missing link for what data needs to be pushed to the cloud, and that can be analyzed locally, at the edge. This makes fog nodes to process data quicker than sending the request to the cloud for centralized processing.

[review: "have a higher processing and storage capability" — higher than the end devices (sensors, phones), not higher than the cloud. Fog nodes have less capacity than cloud data centers; their advantage is being close to the data source.]

What distinguishes fog computing from cloud computing is its closer proximity to small end-users, its wider consumer reach, and better mobility. Rather than requiring devices to go through the network backbone infrastructure, fog computing permits devices to connect directly with their destination with ease and allows them to handle their connections and tasks in any way they see fit. As a result, fog computing improves the quality of service, reduces latency, and enhance user experience.

Fog computing smoothly supports the emerging Internet of Things (IoT) physical things (vehicles, home appliances, and even clothes) that are embedded with sensors to enable them to send/receive data. This advantage makes it easier to run a real-time, Big-Data operation with the ability to support billions of nodes in highly dynamic and diverse environments.

### Key Terms

- **Big Data** – refers to complex and large data sets that have to be processed and analyzed to uncover valuable information that can benefit businesses and organizations
- **Cloud Computing** – The distribution of various services, such as data storage, servers, databases, networking, and software, through the Internet
- **Fog Computing** – is a decentralized computing infrastructure in which data, compute, storage and applications are located somewhere between the data source and the cloud.
- **Infrastructure as a Service** – is a form of cloud computing that provides virtualized computing resources over the internet.
- **Internet** – a global computer network providing a variety of information and communication facilities, consisting of interconnected networks using standardized communication protocols.
- **Internet of Things** – describes physical objects with sensors, processing ability, software, and other technologies that connect and exchange data with other devices and systems over the Internet or other communications networks.
- **Platform as a Service** – is a type of cloud computing that offers users a whole cloud platform, including hardware, software, and infrastructure, for creating, administering, and using applications.
- **Software as a Service** – is a way of delivering applications over the Internet—as a service where users can avoid complicated software and device maintenance by just accessing software via the Internet rather than installing and maintaining it.

(p.57)

### Part III: Give short answers to the following questions

1. What is the aim of IoT? What makes it different from networking?

**Answer:** The aim of IoT is to extend Internet connectivity beyond computers and smartphones to everyday physical objects (home appliances, vehicles, wearables, sensors, even animals and people with tracking or health devices), so they can collect and exchange data, be controlled remotely and become "smart", reducing human effort. Ordinary networking connects computers and devices mainly so that *people* can share resources and communicate. IoT connects *things* that have sensors, software and processing ability; they gather data from the physical world by themselves, send it for processing (often in the cloud), and can act automatically using AI and predefined rules, without human interaction.
**Why:**
- Text: "IoT aims at extending Internet connectivity beyond computers and smartphones to other devices people use at home, or for business"; devices "can be used without involving human interaction"; "IoT makes things virtually smart through Artificial Intelligence algorithms, data collection, and networks".
- Key term: IoT "describes physical objects with sensors, processing ability, software… that connect and exchange data".
- IoT uses networking (connectivity is only one of its four components) but adds sensors, data processing and a user interface/automatic action.

2. Mention two practical examples of IoT applications.

**Answer:** (Any two.) (1) A smart home: a smoke detector that sounds an alarm and sends a phone notification, or smart bulbs and door locks controlled from a smartphone. (2) Remote patient monitoring: a diabetes monitor or smartwatch that sends glucose or heart-rate readings to a doctor. Others: smart city traffic management (traffic lights adjusting to traffic), automatic watering using soil-moisture sensors, animal tracking devices, real-time goods tracking in the supply chain, connected cars with automatic parking and billing.
**Why:**
- All are listed in 2.4.3 (Smart Home, Smart City, Supply chain, Connect Health, Connected cars, Smart outlets), Table 2.1 and Figure 2.29.
- Each one has sensors/devices, connectivity, data processing and a user interface, so it is a real IoT system.

3. Discuss applications of Bigdata in the education sector with examples.

**Answer:**
- **Personalised learning:** analysing each student's grades, participation, achievement and learning style lets teachers make a balanced learning plan and tailor programs to the student's level and attention span (e.g. an e-learning platform recommends extra practice in topics a student fails).
- **Blended learning:** combining offline and online learning based on the data.
- **Admission/selection:** universities analyse applications to decide who fits the institution, which shortens the selection process.
- **Better programmes and decisions:** data on results and drop-outs improves the student experience and academic programming, supports evidence-based decisions, and helps the school respond strategically to global trends (e.g. a ministry using national exam data to see which regions need more teachers).
**Why:**
- All points are from 2.1.3 "Education" and Figure 2.6 (analysing students' participation, achievement and learning style → personalized education).

4. Mention common advantages of fog computing services.

**Answer:** Fog computing (1) processes data close to the source in fog nodes, so responses are faster (reduced latency); (2) reduces network congestion and bandwidth use because only the needed data is pushed to the cloud; (3) improves quality of service and user experience; (4) is closer to end-users, with wider consumer reach and better mobility; (5) supports real-time IoT and Big-Data operations with billions of nodes (e.g. video surveillance).
**Why:**
- Text, 2.3: fog nodes "process data quicker than sending the request to the cloud for centralized processing"; fog "improves the quality of service, reduces latency, and enhance user experience"; its distinguishing features are "closer proximity to small end-users, its wider consumer reach, and better mobility"; it "smoothly supports the emerging Internet of Things".

(p.59)

## Notes for lesson design

### Key ideas by subsection
- **Unit overview:** an emerging technology is new, or an evolution of an existing one. It is in development or will arrive within 5–10 years and has significant social/economic effects. Grade 11 covered data science, AI, VR and AR. This unit covers Bigdata, cloud, fog and IoT.
- **2.1 Introduction to Bigdata:** data sets too massive and complex for typical software or database tools. Sources are point-of-sale/transactions, IoT endpoint devices, social media, smartphone location data and surveys. Steps: identify sources → build a pipeline → store for organisational use (Fig 2.2). Example: Google uses search history, location and trends.
- **2.1.1 Characteristics (5 V's):** **Volume** (huge amount), **Variety** (structured: spreadsheet, relational DB; unstructured: audio, image, text, video; semi-structured: JSON, XML, HTML), **Velocity** (speed of creation in real time), **Veracity** (reliability; inconsistency and uncertainty), **Value** (useful, trustworthy data that gives insights).
- **2.1.2 Benefits:** customer acquisition and retention, targeted ads, product development, price optimization, risk management, improved decision-making. Ethiopian users: Ethiopian Airlines, Ethio Telecom, the AI Center, banks.
- **2.1.3 Applications:** healthcare, education, banking (real-time fraud detection), agriculture (precision farming), manufacturing, retail, transportation. Each figure follows the same pattern: data sources → Bigdata analytics → better service or outcome.
- **2.1.4 Challenges:** managing growth (storage), lack of data professionals, securing data, integrating data from many sources.
- **2.2.1 Cloud computing:** storing and accessing data and computing services (servers, storage, networking, databases) over the Internet, on demand. Examples: Gmail, Google Maps, Netflix, PayPal, Spotify.
- **2.2.2 Benefits:** accessibility and mobility (incl. backup, inclusive education tools), flexibility (shared real-time collaboration), updating and scalability, optimized security, controlled costs (pay as you go). Worked example: hosting a website on your own server (buy server → public IP → install OS + web services → publish) vs cloud hosting (upload → live in minutes).
- **2.2.3 Limitations:** server downtime/Internet dependence, security and privacy, data ownership and transparency, inflexibility (vendor formats), lack of support, cost (unknown fees).
- **2.2.4 Types (deployment):** public (shared across clients, e.g. 10 GB slice of one big disk), private (dedicated, full control), hybrid (public + private), community (organisations in the same field share; privately managed).
- **2.2.5 Services:** SaaS (finished apps for end users; subscription), PaaS (platform for developers, like the teacher giving paint, brushes and paper), IaaS (virtual servers, storage and network for infrastructure architects). Fig 2.23: the provider manages more from IaaS → PaaS → SaaS.
- **2.3 Fog computing:** an extension of the cloud. Fog nodes near the data source process data locally ("at the edge") and push only what is needed to the cloud. This means less congestion, lower latency, better mobility and support for IoT. Example: video surveillance. Three layers: Cloud → Fog → Devices (Fig 2.27).
- **2.4 IoT:** a network of physical objects ("things") with software, electronics, network and sensors that collect and exchange data. It extends Internet connectivity beyond PCs and phones; things become "smart" through data, AI and networks. Examples: animal trackers, diabetes monitors, AC sensors, smart wearables.
- **2.4.1 Advantages / limitations:** the advantages are efficiency, technical optimization (one phone controls everything), convenience, improved customer experience, conservation (smart cities) and personalization. The limitations are security and privacy, connectivity and power dependence, complexity and integration, and higher cost.
- **2.4.2 How IoT works:** four components in order: **Sensors/devices → Connectivity (cellular, satellite, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Ethernet) → Data processing (in the cloud) → User interface (alerts, app; manual action or automatic predefined rules)**.
- **2.4.3 Applications:** smart home, smart city, supply chain, connected health, connected cars, smart outlets. Table 2.1 maps area → use.

### Common student mistakes
- Thinking Bigdata is just "a lot of data". It is also defined by variety, velocity, veracity and value, and by needing non-conventional tools (Review I.1).
- Mixing up the V's: **Velocity** = speed, **Volume** = amount, **Veracity** = reliability/truth, **Value** = usefulness. Veracity and Value are the most often swapped.
- Classifying JSON/XML/HTML as structured or unstructured. The book says semi-structured. Text, images, audio and video are unstructured.
- Confusing cloud **types** (public, private, hybrid, community = who uses it) with cloud **services** (SaaS, PaaS, IaaS = what is supplied). Activity 2.3 Q1 itself mixes them up ("three types").
- Putting Dropbox/Office 365 under PaaS, or Azure/Heroku under SaaS. Rule of thumb: end users use it → SaaS; developers build on it → PaaS; you rent virtual machines → IaaS.
- Thinking fog computing replaces the cloud, or that fog nodes are more powerful than the cloud. Fog sits between devices and the cloud and works with it.
- Writing "Word Processing" for the IoT component "Data Processing" (Review I.4), or forgetting the User Interface component.
- Thinking every Internet service is IoT. Music streaming (the book's own example) is really cloud. IoT needs physical things with sensors or actuators.
- Believing cloud data is 100 % safe because of "optimized security". The limitations section says cloud services "are not completely safe".
- Thinking "public cloud" means anyone can see your data. Resources are shared but each client's part is logically isolated.

### Ideas for interactive practice and pictures
- **Sorting – 5 V's:** drag statements into Volume / Variety / Velocity / Veracity / Value bins (e.g. "500 million tweets per day" → Volume; "data arrives every millisecond from sensors" → Velocity; "some customer ages are recorded as 300" → Veracity). Randomise from a bank of about 30 statements.
- **Sorting – data variety:** drag file types (CSV spreadsheet, SQL table, JPG, MP3, MP4, email body, JSON, XML, HTML) into structured / semi-structured / unstructured.
- **Matching – SaaS/PaaS/IaaS:** product cards (Google Workspace, Dropbox, Office 365, Slack, WebEx, Heroku, Google App Engine, OpenShift, AWS EC2, Google Compute Engine) → service model. Add an interactive "stack" picture (Fig 2.23): layers Server, Storage, Network, OS & Middleware, Packaged Software. Students tap which layers the provider manages for each model.
- **Matching – deployment type ↔ scenario:** "a bank keeps customer records in-house but runs its public website on AWS" → hybrid; "several hospitals share one patient-record system" → community; "you rent 10 GB from Google Drive" → public; "Ethio Telecom's own data center for its internal systems" → private.
- **Ordering steps:** website hosting Option 1 (buy server → place in data room → get public IP → install OS → install web services → upload site) vs Option 2 (sign up → upload → live). A timer or cost slider could compare them, e.g. a server costing birr X up front vs Y birr/month: after how many months does renting cost the same? Numbers can be generated.
- **Ordering steps – IoT pipeline:** put the four components in order for random scenarios (smoke alarm, smart irrigation, fridge temperature alert), then pick the right sensor (smoke, soil-moisture, temperature, motion, infrared) and connectivity (Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular, satellite, Ethernet) for each.
- **Rule builder (IF–THEN):** students write predefined IoT rules such as "IF soil moisture < 30 % THEN pump ON" or "IF temperature > 8 °C THEN SMS". A simulator generates random sensor readings and students predict the action.
- **Interactive picture – Cloud vs Fog latency:** an animated three-layer diagram (devices → fog nodes → cloud). Students drag a task (video analysis, monthly report, collision warning) to where it should be processed. A latency meter shows e.g. 5 ms at fog vs 150 ms at cloud (generated values) and a bandwidth meter shows the congestion.
- **Interactive picture – smart home (Fig 2.31):** tap each device to see its sensor, what data it sends and what action it triggers. Toggle "power cut" or "Internet down" to show the dependence limitation.
- **Matching – IoT area ↔ use** (Table 2.1 / Review II(b)), with distractors like drones and driverless cars.
- **Benefit vs limitation sort:** cards for cloud computing and for IoT (e.g. "pay as you go", "server downtime", "vendor formats", "personalization", "incompatible devices") sorted into advantage/limitation columns.
- **Bigdata flow builder:** for a chosen sector (bank, farm, hospital, shop, transport), students connect data-source icons → analytics → outcome, copying the pattern of Figs 2.5–2.11 (e.g. bank: fraud historical data → fraud analytics → inspect transactions → "Fraud detected").
