## UNIT 1: APPLICATION OF BIOLOGY

The applications of biology in our daily to day life are numerous. Biology is the science in charge of studying all living beings. It helps us to understand every living organism, from the smallest bacteria to the blue whales. Biologists often focus on a different subset of living organisms, such as birds, plants, or bacteria, to determine their character.

Biological science is very useful science to determine where some diseases and pests come from, such as infections, animal pathologies, and damage to plants. Biology covers the study of the functions of living organisms, the evolution of species, the factors that produce diseases, as well as the discovery of new drugs.

This discipline allows human beings to explore topics such as genetic engineering, research applications with mother cells, and global warming. It also helps to understand nature and how humans, animals, and plants interact in life.

Biology offers a vision of how living things evolve. Understanding the rates of extinction and how a species depends on and affects the habitat where it lives improves the effectiveness of conservation efforts.

A practical application of biology with which most people are familiar is hand washing, domestication, and traditional fermentation. Regular soap washing removes acquired microbes from the skin and helps control the spread of infectious diseases.

In addition, another application of biology is the set of instructions given to take all the pills of antibiotics in a recipe. Then, to broaden your understanding of this further you will look at the more everyday examples of the application of this science in the next topics.

### Self-questioning

- Do you agree with the conservations of natural resources? Why?

**Answer:** Yes. Conservation is needed so that natural resources do not disappear and remain available for people now and in the future.
**Why:**
- Many resources (metals, oil, natural gas, coal) are non-renewable: once used up they cannot easily be replaced.
- Even renewable resources (forests, soil, water, wildlife) can be lost if they are used faster than they are replaced.
- Losing species and habitats reduces biodiversity, and species can become extinct.
- Wise use protects the environment, the economy and livelihoods (farming, tourism) that depend on these resources.

- Explain the concept that human should practice sustainable management of natural resources?

**Answer:** Sustainable management means using natural resources to meet our present needs without destroying them, so that future generations can also meet their needs.
**Why:**
- Use renewable resources no faster than they can regrow or be replaced (e.g., cut trees only as fast as new ones are planted and grow).
- Reduce, reuse and recycle to use less of non-renewable resources.
- Prefer renewable energy (sun, wind, water, geothermal, biomass) over fossil fuels to reduce pollution and climate change.
- Protect soil, water and biodiversity through practices such as terracing, afforestation and protected areas.

- How the knowledge and application of conservation is critical to the survival of our people?

**Answer:** The people of Ethiopia depend directly on soil, water, forests, crops and livestock for food, energy, water and income, so conserving them is necessary for survival.
**Why:**
- Most people depend on agriculture; soil erosion and deforestation reduce crop yields and cause food insecurity.
- Clean water from lakes, rivers and springs is needed for drinking, irrigation and hydro-electric power.
- Forests and biodiversity provide fuel, medicine, timber and stable climate and rainfall.
- Knowing how to conserve (e.g., terracing, tree planting, controlled grazing) helps prevent drought, famine and loss of livelihoods.

Natural resources can be classified as renewable or non-renewable. Renewable resources are mainly living things and their products. The main sources of renewable resources are sun, wind, water, the earth's heat (geothermal) and biomass (which relates to living things). When managed carefully, they can be used, reused and replaced. Examples of renewable resources are crop plants, trees, cattle, and chickens. Non-renewable resources are natural substances that are not replaced at the same speed as that are used up. They are finite resources. Examples of non-renewable resources include metals such as gold and iron and fossil fuels such as oil, natural gas, and coal.

### Key word

**Food Security:** The state in which all people have physical, social and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food.

Food security, as defined by the United Nations' Committee on World Food Security, is a state in which when all the people have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food that meets their food preferences and dietary needs for an active and healthy life at all times. However, food insecurity is often rooted in poverty and has long-term impacts on the potential of families, communities, and countries to develop and prosper. Prolonged undernourishment stunts growth (Figures 1.4 & 1.5), slows cognitive development and increases susceptibility to illness.

- Improved/high-yield and drought- or pest-resistant crop varieties produce more food on the same land.

- Traditional biotechnology (fermentation, e.g., injera, bread, teji, tella) and food preservation reduce food waste and improve nutrition; knowledge of nutrition helps people eat a balanced diet.

### Activity 1.3a. Individual work.

Write a short set of notes in order to answer the following question(s). Use the internet, as well as digital and print educational resources.

- What factor influence food availability?

**Answer:** Food availability depends on how much food is produced, stored, imported and distributed in an area. Main factors: climate and weather (rainfall, drought, floods), soil fertility and land, water for irrigation, pests and crop/livestock diseases, farming methods and technology (seeds, fertilizer, irrigation), population size, post-harvest losses, transport and markets, conflict, and government policy/imports.
**Why:**
- Natural factors: drought or too much rain, poor or eroded soil, and pests lower crop and livestock yields.
- Technology factors: improved seeds, fertilizers, irrigation and good storage increase supply and reduce losses.
- Human/social factors: rapid population growth raises demand; conflict, poor roads and markets stop food reaching people.
- Economic/policy factors: prices, imports, food aid and agricultural policy affect how much food is in the country.

- What is the difference between food security, nutritional security, and food insecurity?

**Answer:**
- **Food security:** all people at all times have physical, social and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food for an active, healthy life.
- **Nutrition (nutritional) security:** goes further — people have nutritious food AND safe water, adequate sanitation, access to health care, and knowledge of good child care, food storage and preparation and hygiene, so the body can actually use the food well.
- **Food insecurity:** the opposite of food security — people do not have reliable access to enough safe, nutritious food (often rooted in poverty).
**Why:**
- Food security is about access to enough food; nutrition security adds the conditions (water, sanitation, health, knowledge) needed for good nutrition.
- A family can be food secure (enough food) but not nutrition secure (e.g., unsafe water causes illness, or the diet lacks micronutrients).
- Food insecurity means one or more of availability, access or utilization fails.

- What are the effects of food and nutritional insecurity?

**Answer:** Malnutrition and its consequences: stunting, wasting, micronutrient deficiencies (and sometimes over-nutrition from poor-quality diets), slowed cognitive development, more illness, and lower productivity and economic development.
**Why:**
- Prolonged undernourishment stunts growth and slows cognitive development (book, p.11), so children learn less at school.
- It weakens the body and increases susceptibility to illness and death, especially in young children.
- Adults become less able to work, so income falls and poverty continues (a cycle).
- At community and country level it reduces the potential to develop and prosper, and can cause migration and dependence on food aid.

## 1.4. Applications in biotechnology

**Biotechnology** is the application of technologies that involve the use of living organisms, or products from living organisms, for the development of products that benefit humans. Genetically modified organisms (GMOs) have received genetic material via recombinant DNA technology. If an organism has received genetic material from a different species, it is called a transgenic organism. A gene from one species that is introduced into another species is called a transgene.

The organisms used may be genetically modifed to make them more suitable. Crops can be modifed to increase yields and to obtain novel products. Biotechnology can be used in the prevention and mitigation of contamination from industrial, agricultural and municipal wastes. Biotechnology can also be used in the diagnosis and treatment of disease.

#### (c). Production of beer, wine, and spirits

Beer and wine are made by fermenting sugary juices; spirits, such as whiskey, gin, and rum, are made by fermenting

(p.17)

juices and distilling the fermented product. Distillation separates alcohol and other volatile substances from solid and nonvolatile substances. Strains of *Saccharomyces* are the fermenters for all alcoholic beverages. Many different strains have been developed, each having distinctive characteristics. Both the organisms and how they are used are carefully guarded brewers' secrets.

To make beer, cereal grains (usually barley) are malted (partially germinated) to increase the concentration of starch-digesting enzymes that provide the sugar for fermentation. Malted grain is crushed and mixed with hot water (about 65 °C), producing mash. After a few hours, a liquid extract called wort is separated from the mix. Hops (flower cones from the hop plant) are added to the wort for flavoring, and the mixture is boiled to stop enzyme action and precipitate proteins. A strain of *Saccharomyces* is added. Fermentation produces ethyl alcohol, carbon dioxide, and other substances, including amyl and isoamyl alcohols and acetic and butyric acids, which add to the flavor of the beer. After fermentation, the yeast is removed, and the beer is filtered, pasteurized, and bottled.

Beer-making steps in order: malting (barley partially germinated → more starch-digesting enzymes) → mashing (crushed malt + hot water ≈ 65 °C → mash) → wort separated → hops added, boiled (stops enzymes, precipitates proteins) → *Saccharomyces* added → fermentation (ethyl alcohol + CO₂ + flavour substances) → yeast removed → filtered, pasteurized, bottled.

Most wine is made from juice extracted from grapes, although it can be made from any fruit and even from nuts or dandelion blossoms. Juice is treated with sulfur dioxide to kill any wild yeasts that may already be present. Sugar and a strain of *Saccharomyces* are then added, and fermentation proceeds. Although ethyl alcohol is the main product of fermentation, other products similar to those in beer add to the flavor of the wine. In both beer and wine, the particular characteristics of the juice and the yeast strain determine the flavor of the final product. When fermentation is completed, liquid wine is siphoned to separate it from yeast sediment and, if necessary, cleared with agents such as charcoal to remove suspended particles. Finally, it is bottled and aged in a cool place.

Main fermentation reaction (yeast, added for reference, not printed on this page):

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂

Spirits are made from the fermentation of a variety of foods, including malted barley (Scotch whiskey), rye (rye whiskey, gin), corn (bourbon), wine or fruit juice (brandy), potatoes (vodka), and molasses (rum). After fermentation, distilling separates alcohol and other volatile substances that impart flavor from the solid and nonvolatile substances. Because of distillation, the alcohol content of spirits ranges from 40 to 50% much higher than the typical 12% for wine and 6% for beer.

1. Discuss in your groups the ingredients, recipe, and biotechnology of the following traditional fermentation products.

i) Making "*Injera*"

**Answer:** Injera is a sour, spongy flatbread made by fermenting teff (or mixed-grain) batter with a starter (ersho) and baking it on a clay griddle (mitad).

**Why:**
- Ingredients: teff flour (sometimes mixed with barley, maize or sorghum flour), water, and *ersho* — liquid saved from a previous fermentation used as starter.
- Recipe: mix flour, water and ersho into a batter → ferment about 2–3 days → pour off the yellowish liquid on top (the next ersho) → often a small part of the batter is boiled (*absit*) and stirred back in → leave a short time to rise → pour in a circle on the hot mitad, cover and bake until the "eyes" (holes) form.
- Biotechnology: lactic acid bacteria and yeasts ferment the flour sugars; acids (mainly lactic, some acetic) give the sour taste and help preserve it; CO₂ gas makes the bubbles/eyes and spongy texture.

ii) Making "*Areki*"

**Answer:** Areki is a traditional distilled alcoholic drink: a fermented mash is distilled to get a strong spirit.

**Why:**
- Ingredients: water, ground *gesho* (leaves/stems of the gesho plant), malted grain (*bikil*), and cereal flour (e.g. maize, sorghum, barley, teff), sometimes prepared as *kita* (thin baked bread).
- Recipe: soak gesho in water → add malt and flour/kita → ferment for several days → heat the fermented mash in a clay pot connected to a collecting pipe/container → collect the condensed liquid (areki).
- Biotechnology: malt enzymes turn starch into sugar; yeast (*Saccharomyces*) ferments sugar to ethyl alcohol and CO₂; distillation separates the volatile alcohol from solids and water, so areki is much stronger than tella (the same idea as spirits in the book).

iii) Making "*Tej*"

**Answer:** Tej is Ethiopian honey wine made by fermenting honey diluted in water, flavoured with gesho.

**Why:**
- Ingredients: honey, water, gesho (leaves/stems, boiled or soaked).
- Recipe: dilute honey with water in a clean container → add gesho → cover and ferment for several days to a few weeks (stirring, removing wax/foam) → strain and serve; longer fermentation gives a drier, stronger tej.
- Biotechnology: yeasts (mainly *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) ferment the honey sugars to ethyl alcohol and CO₂; lactic acid bacteria add acids; gesho gives bitterness and flavour and helps control fermentation.

iv) Making "*Tella*"

**Answer:** Tella is a traditional home-brewed beer made from cereals, malt and gesho.

**Why:**
- Ingredients: water, gesho, malt (*bikil*, germinated barley or wheat), cereal flour (barley, maize, sorghum, teff) and *kita* (roasted/baked thin bread from the flour).
- Recipe: soak ground gesho in water for a few days → add malt and broken kita/flour → ferment → add more roasted flour (*enkuro*) and water → ferment further for several days → filter and serve.
- Biotechnology: as in beer making, malt enzymes break starch to sugars; *Saccharomyces* yeast ferments them to ethyl alcohol and CO₂; lactic acid bacteria give a slightly sour taste; gesho gives bitterness (like hops). Tella is not distilled, so its alcohol content is low.

#### (d). Bread making

Microorganisms accomplish three functions in bread making:
1. leavening the flour-based dough,
2. imparting flavor and odor, and
3. conditioning the dough to make it workable.

### Cloning

Cloning is a method of producing identical copies of genes, cells, or organisms. The products of cloning are called a **clone**. ### X. Biological warfare

**Biological warfare** (BW) also known as germ warfare is the use of biological toxins or infectious agents such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi with the intent to kill or incapacitate humans, animals or plants as an act of war.

Biological weapons include any microorganism (such as bacteria, viruses, or fungi) or toxin (poisonous compounds produced by microorganisms) found in nature that can be used to kill or injure people. The act of bioterrorism can range from a simple hoax to the actual use of these biological weapons, also referred to as agents. **Answer the following questions**

**1. Define genetic engineering.**

**Answer:** Genetic engineering is the process of transferring DNA (genes) from one organism into another, often unrelated, organism so that the receiving organism is genetically modified; the result is a transgenic organism.
**Why:**
- Book (1.5, p.19): "the process of transferring DNA from one organism into another that results in a genetic modification; the production of a transgenic organism."
- Summary (p.38): "the transfer of genes from one organism to an unrelated species."

**2. Differentiate between gene cloning and gene therapy.**

**Answer:** Gene cloning is making many identical copies of a particular gene (for example by putting it into a plasmid in bacteria that multiply). Gene therapy is putting a normal, working gene into the cells of a patient to treat a disease caused by a faulty gene.
**Why:**
- Cloning = "producing identical copies of genes, cells, or organisms" (p.36); its aim is to copy/multiply a gene (e.g. to make its protein, like insulin).
- Gene therapy (named on p.19 and p.38 as a use of genetic engineering) aims to treat a person.
- So: cloning → many copies of a gene, done in the lab/microbes; therapy → a corrected gene given to a patient's body cells.
- [review: the unit names gene therapy but never defines it; the definition here is the standard school-level one.]

**3. List at least three different properties that have been acquired by crop plants via genetic engineering.**

**Answer:** Any three of: disease resistance; insect (pest) resistance, e.g. Bt-cotton and Bt-maize; herbicide (glyphosate) resistance; drought tolerance; better nutritional value; longer shelf life.
**Why:**
- Book p.35: GMOs have "disease resistance, herbicide and pesticide resistance, better nutritional value, and better shelflife"; "tolerant of herbicides and drought".
- Book pp.34–36: insect-resistant maize (corn borer), cotton (boll weevil) and Bt-cotton.

**4. What is single-cell protein?**

**Answer:** Single-cell protein (SCP) is protein-rich food or animal feed made by mass-culturing microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, algae) on cheap waste materials.
**Why:**
- Book p.15: SCP "is produced from waste materials such as molasses from sugar refining, petroleum by-products, and agricultural wastes."
- Examples from the book: Pruteen (animal feed from the bacterium *Methylophilus methylotrophus*), mycoprotein (from the fungus *Fusarium venenatum*), and *Spirulina* (a cyanobacterium).
- It helps increase world food productivity.

**5. What are the different communities of anaerobic microbes involved in biogas production?**

**Answer:** Three communities: (1) bacteria that break down raw organic waste into organic acids, alcohol, hydrogen and carbon dioxide; (2) bacteria that turn the organic acids and alcohol into acetate, CO₂ and hydrogen; (3) methanogens (Archaea) that make methane.
**Why:**
- Book p.29: "Three different communities of anaerobic microbes are required." The first two are Eubacteria; the last group are Archaea called methanogens.
- Methanogens make methane by:
  CO₂ + 4H₂ → CH₄ + 2H₂O
  CH₃COOH → CH₄ + CO₂
- Check the first equation balances: C 1 = 1; O 2 = 2; H 8 = 4 + 4. Second: C 2 = 1 + 1; H 4 = 4; O 2 = 2.

**6. Explain some medical applications of DNA technology?**

**Answer:** DNA technology is used to make medicines such as human insulin and growth hormone in transgenic bacteria; to make vaccines and antibiotics; for gene therapy; to make human proteins in the milk of transgenic sheep and goats; and for diagnosis of disease (e.g. PCR and biosensors).
**Why:**
- p.20 (1.6): insulin, growth hormone, tissue plasminogen activator are made by transgenic bacteria carrying the human gene.
- p.35: human proteins expressed in milk of transgenic sheep and goats and in chicken eggs.
- p.19 and p.38: gene therapy and pharmaceuticals; p.15: biotechnology used in "diagnosis and treatment of disease".

**7. Describe how bioremediation is helpful to humans?**

**Answer:** Bioremediation uses living organisms (mainly microbes) or their products to remove pollutants, so it cleans soil and water cheaply and makes the environment safer for people.
**Why:**
- Book p.27–28: microbes break down toxic pollutants into harmless compounds (biodegradation).
- Example: mercury-resistant bacteria change toxic metallic mercury (which damages the nervous system) into a non-toxic compound.
- Toxic heavy metals can be made less toxic by oxidation or reduction carried out by microorganisms.
- Wastewater treatment by microbes reduces waterborne diseases such as cholera (p.26).

**8. Explain how the practical application of DNA-based biotechnology affect the human lives in many ways.**

**Answer:** It affects (1) health — medicines like insulin, vaccines, gene therapy, diagnosis; (2) food — GM crops with higher yield, pest resistance and better nutrition, improved livestock; (3) environment — bioremediation, less pesticide use; (4) forensics — DNA fingerprinting to identify criminals and settle paternity cases.
**Why:**
- Each point comes from sections of the unit: 1.6 health (p.20), transgenic plants/animals (pp.34–35), bioremediation (p.27), forensic science and PCR (p.23–24).
- It can also raise concerns (gene spread, safety of GMOs), which is why GMOs are monitored by government agencies (p.35).

**9. Why has the production of human insulin by recombinant DNA methods had significant medical advantages for diabetics?**

**Answer:** Recombinant human insulin is identical to the insulin the body makes, so it causes fewer allergic reactions than insulin taken from cows or pigs; it can be made cheaply in very large amounts by bacteria, so supply is reliable; and it has no risk of carrying animal diseases.
**Why:**
- The human insulin gene is put into bacteria, which are fermented to produce insulin (book p.20).
- Before this, insulin came from the pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs, which is slightly different from human insulin and in limited supply. [added: background not in this unit.]
- Bacteria multiply fast → large, steady, cheaper supply.

**10. How are the two fusion proteins used to make human insulin?**

**Answer:** Insulin has two chains, A and B. The gene for each chain is put separately into bacteria (*E. coli*), joined to a bacterial gene, so each bacterium makes a "fusion protein" (bacterial protein + insulin chain). The two fusion proteins are collected, the insulin chains are cut off from the bacterial part, and then the A and B chains are joined together (by disulfide bonds) to form active human insulin.
**Why:**
- Step 1: make/obtain the DNA for chain A and chain B.
- Step 2: insert each into a plasmid next to a bacterial gene (β-galactosidase) and put into separate *E. coli* cultures.
- Step 3: bacteria produce two fusion proteins; extract and purify them.
- Step 4: chemically cut off the A and B chains; mix them so they join → insulin.
- [review: the unit does not describe fusion proteins at all (insulin is only mentioned on p.20); this answer is the standard textbook method, kept at school level.]

**11. Write the benefits of biofuels.**

**Answer:** Biofuels (e.g. ethanol, biogas) are renewable; they reduce dependence on fossil fuels; they can be made from crop residues and wastes; and they produce less pollution/net CO₂ than fossil fuels.
**Why:**
- Book p.28: the need to become independent of fossil fuels is driven by political and environmental concerns; biofuel (chiefly ethanol) is made by fermentation of plant material; using crop residues can boost yields.
- Book p.29: biogas is a cooking/lighting fuel, cheaper than LPG, and leaves slurry that is a soil conditioner (manure).

**12. Explain how can plant biotechnology be used for enhancing the nutritive qualities of foods.**

**Answer:** Genes can be inserted into crop plants so they make more of a nutrient (e.g. vitamins, better proteins, minerals), giving GM crops with "better nutritional value" and longer shelf life; microbes can also be used to make vitamins and single-cell protein.
**Why:**
- Book p.35: GMOs have "better nutritional value" and "improved nutritional quality".

**13. Explain the strategies adopted for creating transgenic plants with the following traits: (a) Disease resistance (b) Herbicide tolerance (c) Insect resistance.**

**Answer:**
(a) Disease resistance — a gene that protects against a disease (e.g. a gene from a virus or a resistance gene from another plant) is put into the crop, usually with the Ti plasmid of *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*.
(b) Herbicide tolerance — a gene for an enzyme that breaks down glyphosate (or a glyphosate-resistance gene) is inserted into the Ti plasmid with an antibiotic-resistance gene; plant cells are infected, grown on antibiotic, and only transformed cells survive to form callus → resistant plants.
(c) Insect resistance — the toxin gene from *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) is introduced with a bacterial vector; the plant makes Bt toxin that kills insect larvae (Bt-cotton, Bt-maize).
**Why:**
- (b) is described step by step on pp.32–33 (Figure 1.15) and p.36 (Herbicide resistance).
- (c) is on p.34 and p.36 (Bt toxin gene, bacterial vector).
- (a) the book only says transgenic crops "resist disease" (p.35); the method uses the same Ti-plasmid vector. [review: the book gives no specific method for disease resistance.]

**14. What are the social and ethical problems associated with plant genetic engineering? How can these be addressed?**

**Answer:**
Problems: worry about safety of GM food for health (e.g. allergies); added genes spreading to wild plants; insects evolving resistance; harm to non-target insects; seed controlled by big companies so poor farmers depend on them; ethical feeling that it is "not natural".
Addressed by: extensive testing before release; close monitoring by government agencies; labelling of GM foods; laws/biosafety rules; public education and involving farmers.
**Why:**
- Book p.34: detrimental effects — evolution of resistance, damaging effect on other insects, transfer of the added gene to other plants.
- Book p.35: "not natural", so closely monitored by government agencies; extensive testing needed for ecological stability.

**15. Explain the social, economical, and environmental implications of genetic engineering techniques.**

**Answer:**
- Social: better health (insulin, vaccines, gene therapy), more food; but public fear, ethical/religious objections and cloning debates.
- Economic: higher crop yields and better livestock, cheaper drugs, new industries and jobs; but costly technology and patent/seed costs for poor farmers.

## Notes for lesson design

**Key ideas by subsection (pp.32–39)**
- Biological washing powders: proteases + lipases digest large insoluble stains into small soluble molecules; work at low temperature (saves energy); denatured if water is too hot.
- Biopesticides: bacteria (Bt, *B. popilliae*), viruses (NPV, GV, CPV) and fungi (*Beauveria*, *Metarhizium*, *Verticillium*, *Entomophthora*) kill specific insects.
- Ti plasmid as vector: gene of interest + antibiotic-resistance marker → *A. tumefaciens* → plant cells → antibiotic selection → callus → plant.
- Insect/pest-resistant crops: Bt toxin gene in maize and cotton; yield up, less spraying; risks: resistance, non-target effects, gene transfer.
- Transgenic animals: sheep, goats, chickens, mice making human proteins in milk/eggs.
- Transgenic plants: disease, herbicide, drought resistance, nutrition, shelf life; monitored by government.
- Herbicide resistance: glyphosate-degrading enzyme gene.
- Cloning: identical copies of genes, cells, organisms; Dolly by nuclear transfer from udder cell into enucleated egg.
- Biological warfare: toxins/infectious agents used to kill or incapacitate as an act of war; bioterrorism.

**Common student mistakes**
- Thinking Dolly is identical to the egg donor or the surrogate mother — she is identical to the udder-cell (nucleus) donor.

- Saying hot water makes biological powders work better — enzymes denature.
- Confusing biopesticides (living agents sprayed) with Bt crops (plant makes the toxin itself).
- Confusing gene cloning (copies of a gene) with gene therapy (treating a patient).
- Mixing the book's mislettered headings (two "F)"s) and wrong figure numbers.
