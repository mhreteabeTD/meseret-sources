### Self-Assessment Questions

1. How do you define microorganism?

**Answer:** A microorganism (microbe) is a living organism too small to be seen clearly with the unaided eye, so it must be viewed with a microscope.
**Why:**
- The overview defines microorganisms as "organisms too small to be seen clearly by the unaided eyes".
- "Micro" means very small — small enough that a microscope is needed.
- Examples: bacteria, archaea, fungi (yeasts, moulds), protozoa, microscopic algae; viruses are acellular but are also studied in microbiology.

2. Why is microscopic life vital for all life on earth?

**Answer:** Microbes recycle nutrients, produce much of the oxygen, fix nitrogen and form the base of food webs, so other life depends on them.
**Why:**
- Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) break down dead matter and return C, N, P to soil and air.
- Photosynthetic microbes (cyanobacteria, algae) produce a large share of the world's oxygen and fix CO₂.
- Nitrogen-fixing bacteria turn N₂ into forms plants can use.
- Microbes live symbiotically in guts of animals (digestion, vitamins) and roots of plants.

3. Where do microorganisms live?

**Answer:** Almost everywhere — soil, air, fresh and sea water, on and inside plants, animals and humans, and in extreme places such as hot springs, salt lakes and deep-sea vents.
**Why:**
- Bacteria are described in the text as "omnipresent i.e. present in soil, air and water".
- Archaea live in extreme habitats (very hot, very salty, oxygen-free).
- The human gut, skin and mouth carry huge numbers of microbes.

4. Why is it important to study microbiology?

**Answer:** To prevent and treat infectious diseases, to use microbes in industry, food and agriculture, and to understand their role in the environment.
**Why:**
- Many diseases (tuberculosis, cholera, malaria, ringworm) are caused by microbes; knowing them guides prevention, vaccines and drugs.
- Microbes make bread, yoghurt, cheese, beer, antibiotics, vitamins and enzymes.
- Microbes recycle nutrients, clean sewage and fix nitrogen for crops.
- Microbes are model organisms in genetics and biotechnology.

### Self- questioning

- Describe the following : outer membrane, periplasmic space, envelope, teichoic acid, adhesion site, lipopolysaccharide, and porin protein.

**Answer:**
- *Outer membrane*: a second lipid bilayer outside the thin peptidoglycan of Gram-negative bacteria; its outer leaflet is made of lipopolysaccharide; it is a barrier to many antibiotics, detergents and bile.
- *Periplasmic space*: the gap between the plasma membrane and the outer membrane in Gram-negative bacteria (a smaller one exists in Gram-positives); contains the peptidoglycan and many enzymes and binding proteins.
- *Envelope*: the cell membrane plus everything outside it (cell wall, outer membrane, and capsule if present).
- *Teichoic acid*: chains of glycerol- or ribitol-phosphate in the Gram-positive wall, linked to peptidoglycan (wall teichoic acid) or to membrane lipids (lipoteichoic acid); give a negative charge, help hold ions and maintain the wall.
- *Adhesion site*: places where the plasma membrane and outer membrane of Gram-negative cells appear to touch/join, thought to be routes for moving materials across the envelope.
- *Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)*: molecule of lipid A + core polysaccharide + O-side chain in the outer membrane of Gram-negatives; lipid A is the endotoxin that causes fever and shock.
- *Porin protein*: protein channels in the outer membrane that let small hydrophilic molecules pass through.
**Why:**
- These are the parts of the Gram-negative and Gram-positive envelopes described in the cell wall section (p.48–49) and in Figure 2.5.

- Describe in detail the structure and composition of peptidoglycan of, gram-positive and gram-negative cell walls. Include labeled diagrams in your answer.

**Answer:** Peptidoglycan is a mesh of glycan chains of alternating N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) linked by β(1→4) bonds; a short peptide (tetrapeptide, with D- and L-amino acids) hangs from each NAM, and peptides of neighbouring chains are cross-linked. Gram-positive wall: thick (20–80 nm), many layers of peptidoglycan (up to ~90% of the wall), with teichoic and lipoteichoic acids, no outer membrane. Gram-negative wall: thin peptidoglycan layer (2–7 nm, about 10% of the wall) in the periplasm, covered by an outer membrane containing LPS, porins and lipoproteins (Braun's lipoprotein links it to the peptidoglycan). Diagram: Gram-positive — membrane, thick peptidoglycan with teichoic acids sticking through; Gram-negative — membrane, periplasm with thin peptidoglycan, outer membrane with LPS and porins.
**Why:**
- The book's cell wall section and Figure 2.5 show these layers.
- The thickness of peptidoglycan is what decides the Gram stain result.

- Explain the role of a cell wall in protecting against lysis and illustrate how this role may be experimentally demonstrated.

**Answer:** The cytoplasm of bacteria is more concentrated than most surroundings, so water enters by osmosis; the rigid peptidoglycan wall resists the swelling and stops the cell bursting (osmotic lysis). Experiment: treat bacteria (e.g. a Gram-positive) with lysozyme or penicillin in plain water — cells swell and burst (the suspension clears). Repeat in an isotonic solution (e.g. 0.3–0.5 M sucrose) — cells lose their wall but survive as round protoplasts/spheroplasts; if these are then put into water they burst.
**Why:**
- Lysozyme breaks the NAG–NAM bond; penicillin blocks cross-linking of new peptidoglycan.
- Comparing water vs isotonic sucrose shows that it is the wall (not the membrane) that prevents lysis.

- Distinguish the difference between protoplasts and spheroplasts.

**Answer:** A protoplast is a cell with its wall completely removed (usually from a Gram-positive bacterium, e.g. by lysozyme); a spheroplast still keeps part of its wall — usually a Gram-negative cell that keeps its outer membrane after the peptidoglycan is digested. Both are round and burst in hypotonic solution.
**Why:**
- Gram-positive walls are almost all peptidoglycan, so lysozyme removes the whole wall → protoplast.
- Gram-negative cells have an outer membrane that lysozyme does not digest → part of the envelope remains → spheroplast.

### Bacterial cell wall

Besides their shape bacteria can be classified based on their cell wall composition. One of these ways is whether they are retaining dyes during Gram's stain (Figure 2.6a & b). In this case, bacteria can be categorized into two:

- **Gram-positive** – Gram-positive bacteria have a distinctive purple appearance when observed under a light microscope following **Gram staining**. This is due to retention of the purple crystal violet stain in the thick peptidoglycan layer of the cell wall (Figure 2.5). Examples of Gram-positive bacteria include all staphylococci, all **streptococci** and some **listeria** species.

**Gram-negative** – Gram-negative bacteria **lose the crystal violet** stain (and take the color of the red counterstain) in Gram's method of staining. This is characteristic of bacteria that have a cell wall composed of a thin layer of a particular substance (called **peptidoglycan**).

### 2.1.2. Nutritional types of bacteria

Bacteria have evolved many mechanisms to acquire the energy and nutrients they need for growth and reproduction. Many are autotrophs, organisms that obtain their carbon from inorganic CO₂. Autotrophs that obtain their energy from sunlight are called **photoautotrophs,** while those that harvest energy from inorganic chemicals are called **chemoautotrophs.** Other bacteria are heterotrophs, organisms that obtain at least some of their carbon from organic molecules like glucose. Heterotrophs that obtain their energy from sunlight are called **photoheterotrophs,** while those that harvest energy from organic molecules are called **chemoheterotrophs**. Sources of energy includes;

(1) light energy, and
(2) the energy derived from oxidizing organic or inorganic molecules.

**Phototrophs** use light as their energy source; **chemotrophs** obtain energy from the oxidation of chemical compounds (either organic or inorganic). Bacteria also have only two sources for electrons. **Lithotrophs** (i.e., "rock-eaters") use reduced inorganic substances as their electron source, whereas **organotrophs** extract electrons from reduced organic compounds.

Despite the great metabolic diversity seen in bacteria, most may be placed in one of five nutritional classes based on their primary sources of carbon, energy, and electrons. The majority of bacteria thus far studied are either photolithoautotrophic or chemoorganoheterotrophic.

**Photolithoautotrophs** (often called simply **photoautotrophs**) use light energy and have

(p.53)

CO₂ as their carbon source. Photosynthetic bacteria and cyanobacteria employ water as the electron donor and release oxygen. Other photolithoautotrophs, such as the purple and green sulfur bacteria, cannot oxidize water but extract electrons from inorganic donors such as hydrogen, hydrogen sulfde, and elemental sulfur.

[review: "Photosynthetic bacteria and cyanobacteria employ water…" — only cyanobacteria (and algae) use water and release oxygen; other photosynthetic bacteria do not. Typo "sulfde" → "sulfide".]

**Chemoorganoheterotrophs** (sometimes called **chemoheterotrophs** or chemoorganotrophs) use organic compounds as sources of energy, hydrogen, electrons, and carbon.

Frequently the same organic nutrient will satisfy all these requirements. Nearly all pathogenic microorganisms are chemoorganoheterotrophs. The other nutritional types have fewer known microorganisms but are very important ecologically. Some photosynthetic bacteria (purple and green bacteria) use organic matter as their electron donor and carbon source. These **Photoorganoheterotrophs** are common inhabitants of polluted lakes and streams. Some of these bacteria also can grow as photolithoautotrophs with molecular hydrogen as an electron donor.

**Chemolithoautotrophs** oxidize reduced inorganic compounds such as iron, nitrogen, or sulfur molecules to derive both energy and electrons for biosynthesis. Carbon dioxide is the carbon source.

**Chemolithoheterotrophs** use reduced inorganic molecules as their energy and electron source but derive their carbon from organic sources. Chemolithotrophs contribute greatly to the chemical transformations of elements (e.g., the conversion of ammonia to nitrate or sulfur to sulfate) that continually occur in ecosystems.

Most bacterial organisms reproduce by an asexual process called **binary fission**, which usually occurs after a period of growth in which the cell doubles in mass. At this time, the chromosome (DNA) replicates and the two DNA molecules separate (Figure 2.7). Chromosome segregation is not well understood. Unlike eukaryotic cells, bacterial cells lack a mitotic spindle to separate replicated chromosomes. The segregation process does involve specialized chromosomal-associated proteins but there is no clear picture describing how most of these proteins work to ensure accurate chromosome segregation. In any event, cell fission at midcell involves the synthesis of a partition, or septum, that separates the mother cell into two genetically identical daughter cells.

**Answer:** Fungi live almost everywhere but mostly in cool, dark, moist places with decaying material — soil, dead wood, leaf litter, foods, on/in plants and animals, and in water (chytrids, water moulds). **Why:**

- What features of fungal species may be interpreted as adaptations to life as a saprobe, a pathogen, or a mutualistic/symbiont? Which fungal features permit some fungal species and not others to be pathogenic on humans?

**Answer:** Adaptations: hyphae give a large surface area and grow at the tips into food; secretion of digestive enzymes and absorptive nutrition; huge numbers of light spores for spread; tough chitin walls resisting drying. - Absorptive, chemoheterotrophic nutrition (point 8) needs high surface area and secreted enzymes.

- Why are there two names for the same fungus or fungal group? Provide specific examples.

**Answer:** Because the asexual (anamorph) and sexual (teleomorph) stages of many fungi were found and named separately before they were known to be the same organism. **Why:**
- Many fungi rarely show their sexual stage, so the asexual form was named first; the sexual form was later given a different name.

Microbiologists use the term **fungus** [pl., fungi; Latin *fungus,* mushroom] to describe eucaryotic organisms that are spore-bearing, have absorptive nutrition, lack chlorophyll, and reproduce sexually and asexually. Scientists who study fungi are **mycologists** [Greek *mykes,* mushroom, and *logos,* science], and the scientific discipline devoted to fungi is called **mycology.** The study of fungal toxins and their effects is called **mycotoxicology,** and the diseases caused by fungi in animals are known as **mycoses** (s., mycosis).

### 2.3.2. Ecology of Fungi

Fungi have colonized nearly all environments on Earth, but are frequently found in cool, dark, moist places with a supply of decaying material. Fungi are saprobes that decompose organic matter. Many successful mutualistic relationships involve a fungus and another organism. Many fungi establish complex mycorrhizal associations with the roots of plants. Some ants farm fungi as a supply of food.

Lichens are a symbiotic relationship between a fungus and a photosynthetic organism, usually an algaor cyanobacterium. The photosynthetic organism provides energy derived from light and carbohydrates, while the fungus supplies minerals and protection. Some animals that consume fungi help disseminate spores over long distances.

#### B) Sexual reproduction

(p.66)

Many fungi also produce spores by sexual reproduction. In this process, opposite mating types come together and fuse (Figure 2.16). Because the nuclei are genetically different in each mating type, the fusion cell represents a heterokaryon (*hetero* = "different"; *karyo* = "nucleus"); that is, a cell with genetically dissimilar nuclei existing for some length of time in a common dikaryotic cytoplasm. Eventually the nuclei fuse and a diploid cell is formed. The chromosome number soon is halved by meiosis, returning the cell or organism to a haploid condition.

A visible fruiting body often results during sexual reproduction and it is the location of the haploid spores. Perhaps the most recognized fruiting body from which spores are produced is the mushroom.

### 2.3.5. Economic importance of fungi

Fungi are among the economic important microrganisms. They have both beneficial and harmnful aspects.

#### Beneficial aspects of fungi

1. Fungi exist either as *saprobes* or *parasites*. Their preeminent ability to break down complex organic substrates of almost every type is an important and essential activity in the recycling of carbon and other elements in the cycle of life.

### Self-questioning

- **Can protozoan reproduce?**

  **Answer:** Yes. Protozoa reproduce mainly asexually, and many also have a sexual stage.

  **Why:**
  - Asexual: binary fission (Amoeba, Euglena, Paramecium), budding, multiple fission (schizogony, e.g. *Plasmodium*), and cysts.
  - Sexual: conjugation (ciliates such as Paramecium) or syngamy (fusion of male and female gametes to form a zygote, e.g. *Plasmodium* in the mosquito).

- **How are Amoeba, Euglena, and Paramecium reproduced?**

  **Answer:** Amoeba by binary fission; Euglena by longitudinal binary fission; Paramecium by transverse binary fission (asexual) and conjugation (sexual exchange of micronuclei).

  - Amoeba: the nucleus divides by mitosis and the cytoplasm pinches in two (Figure 2.20, "binary fission (ameba)"); in bad conditions it can form a cyst.

  - Paramecium: the cell splits across its middle (transverse fission); the micronucleus divides by mitosis and the macronucleus elongates and divides.
  - Paramecium conjugation: two cells pair and swap haploid micronuclei; this gives genetic variation but no increase in number at the moment of exchange (Figure 2.21).

#### Reproduction in protozoa

Most protozoa are asexual and reproduce in one of three ways. These are:

- *Fission:* Fission occurs when a cell divides evenly to form two new cells (Figure 2.20).
- *Budding:* Budding occurs when a cell divides unevenly.
- *Multiple fission (schizogony):* Multiple fission is when the nucleus of the cell divides multiple times before the rest of the cell divides. Forms around each nucleus when the nucleus divides then each nuclei separates into a daughter cell.

#### Nutrition in Protozoans

Protists receive nutrients by breaking down organic matter (*heterotrophic*) and can grow in both aerobic and anaerobic environments, such as protists that live in the intestine of animals. Some protists, such as *Euglena*, receive nutrients from organic matter and through photosynthesis because they contain chlorophyll. These protists are considered both algae and protozoa. Protists obtain food in one of three ways:

- **Absorption:** Food is absorbed across the protist's plasma membrane.
- **Ingestion:** Cilia outside the protist create a wave-like motion to move food into a mouth-like opening in the protist called a *cytosome*. An example is the paramecium.
- **Engulf:** Pseudopods (meaning "false feet") on the protist engulf food, then pull it into the cell using a process called *phagocytosis*. An example of this type of protist is the amoeba.

## 2.5. Viruses

### Keyword

**A virus** is a very small, non-cellular parasite of cells. Its genome, which is composed of either DNA or RNA, is enclosed in a protein coat. Viruses are different from other micro-organisms because they have no cellular organelles and so cannot carry out any metabolic processes; they must all enter other cells to reproduce

### Self-questioning

- **Why we studying viruses? Discuss in-group and present to your class.**

  **Answer:** Because viruses cause many serious diseases of humans, animals and plants, and are also useful tools in science and medicine.

  **Why:**
  - Health: HIV/AIDS, hepatitis B, measles, polio, rabies, influenza, COVID-19 — knowing how they spread lets us prevent them (vaccines, hygiene, safe sex).
  - Economy: viruses destroy crops (e.g. maize streak, tobacco mosaic) and livestock (e.g. foot-and-mouth disease, Newcastle disease in poultry).
  - Science and technology: vaccines, gene therapy vectors, phage therapy against bacteria, and understanding of DNA, genes and cancer.

- **How viruses differ from other organisms?**

  **Answer:** Viruses are not cells: they have no cytoplasm, ribosomes or other organelles, contain only one type of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA), cannot carry out metabolism, and can only multiply inside a living host cell.

  **Why:**
  - Organisms (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, plants, animals) are cellular, have both DNA and RNA, make their own proteins and energy, and grow and divide.
  - Viruses are assembled from parts, not divided; outside a host they are inert particles that can even be crystallized.
  - They are much smaller (about 20–450 nm, seen only with an electron microscope) and are not affected by antibiotics.

- **Write in brief the general features and occurrence of viruses.**

  **Answer:** Viruses are ultramicroscopic (20–450 nm), acellular, obligate intracellular parasites made of a DNA or RNA genome inside a protein capsid (some also with a lipid envelope). They occur wherever there are living cells — in bacteria (bacteriophages), fungi, algae, protozoa, plants and animals, including humans.

  **Why:**
  - These features are listed in 2.5.1 (p.79).
  - Outside cells they survive as inert virions in air, water, soil, body fluids and on surfaces, waiting to reach a suitable host.

Viruses are small, obligate, intracellular particles; that is, most can be seen only with the electron microscope (Figure 2.23) and they must infect and take over a host cell in order to replicate. This is because they lack the chemical machinery for generating energy and synthesizing large molecules. Viruses, therefore, must find an appropriate host cell in which they can replicate—and, as a result, often cause disease.

### 2.5.1. Characteristics of virus

- Viruses have an inner core of nucleic acid surrounded by protein coat known as an envelope. [review: the protein coat is called the **capsid**, not the envelope; the envelope is the extra lipid membrane some viruses have outside the capsid — as the book itself says below.]
- They cannot be grown on artifcial cell free media (However, grow in animals, eggs or tissue culture).
- Viruses do not have a cellular organization. They do not have cell wall or cell membrane or cellular organelles including ribosomes.
- They do not occur free in nature but act as obligate intracellular parasite.
- They lack the enzymes necessary for protein and nucleic acid synthesis and are dependent for replication on the synthetic machinery of host cells.
- They are unaffected by antibacterial antibiotics.
- Viruses are inert (nucleoprotein) filterable Agents
- Virus occupy a space in between living and non-living, because they are crystallizable and non-living outside the body of host.
- Obligate intracellular parasites of bacteria, protozoa, fungi, algae, plants, and animals.
- Ultramicroscopic size, ranging from 20 nm up to 450 nm (diameter)
- Do not independently fulfill the characteristics of life
- Inactive macromolecules outside the host cell and active only inside host cells
- Basic structure consists of protein shell (capsid) surrounding nucleic acid core.

### 2.5.2. Viral symmetry

#### 1. Helical symmetry

There are several viruses found with a **helical** morphology. These viruses consist of identical protein subunits or protomers which assembled in a **helical** structure around the genome (Figure 2.14). This type of protein subunits generally forms a rigid nucleocapsid. Moreover, the helical structure provides flexibility to the filaments. The most common example of a helical virus is the tobacco mosaic virus. Apart from that Sendai virus is also reported to have a helical structure.

#### 1. Icosahedral Symmetry

An icosahedron structure refers to a type of **polyhedron** with 20 equilateral triangular faces and 12 vertices. The rigid structure provides protection to the genome (Figure 2.14). The common examples of viruses reported to have an icosahedral structure are papovavirus, picornavirus, adenovirus, toga virus, etc.

- The viruses that contain DNA as their genetic material are called the DNA viruses. RNA viruses, on the other hand, contain RNA as their genetic material.

- **Can viruses reproduce? What does it mean by the lytic and lysogenic cycle?**

  **Answer:** Viruses cannot reproduce on their own; they replicate only inside a living host cell using its machinery. In the lytic cycle the virus multiplies at once and bursts (lyses) the host cell to release new viruses. In the lysogenic cycle the viral DNA joins the host chromosome (as a prophage) and is copied with it each time the cell divides, without killing it, until it is triggered to enter the lytic cycle.

  **Why:**
  - Lytic steps: attachment → penetration (injection of DNA) → biosynthesis of viral parts → assembly (maturation) → release by lysis.
  - Lysogenic: after penetration the phage DNA integrates into the bacterial DNA; it can stay dormant for many generations; stress such as UV light can switch it to the lytic cycle.

### Self-questioning

- **What are Retroviruses?**

  **Answer:** Retroviruses are enveloped RNA viruses that carry the enzyme reverse transcriptase, which copies their RNA genome into DNA after infection; this DNA is then inserted (integrated) into the host cell's chromosome. HIV is the best-known example.

  **Why:**
  - Key words (p.83): "an RNA virus that converts its genetic information from RNA into DNA after it has infected a host".
  - "Retro" = backwards: information flows RNA → DNA, the reverse of the usual DNA → RNA (p.81: RNA-dependent DNA polymerase = reverse transcriptase).

- **How does HIV reproduce and cause AIDS?**

  **Answer:** HIV infects CD4 (helper T) cells, copies its RNA into DNA, inserts it into the cell's DNA and uses the cell to make new viruses, which bud off. Over years the CD4 cells are destroyed faster than they are replaced; when the count falls very low (below 200 cells/mm³) the immune system fails and opportunistic infections and cancers appear — this stage is AIDS.

  **Why:** (steps)
  - Attachment: the gp120 spike binds CD4 and a co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4) on the T cell.
  - Fusion: gp41 fuses the viral envelope with the cell membrane; the capsid enters.
  - Reverse transcription: reverse transcriptase makes DNA from the viral RNA.
  - Integration: integrase inserts the viral DNA into the host chromosome (provirus).
  - Synthesis and assembly: the cell makes viral RNA and proteins; protease cuts the proteins into working parts.
  - Budding: new virions bud out with a piece of host membrane as envelope; infected cells die.
  - Loss of helper T cells removes the "coordinators" of both antibody and cell-mediated immunity → TB, pneumonia (*Pneumocystis*), candidiasis, Kaposi's sarcoma, etc.

- **Can AIDS be treated?**

  **Answer:** AIDS cannot yet be cured, but it can be controlled. Antiretroviral therapy (ART, a combination of 3 drugs taken every day for life) stops HIV from multiplying, lets CD4 counts recover and allows people to live long, healthy lives; opportunistic infections are treated separately.

  **Why:**
  - ART drugs block reverse transcriptase, integrase or protease.
  - When the viral load becomes undetectable, HIV is not passed on sexually (U = U), and mother-to-child transmission can be prevented (PMTCT).
  - There is no vaccine yet; the provirus hidden in host DNA is why a cure is hard.

- **What is the social and economic impact of AIDS?**

  **Answer:** AIDS mostly hits people aged 15–49, the most productive age group, so it harms families, the workforce and the national economy.

  **Why:**
  - Social: orphans, stigma and discrimination, broken families, children leaving school to care for sick parents or earn money.
  - Economic: loss of workers and skills, lower farm and factory output, lost income, high cost of treatment and care for families and the health system.
  - Health system: hospital beds and budgets used for HIV and opportunistic infections such as TB.

#### Viral Replication

A virus invades a living host cell a thousand or more times its size, hijacks the metabolism of the cell to produce copies of itself, and often destroys the host cell when new virions (a completely assembled virus outside its host cell ) are released.

Replication has been studied in a wide range of viruses and their host cells. We examine the bacteriophages first and then discuss the animal viruses. One of the best studied processes of replication is that carried out by bacteriophages of the T-even group (T for "type").

In general, viruses go through the following five steps in their **replication cycles** to produce more virions:

1. **Adsorption**, the attachment of viruses to host cells.
2. **Penetration**, the entry of virions (or their genome) into host cells.
3. **Synthesis**, the synthesis of new nucleic acid molecules, capsid proteins, and other viral components within host cells while using the metabolic machinery of those cells.
4. **Maturation**, the assembly of newly synthesized viral components into complete virions.
5. **Release**, the departure of new virions from host cells. Release generally, but not always, kills (lyses) host cells.

#### Phages exhibit two different types of life cycle:

(p.86)

**Lytic cycle:** also known as virulent cycle. In this cycle, intracellular multiplication of the phage results in the lysis of host bacteria, resulting in release of progeny virions (Figure 2.28).

**Lytic cycle** of bacteriophage (Replication of a virulent bacteriophage). A virulent phage undergoes a lytic cycle to produce new phage particles within a bacterial cell. Cell lysis releases new phage particles that can infect more bacteria).

**Lysogenic cycle**: Infection with every phage does not result in lysis of the host cells (Figure 2.29). The integrated phage nucleic acid is known as the prophage. The prophage behaves like a segment of the host chromosome and multiplies synchronously with it. This phenomenon is (p.87) known as lysogeny. The bacterium that carries a prophage within its genome is called lysogenic bacterium and such phages are called lysogenic or temperate phages.

### Common student mistakes
- Calling the protein coat the "envelope" (the book itself does this on p.79) — capsid = protein coat; envelope = lipid membrane outside it.
- Thinking asexual fungal spores are made by meiosis — they come from mitosis; meiosis follows nuclear fusion in sexual reproduction.
- Mixing up sporangiospores (enclosed) and conidia (exposed); thinking heterokaryon = diploid (it has two separate haploid nuclei).
- Believing conjugation in Paramecium increases cell numbers — it only exchanges micronuclei; numbers increase by fission afterwards.
- Swapping vectors: tsetse (trypanosomes) vs sand fly (Leishmania) vs Anopheles (Plasmodium); thinking giardiasis is spread by insects.
- Thinking antibiotics cure viral infections.
- Confusing the order of replication steps, or thinking the whole T4 phage enters the bacterium (only the DNA is injected).
- Thinking the lysogenic phage is harmless forever — induction can switch it to the lytic cycle.
- Assuming "all DNA viruses replicate in the nucleus / all RNA viruses are single-stranded" (exceptions: poxvirus, reovirus).
- The figure captions on p.69 are swapped (scalp = tinea capitis, nails = onychomycosis) — warn students.

### The Germ Theory of Disease and Koch's Postulates

In order to prove whether or not diseases are caused by microorganisms, Koch used mice as experimental animals. Using appropriate controls, Koch demonstrated that when a small amount of blood from a diseased mouse was injected into a healthy mouse, the latter quickly developed anthrax. He took blood from this second animal, injected it

(p.92)

into another, and again observed the characteristic disease symptoms (Figure 2.31). However, Koch carried this experiment a critically important step further. He discovered that the anthrax bacteria could be grown in nutrient fluids outside the host and that even after many transfers in laboratory culture, the bacteria still caused the disease when inoculated into a healthy animal. On the basis of these experiments and others on the causative agent of tuberculosis, Koch formulated a set of rigorous criteria, now known as **Koch's postulates**, for definitively linking a specific microorganism to a specific disease. Koch's postulates state the following:

1. The disease-causing organism must always be present in animals suffering from the disease but not in healthy animals.
2. The organism must be cultivated in a pure culture away from the animal body.
3. The isolated organism must cause the disease when inoculated into healthy susceptible animals.
4. The organism must be isolated from the newly infected animals and cultured again in the laboratory, after which it should be seen to be the same as the original organism

[figure: Figure 2.31 — Koch's postulates shown as a cycle using anthrax. A dead animal (cow) with a micrograph inset labelled "Anthrax bacilli" and "Spore".
- Postulate 1: The same microorganisms are present in every case of the disease.
- Postulate 2: The microorganisms are isolated from the tissues of a dead animal, and a pure culture is prepared (flask of red culture medium with rod-shaped bacteria in a circle).
- Postulate 3: Microorganisms from the pure culture are inoculated into a healthy, susceptible animal (syringe into a mouse). The disease is reproduced (sick mouse).
- Postulate 4: The identical microorganisms are isolated and recultivated from the tissue specimens of the experimental animal (second flask with the same rods).
Arrows connect the steps in order 1 → 2 → 3 → 4.]

**? What is an infectious disease is?**

**Answer:** An infectious disease is a disease caused by a pathogen (a bacterium, virus, fungus, protozoan or parasitic worm) that enters the body and multiplies, and that can usually be passed from one host to another.
**Why:**
- "Infectious" means caused by an infecting organism (pathogen), not by genes, diet or injury.
- The pathogen invades the body, grows and damages tissues, causing symptoms.
- Most infectious diseases are communicable: they spread by contact, air, water, food or vectors (section 2.7).

**? How will you prove that microorganisms can cause disease?**

**Answer:** By following Koch's postulates.
**Why:**
- Show the same microorganism is present in every case of the disease and not in healthy animals.
- Isolate it and grow it in pure culture outside the body.
- Inoculate the pure culture into a healthy, susceptible animal and show it causes the same disease.
- Re-isolate the organism from the newly infected animal and show it is identical to the original.

**Answer:** They protect us from pathogens and help the body in other ways.
**Why:**
- They cover binding sites so pathogens cannot attach.

### Bioremediation

Bioremediation is a natural process that relies on microorganisms and plants and/or their derivatives (enzymes or spent biomass) to degrade or alter environmental contaminants as these organisms carry out their normal life functions. As defined, microbial bioremediation makes use of microorganisms and/or their derivatives (enzymes or spent biomass) to clean-up environmental contaminants.

It is important to note that microorganisms are everywhere and as such pollutants in the different environmental compartments always come into contact with microorganisms. Microbes break down/transform pollutants via their inherent metabolic processes with or without slight pathway modifications to allow the pollutant to be channeled into the normal microbial metabolic pathway for degradation/and biotransformation.

Applied bioremediation methods therefore focus on tapping the naturally occurring microbial catabolic capabilities to degrade, transform or accumulate most of the synthetic compounds such as hydrocarbons (e.g., oil), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), polyaromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), radionuclides and metals. The natural existence of a large diversity of microbial species expands the variety of chemical pollutants that are degraded or detoxified.

Bioremediation is a natural process, it takes a little time, as an acceptable waste treatment process for contaminated material such as soil. Microbes able to degrade the contaminant and increase in numbers when the contaminant is present. When the contaminant is degraded, the biodegradative population declines. The residues for the treatment are usually harmless products including water, carbon dioxide and cell biomass.

2. Which are the main types of microorganisms in Ethiopian food fermentation?

**Answer:** Yeasts (mainly *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*) and lactic acid bacteria (e.g. *Lactobacillus*, *Leuconostoc*, *Streptococcus*/*Lactococcus*), with some moulds and acetic acid bacteria in a few products.

**Why:**
- Yeasts ferment sugars to alcohol and CO₂ — this gives the alcohol in tella and tej and the "eyes" (bubbles) in injera and bread.
- Lactic acid bacteria ferment sugars to lactic acid — this gives the sour taste of injera, kocho, ergo and siljo and helps preserve them.
- Most traditional products use a mixed culture of both, often from a back-slopping starter (ersho for injera, previous batch for tella).

3. What microorganisms are important in the spoilage of foods?

**Answer:** Bacteria (e.g. *Pseudomonas*, *Bacillus*, *Clostridium*, lactic acid bacteria in drinks), moulds (e.g. *Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*, *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*) and yeasts.

**Why:**
- Bacteria spoil moist, protein-rich foods such as meat, milk and fish (souring, slime, bad smell).
- Moulds grow on bread, fruits, grains and stored food; some (e.g. *Aspergillus flavus*) make mycotoxins such as aflatoxin (book section 2.3.5).
- Yeasts spoil sugary foods and drinks (fruit juices, honey, jam) by fermenting them.

4. What are the industrial roles of microorganisms?

**Answer:** Microorganisms are used to make foods and drinks, medicines, chemicals and fuels, and to treat waste.

**Why:**
- Food and drink: bread, cheese, yoghurt, butter (Figure 2.34), beer, wine, tella, tej, vinegar.
- Medicines: antibiotics (penicillin from *Penicillium*), vaccines, and human insulin and growth hormone made by genetically modified bacteria/yeast (Figure 2.35).
- Chemicals: organic acids (citric acid from *Aspergillus*), enzymes, industrial alcohol, amino acids, vitamins.
- Energy and environment: biogas (methane from methanogens), ethanol fuel, sewage treatment and cleaning up pollution (bioremediation).

#### The phosphorus cycle

Phosphorus (P) occurs in soils as both organic and inorganic forms (Figure 2.40). Phosphorus can be found dissolved in the soil solution in very low amounts or associated with soil minerals or organic materials. The relative amounts of each form of phosphorus vary greatly among soils, with the total amount of P in a clayey-textured soil being up to ten times greater than in a sandy soil.

(p.106)

**Organic P in soils**. A large number of compounds make up the organic P in soils, with the majority being of microbial origin. Organic P is held very tightly and is generally not available for plant uptake until the organic materials are decomposed and the phosphorus released via the mineralization process. Mineralization is carried out by microbes, and as with nitrogen, the rate of P release is affected by factors such as soil moisture, composition of the organic material, oxygen concentration and pH.

**Inorganic P in soils**. The concentration of inorganic P (orthophosphates) in the soil solution at any given time is very small, amounting to less than 1 lb. /A. Phosphorus in the inorganic form occurs mostly as aluminum, iron or calcium compounds.

[added note: "1 lb./A" means 1 pound per acre, which is about 1.1 kg per hectare.]
