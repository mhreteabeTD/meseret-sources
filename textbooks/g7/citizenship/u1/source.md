### Introduction

As you may recall, in your lower grade you have learned about moral education and the values that make a good citizen. In this grade level, you will be aware of the connection between moral and civic education. This grade level is entirely dedicated to citizenship education, with the first unit covering the definition of citizenship, its essence, characteristics of good citizenship, and the significance of good citizenship. This unit also examines how a person obtains and loses citizenship. This unit will conclude by discussing some key aspects of Ethiopian citizenship law

### Keywords

- Citizen; Citizenship; Citizenship Law; Constitution, Good Citizenship; Patriotism; Responsibility; Tolerance.

(p.3)

### Unit Outcomes

At the end of this unit, learners will be able to:

- Comprehend the essence of citizenship;
- Identify features of citizenship;
- Explain the importance of good citizenship;
- List ways of acquiring and losing citizenship;
- Explain the key elements of citizenship law in Ethiopia.

## 1.1 Citizenship

*(Section 1.1 starts on book p.3.)*

This section will teach you about the essence of citizenship, the characteristics of good citizenship, and the significance of good citizenship. However, before diving into each section, we will try to discuss the following questions based on your prior knowledge.

#### Start-up questions (p.3)

1. What do you understand when we say citizenship?

**Answer:** Citizenship means full and responsible membership of the state; it is both a legal status and an identity.
**Why:** The book defines citizenship on p.5 as "full and responsible membership of the state", where members have a duty to the State and the State has an obligation to protect the member, "Therefore, citizenship is a legal status and an identity."

2. Is there any difference between citizen and citizenship?

**Answer:** Yes. A citizen is the *person* who has specific rights and responsibilities under the law; citizenship is the *status/membership* that person holds in the state.
**Why:** p.4 says "A citizen is a person who has specific rights and responsibilities under the law", while p.5 says "Citizenship in turn refers to full and responsible membership of the state … a legal status and an identity." So one is the person, the other is the membership/status.

(p.4)

### 1.1.1 The Essence of Citizenship

*(Lesson 1.1.1 starts on book p.4.)*

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Describe what citizen is;
- Realize the rights and responsibilities of citizens;
- Differentiate citizen and citizenship.

Before discussing about the essence of citizenship, it is necessary to define what it means to be a citizen. A citizen is a person who has specific rights and responsibilities under the law. Citizens are granted specific rights and benefits by the state. Citizens are expected to follow their country's rules and defend it against its enemies in exchange.

As Ethiopian citizens, we have the right to express ourselves, to be protected by the law, and to bear responsibilities such as obeying the law and paying taxes. As a result, a citizen is someone who has the right to be called a citizen.

> **REMEMBER**
> A citizen is someone who has the right to be called a citizen.

[review: the definition "a citizen is someone who has the right to be called a citizen" is circular as printed (p.4 text and Remember box). For lessons, the clearer book definition is the first one on p.4: "a person who has specific rights and responsibilities under the law."]

(p.5)

Citizenship in turn refers to full and responsible membership of the state. In this situation, membership refers to a political society in which members have a reciprocal obligation of duty to the State and the State has an obligation of protection to the member. Therefore, citizenship is a legal status and an identity.

[Figure 1.1: A person who is legally protected — two smiling men in dark suits hold up a framed certificate bearing a red circular stamp reading "PATENTED" with "INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY" around the edge.]

> **REMEMBER**
> Citizenship is both a legal status and a sense of identity.

#### Case Study (p.5–7)

In a small village, there were two children. These were children who helped their parents with various tasks and were well-liked by their family as well as the local community.

(p.6)

Teachers who arrived in that small village realized it was time for the children to return to school. As a result, the teachers approached the children's parents and asked them to send their children to school the following school year. After carefully listening to the teachers' ideas, the children's parents stated that they did not agree with the suggestion and did not want to send their children to school. The parents of the children claimed that rather than sending their children to school, it would be far better for them to obtain and continue to receive much more assistance in their daily activities from them.

[Figure 1.2: Discussion between a teacher and parents — in a rural setting with cattle and a wooden fence behind, a teacher in a white coat holding papers talks to a mother (pink dress) and father (pink shirt); thought/speech bubbles read "1. Good morning?" (teacher), "2. Good morning Teacher?" (father), "3. Why don't you send the children to school?" (teacher).]

Based on the above case study answer and discuss the questions below with a group of five students:

1. What do you think will be the end of this story?

**Answer:** [open question] A good ending is that the teachers (and the community) convince the parents that education is the children's right, and the children go back to school while still helping at home after class.
**Why:** The section teaches that citizens have rights protected by the law and that the State has "an obligation of protection to the member" (p.4–5); obtaining an education is named as a right and responsibility of a good citizen (p.11).

2. How convincing is the parents' justification for not sending their children to school?

**Answer:** Not convincing. Wanting help with daily work does not outweigh the children's right to education; the children can help their parents and still attend school.
**Why:** The parents' only reason is to "receive much more assistance in their daily activities" (p.6). The book later lists "obtaining an education" as a responsibility of a good citizen (p.11) and says "one of the primary responsibilities of students is to learn" (p.11).

(p.7)

3. Do you believe that a child's right to attend school is only dependent on his or her parents' goodwill?

**Answer:** No. Going to school is the child's own right as a citizen, protected by law; it does not depend only on what the parents want.
**Why:** p.4 says citizens have rights "under the law" and are "protected by the law"; the State has an obligation to protect its members (p.5). A right protected by law is not just a favour from parents.

4. In your opinion, what are the rights and obligations of the children?

**Answer:** [open question] Rights: to go to school, to express themselves, to be protected by the law. Obligations: to learn, to help their family, to respect parents and teachers, and to obey rules.
**Why:** p.4 lists the rights "to express ourselves, to be protected by the law" and responsibilities "such as obeying the law"; p.11 says students' primary responsibility is to learn, complete homework and respect classmates and teachers.

Based on the preceding story, we will examine a few of the characteristics of a good citizen.

#### A.) Obey the law

**A.) Obey the law:** is one of the most important public duty of a good citizen is to obey the law. A law is a set of rules established by a community that governs how people act and behave. A good citizen follows the rules, obeys parents, teachers, and those in positions of authority, observes just laws, and upholds democratic principles. A good citizen is also required to follow the laws of the country, such as paying taxes, preserving the environment, and treating people with respect. As a result, in the preceding story, one of the students' major contributions was stated as respecting the school's rules and regulations. Indeed, every one of you is responsible for adhering to the school's rules and regulations.

#### B). Responsibility

**B). Responsibility:** is the other important characteristic of good citizenship. Responsibility entails taking action and encompasses many aspects of what we consider to be good citizenship. You might want to emphasize that one of the primary responsibilities of students is to learn. At home, you are expected to complete your homework, and at school, you are expected to respect your classmates and teachers. You also have the responsibility to report any wrongdoing if you witness it. For instance, as a good citizen, you have a responsibility to report tax fraud and smuggling if you see or hear about it.

Furthermore, keeping the environment clean, obtaining an education, and participating in community service are all responsibilities of a good citizen. When you look at the student in the preceding story, you will notice that he/she is extremely responsible not only in class but also in his/her extracurricular activities.

## 1.2 Ways of Acquiring and Losing Citizenship

*(Section 1.2 starts on book p.19.)*

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Make a list of ways for obtaining citizenship;
- Compare ways of acquiring of citizenship by birth and descent;
- Write ways of acquiring citizenship through naturalization.

(p.20)

#### Start-up questions (p.20)

1. How does someone become a citizen of a country?

**Answer:** According to that country's rules — most commonly by place of birth (right of soil), by descent from citizen parents (right of blood), or by naturalization (e.g. through marriage or adoption).
**Why:** p.20 says a person obtains nationality "in accordance with the state's rules"; p.21–23 list the three most prevalent ways.

2. Do you think that anyone, at any time, can travel to any country and become a citizen of that country?

**Answer:** No. A person becomes a citizen only if he or she meets the legal conditions of that country, and each country sets its own rules.
**Why:** p.20: "Anyone can become a member of the state if they meet the legal conditions of the country" and "international law leaves it up to each state to define who its citizens are."

Citizenship is a legal status earned by being a registered member of a country. Anyone can become a member of the state if they meet the legal conditions of the country. Citizenship is the virtue of being a citizen of a country in its simplest form. As a general rule, international law leaves it up to each state to define who its citizens are. As a result, different states have distinct rules and procedures in granting and loss of citizenship. In this section, we'll look at how a person can become a citizen of a country, and then at how a person can lose their citizenship.

### A) Ways of Acquiring Citizenship

A person may obtain the nationality of a state in accordance with the state's rules, as described in the section's opening remark. As a result, the procedure for getting citizenship varies each country. However, the most prevalent methods of obtaining citizenship are as follows:

(p.21)

**1. Citizenship by Place of Birth:** Citizenship by birth is the most prevalent method of getting citizenship, and many countries use it. Right of soil is the term used to describe this notion. According to this idea, everyone born inside the territorial bounds of a state automatically becomes a citizen of that state. It simply means that you are eligible to become a citizen of a country if you were born there.

[review: the book says citizenship by place of birth (right of soil) is "the most prevalent method"; worldwide, descent (right of blood) is in fact more widespread, and Ethiopia itself does not use right of soil (p.28). Kept as printed; lessons should avoid stressing "most prevalent".]

[Figure 1.10: A Newborn Baby — a woman in a green top holds a newborn wrapped in white while another woman in grey looks on.]

**2. Citizenship by Descent:** A person can also become a citizen of a country based on the citizenship of one or both of his parents. This is referred to as the notion of blood right. This principle asserts that regardless of where children are born, their citizenship is decided by their parents' nationality.

(p.22)

[Figure 1.11: Parents with their newborn Baby Girl — a mother in a white shawl holds a baby while the smiling father in a blue sweater sits beside her, in front of thatched round huts in a rural village.]

**3. By Naturalization:** Naturalization is the legal process by which a foreign individual becomes eligible to become a citizen of a specific country. A person who wishes to become a citizen through naturalization must fill out an application and submit a request for citizenship by naturalization. Naturalization can be obtained in a variety of methods, but for the purposes of this grade level, we will concentrate on only two of them: marriage and adoption.

**a) Citizenship by Marriage:** Upon application, a foreign national who marries a citizen of a certain country is granted citizenship of that country.

(p.23)

[Figure 1.12: A Couple — a smiling dark-skinned man in a suit and tie and a smiling fair-haired woman embracing him from behind.]

**B) Citizenship by Adoption:** A person or couple recognized as legal parents can adopt a kid born in another country and make him or her citizen. Before this may be given, however, certain steps must be undertaken. The following are some of the most prevalent eligibility criteria:

- The parents (couple or individual) must be permanent citizens of a state, either by birth or naturalization;
- the child must be under the age of 18; and
- the child must be a permanent citizen of the state from which he/she is adopted.

[review: sub-points under "3. By Naturalization" are labelled inconsistently in the book — "a) Citizenship by Marriage" then "B) Citizenship by Adoption" (capital B, which also clashes with the "B) Ways of Losing Citizenship" heading on p.24). Kept as printed; read as a) and b).]

(p.24)

[Figure 1.13: Adopted Child — a smiling dark-skinned woman in a green top carries a fair-skinned laughing toddler in a blue shirt who points ahead.]

#### Check-point (p.24)

1. Can you list the different ways of acquiring citizenship?

**Answer:** (1) By place of birth (right of soil); (2) by descent (right of blood); (3) by naturalization — e.g. (a) by marriage and (b) by adoption.
**Why:** These are the ways listed and explained on p.21–23.

2. What is the main difference between acquiring citizenship by birth and by naturalization?

**Answer:** Citizenship by birth is automatic — a person born within the state's territory becomes a citizen; citizenship by naturalization is acquired later by a foreigner through a legal process of applying and meeting conditions.
**Why:** p.21: by birth, everyone born inside the territory "automatically becomes a citizen"; p.22: naturalization is "the legal process by which a foreign individual becomes eligible" and requires filling out an application and submitting a request.

### B) Ways of Losing Citizenship

*(Starts on book p.24.)*

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Identify ways of losing citizenship;
- Distinguish between voluntary and involuntary ways of losing citizenship;
- Examine the grounds for involuntary citizenship loss.

(p.25)

#### Start-up questions (p.25)

1. How can a person lose his/her citizenship status?

**Answer:** Either voluntarily (by giving up / renouncing it) or involuntarily (when the state revokes it, without the person's consent, for not following certain requirements).
**Why:** p.25: those who lost citizenship "either did so voluntarily or were forced to do so forcefully without their consent"; p.25–26 explain voluntary and involuntary loss.

Citizenship can be lost in a variety of ways, just as it can be gained in a variety of ways. A circumstance in which a citizen is no longer recognized a lawful citizen of the country is referred to as "loss of citizenship." The process of losing citizenship, like the process of gaining citizenship, differs depending on where you live. Individuals who lost their citizenship, on the other hand, had one thing in common: they either did so voluntarily or were forced to do so forcefully without their consent.

**1. Voluntary loss of citizenship:** The act of giving up one's citizenship is referred to as "voluntary loss of citizenship." The decision taken by a person who went to loss his or her citizenship status freely is the main focus here. Almost every country has its own set of requirements for citizens who wish to formally renounce their citizenship.

#### Check-point (p.25)

1. Make a list of reasons why someone would voluntarily give up their citizenship and compare it to the list your partner is making next to you.

**Answer:** [open question] For example: to become a citizen of another country (by naturalization or marriage) where dual citizenship is not allowed; long-term work or residence abroad; joining family living in another country.
**Why:** The book defines voluntary loss as freely giving up one's citizenship (p.25) and later notes that acquiring another nationality voluntarily leads to loss of Ethiopian citizenship (p.30); the specific reasons are not listed in the book.

2. Do you know or have you heard of anyone in your neighborhood who has given up their citizenship voluntarily?

**Answer:** [open question] Personal answer; e.g. a relative who took another country's citizenship after living abroad.
**Why:** Based on the meaning of voluntary loss — a free decision to give up citizenship (p.25).

(p.26)

**2. Involuntary loss of citizenship:** Each state has its own set of rules governing citizenship. A citizen's citizenship may be revoked if he/she does not follow certain requirements. Of course, this might occur for a variety of reasons. However, losing one's citizenship involuntarily is not immediate; it must go through a series of steps in order to be revoked.

#### Check-point (p.26)

1. Make a list of plausible reasons why someone might be forced to loss their citizenship involuntarily, and compare it to your partner's list.

**Answer:** [open question] In some countries: obtaining citizenship by fraud or false information, serious crimes such as treason, or serving a foreign state against one's own country.
**Why:** The book only says citizenship "may be revoked if he/she does not follow certain requirements" and that this "might occur for a variety of reasons" (p.26); the examples are general knowledge, not from the book. Note that under Ethiopian law involuntary loss is not permitted (p.30).

2. Do you know or have you heard of anyone who has been forced to loss their citizenship, whether locally or nationally?

**Answer:** [open question] Personal answer. Students should note that in Ethiopia, no citizen may be stripped of citizenship against his or her will.
**Why:** p.30: Article 33(1) of the FDRE Constitution says no Ethiopian may be deprived of citizenship against his or her will, "This means that under Ethiopian law, involuntary citizenship loss is not permitted."

## 1.3 Citizenship Law in Ethiopia

*(Section 1.3 starts on book p.26.)*

At the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Examine ways of acquiring and losing Ethiopia citizenship;
- Explain the requirements for acquiring Ethiopian citizenship through naturalization;
- Recognize ways of losing citizenship in Ethiopia.

(p.27)

#### Start-up questions (p.27)

1. Are you an Ethiopian citizen? If you answered yes, how did you come to be an Ethiopian citizen?

**Answer:** [open question] Most students: Yes — by descent, because one or both of my parents are Ethiopian.
**Why:** p.27: under Article 6 of the FDRE Constitution, "any person of either gender who has both or either Ethiopian parents is an Ethiopian citizen"; Ethiopian nationality is inherited primarily by descent (p.28).

Ethiopia was enacted the first written citizenship law in 1930 G.C. Prior to it, citizenship was mostly defined by custom and tradition. The Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopian (FDRE) constitution of 1995 G.C. provides certain fundamental ideas about citizenship in Ethiopia at current moment.

According to Article 6 of the FDRE constitution, any person of either gender who has both or either Ethiopian parents is an Ethiopian citizen. As a result, the FDRE constitution recognizes the right to Ethiopian citizenship based on descent or blood.

A general proclamation on attaining Ethiopian citizenship through naturalization is also included in the FDRE constitution. The Ethiopian Nationality Proclamation No. 378/2003, on the other hand, specified the specifics of nationality.

(p.28)

[Figure 1.14: The Cover of Proclamation No. 378/2003 — front page of the Federal Negarit Gazeta of the FDRE (star emblem at top, Amharic and English headers, "10th Year No. 13, ADDIS ABABA-23rd December, 2003"), with Contents "Proclamation No. 378/2003 Ethiopian Nationality Proclamation … Page 2505" and the heading "PROCLAMATION NO. 378/2003. A PROCLAMATION ON ETHIOPIAN NATIONALITY".]

Ethiopian nationality is inherited primarily by descent, according to Proclamation No. 378/2003 on Ethiopian Nationality. In the case of Ethiopia, there are no broad rights to citizenship based on birthplace on the land. The Proclamation also offers measures for naturalization-based acquisition after birth. A foreigner seeking Ethiopian nationality must, however, meet the following requirements:

i. have reached the age of majority and be legally capable under Ethiopian law;

ii. have established his domicile in Ethiopia and lived in Ethiopia for a total of at least four years prior to the submission of his application;

(p.29)

iii. be able to communicate in any of the country's nations/nationalities' languages;

iv. have a sufficient and lawful source of income to support himself and his family;

v. be a person of good character;

vi. have no criminal record;

vii. be able to show that he has been released from his previous nationality or that acquiring Ethiopian nationality will result in such a release, or that he is a stateless person; and

viii. be required to take the oath of allegiance stated in Article 12 of the Proclamation.

The declaration authorizes naturalization in "exceptional cases" in addition to conventional procedures. Allowing citizenship as a manner of giving special recognition to foreigners who have made an exceptional contribution to the country appears to be launched by the government or the state. As a result, regardless of the qualifications specified above, a foreigner who has made a significant contribution in Ethiopia's interest may be granted Ethiopian citizenship by law.

> **REMEMBER**
> Foreigners who have made significant contributions to Ethiopia may be granted citizenship as a form of special recognition.

(p.30)

The modalities of loss of Ethiopian citizenship were also established in Proclamation No. 378/2003. According to the nationality proclamation, no Ethiopian may be stripped of his or her Ethiopian citizenship by a government decision unless he or she renounces his or her Ethiopian citizenship voluntarily or acquires another nationality in a method that the law considers voluntary. Article 33 (1) of the FDRE Constitution additionally declared that no Ethiopian national may be stripped of his or her Ethiopian citizenship against his or her choice. This means that under Ethiopian law, involuntary citizenship loss is not permitted.

#### Check-point (p.30)

Assume an Ethiopian mother and an American father have a child in France. Answer the following questions based on the Ethiopian citizenship law:

1. What do you think a baby's citizenship would be?

**Answer:** Under Ethiopian law the baby is an Ethiopian citizen by descent, because one parent (the mother) is Ethiopian; being born in France does not change this.
**Why:** p.27: Article 6 — a person "who has both or either Ethiopian parents is an Ethiopian citizen"; p.21: by descent, "regardless of where children are born, their citizenship is decided by their parents' nationality"; p.28: Ethiopia has no right-of-soil citizenship.

[review: the question is to be answered "based on the Ethiopian citizenship law", so the model answer covers only Ethiopian citizenship. The baby may also have US citizenship by descent from the father; French citizenship is not automatic from birth in France alone. Ethiopia's handling of dual nationality held from birth (Proclamation 378/2003) is not explained in the book — check before teaching beyond the book.]

2. Is the newborn eligible to become an Ethiopian citizen? If the answer is yes, how can the baby become an Ethiopian citizen? Why not, if the answer is no?

**Answer:** Yes. The baby becomes an Ethiopian citizen by descent (right of blood) through the Ethiopian mother — not by place of birth and not by naturalization.
**Why:** p.27: the FDRE Constitution "recognizes the right to Ethiopian citizenship based on descent or blood"; Article 6 needs only "either" parent to be Ethiopian; p.28: Ethiopian nationality "is inherited primarily by descent".

### Unit Summary

A citizen is a person who has specific rights and obligations under the laws of a country. Citizens are given certain rights and rewards by the government.

(p.31)

Citizens are expected to respect the regulations of their country in exchange for protecting it against its adversaries. Citizenship is both a legal status and a personal identity. Our family, community, friends, and teachers teach us about good citizenship.

A good citizen is someone who respects others, is helpful and considerate of others, listens to other people's opinions, helps people who can't help themselves, lives in harmony with other members of society, and is always open to learn. Students who practice good citizenship learn how to find common ground with those who are not like them. As a result, you must be a good citizen in order to make your school and community a better place for everyone.

Citizenship is a legal status earned by being a registered member of a country. Anyone can become a member of the state if they meet the legal conditions of the country. As a general rule, international law leaves it up to each state to define who its citizens are; as a result, citizenship laws in different countries handle the granting and loss of citizenship in different ways.

Ethiopia was a pioneer in African citizenship law, having enacted the first written citizenship law in 1930 G.C. At the moment, the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopian (FDRE) constitution of 1995 G.C. establishes some important principles governing citizenship in Ethiopia. According to Article 6 of the FDRE constitution, any person of either sex who has both or either parent who is Ethiopian is an Ethiopian citizen.

(p.32)

The FDRE constitution thus enshrines the concept of the right of descent or blood to gain Ethiopian citizenship.

### Glossary

- **Citizen:** is the person an individual who is legally recognized as a member of state.
- **Citizenship:** is the legal status of an individual member to be a citizen of particular state or it is the official recognition of an individual's integration in to the political system state
- **Citizenship law:** is the law of a sovereign state, and of each of its jurisdictions, which determines citizenship rights and responsibilities within the jurisdiction.
- **Constitution:** is the fundamental law of a nation or state that guarantees individual civil rights and liberties.
- **Patriotism:** is the quality of being, devotion to and vigorous for one country.
- **Responsibility:** the state or fact of having a duty to deal with something or of having control over someone.
- **Tolerance:** the ability or willingness to tolerate the existence of opinions or behavior that one dislikes or disagrees with.
