## Introduction

Physics begins with observations of phenomena, events, matter or energy. Through demanding and controlled experimentation and logical thought process, the physical phenomena are described quantitatively using mathematical tools. Any quantitative description of a property requires comparison with a scale of different measuring devises. For example, length needs a meter-stick, time needs a watch, and mass needs a beam balance. In this process, we recognize a very obvious fact that properties of different kinds cannot be compared. You cannot compare the time of travel from point A to B with the distance between the two points, although the two quantities may be related. The time of travel (time) is a physical quantity and the distance (length) is also a physical quantity. They are completely different types of physical quantities measured by different measuring devices and units. In this unit you will learn different types of scales, measurement, classification of physical quantities, and conversion from one system of units to another.

2. What is physical quantity? How can you measure mass, length and time? What are their units of measurement?

**Answer:** A physical quantity is a property of an object or phenomenon that can be measured and expressed as a number times a unit (e.g. 2.5 kg, 1.6 m, 30 s). Mass is measured with a beam balance (or electronic balance), length with a meter-stick/ruler/tape measure, time with a watch, clock or stop-watch. SI units: mass — kilogram (kg); length — metre (m); time — second (s).

**Why:**
- Introduction: "length needs a meter-stick, time needs a watch, and mass needs a beam balance."
- Measuring = comparing the unknown quantity with a standard unit of the same kind (Section 2.2).
- SI units are confirmed on p.15 (Exercise 2.3): m, kg, s.
- A measured value always has two parts: a number (magnitude) and a unit; e.g. "5" alone is meaningless, "5 m" is a length.

3. How can you classify physical quantities?

**Answer:** Two common ways: (1) **basic (fundamental) vs derived** quantities — the seven basic quantities are length, mass, time, temperature, electric current, amount of substance and luminous intensity; all others (area, volume, speed, density, force …) are derived from them; (2) **scalar vs vector** quantities — scalars have magnitude only (mass, time, temperature), vectors have magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, force).

**Why:**
- The book lists the seven basic quantities on p.14 and says "All other quantities in physics can be derived from these seven".
- Derived example: speed = length ÷ time → unit m/s; area = length × length → m².
- Scalar/vector classification is the other standard grouping taught later in this unit (Grade 9 syllabus).

## 2.1 Scales, Standards, Units (prefixes)

### Scales

### Ay the end of this section, you should be able to:

- list the four type of scales;
- distinguish between the four types of scales.

[review: heading typo "Ay the end of this section" → "At the end of this section" (same typo repeats on p.14 and p.17); also "four type of scales" → "four types of scales".]

In general there are four data measuring scales. These are nominal scales, ordinal scales, interval scales and ratio scales.

- **Nominal scales:** are used for labeling variables without any number value. For nominal scales there is no inherent quantitative difference among the categories. Examples are: gender (male, female), hair color (black, brown).
- **Ordinal scales:** are rank-order observations. For this type of scale, there is an underlying quantitative measurement on which the observations differ. Example: Your class rank in the previous grades.
- **Interval scales:** have a constant interval but lack a true zero point. For this type of scale, one can add and subtract values on an interval scale, but one cannot multiply or divide units. Temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit scale is an example of interval scale.
- **Ratio scales:** have the property of equal intervals but also have a true zero point. As a result, one can multiply and divide as well as add and subtract using ratio scales. Units of time (second, minute, hour), length (centimeter, meter, kilometer), weight (milligram, gram, kilogram), volume (centimeter cube) and temperature in Kelvin Scale are ratio scales.

[review: (1) milligram, gram, kilogram are units of **mass**, not weight (weight is a force, measured in newtons) — should read "mass (milligram, gram, kilogram)". (2) "centimeter cube" → "cubic centimetre (cm³)". (3) Interval scales: "cannot multiply or divide units" → "cannot meaningfully multiply or divide values" (e.g. 20 °C is not "twice as hot" as 10 °C).]

Note that even though a ratio scale has a true zero point, the nature of the variable is such that a value of zero will never be observed. For example human height is measured on a ratio scale but every human has a height greater than zero.

[review: this is true only for *some* ratio variables (like human height); many ratio quantities can be zero (a car's speed when parked is 0 m/s; the distance travelled before starting is 0 m; the stop-watch reads 0 s at the start). Better: "for some variables, a value of zero is never observed".]

[figure: Comparison of the four scales a lesson can draw as a table — Nominal: labels only, no order (gender, hair colour) | Ordinal: order/rank, gaps not equal (class rank) | Interval: equal gaps, no true zero, + and − only (°C, °F) | Ratio: equal gaps, true zero, + − × ÷ (m, kg, s, K).]

### Key Concept:

- In physics scale is a set of numbers, amounts, etc., used to measure or compare the level of something.
- Ratio scale is an advanced scale in which addition, subtraction, multiplication and division is possible.

### Activity 2.2

1. What is your weight in kilograms?

**Answer (model):** A typical Grade 9 student reads about 40–60 kg on a bathroom scale, e.g. 50 kg. Strictly, 50 kg is the student's **mass**; the **weight** is W = m × g = 50 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 490 N.

**Why:**
- Step on a bathroom/clinic scale and read the number in kg (e.g. 50 kg).
- The kilogram is the SI unit of mass (p.15), so the scale reading is a mass.
- Weight is the gravitational force on that mass: W = mg.
- Substitute: W = 50 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 490 kg·m/s² = 490 N.
- Check: 490 N ÷ 9.8 m/s² = 50 kg ✓.

[review: "weight in kilograms" mixes mass and weight — kilograms measure mass; weight is measured in newtons (N). Suggested wording: "What is your mass in kilograms?" (everyday language calls it "weight").]

2. Are scales involving division of two ratio scales also themselves ratio scales? Discuss in small groups.

**Answer:** Yes. Dividing one ratio-scale quantity by another gives a quantity that still has equal intervals and a true zero, so it is also a ratio scale (e.g. speed = distance ÷ time, density = mass ÷ volume).

**Why:**
- Ratio scale = equal intervals + true zero.
- Speed: distance (ratio, m) ÷ time (ratio, s) → m/s. A speed of 0 m/s really means "no motion" (true zero).
- Ratios make sense: 20 m/s is twice as fast as 10 m/s (20 ÷ 10 = 2).
- Density: mass (kg) ÷ volume (m³) → kg/m³; 2000 kg/m³ is twice 1000 kg/m³.
- Contrast: dividing interval values does not work (20 °C ÷ 10 °C = 2 has no physical meaning), which is why the result must come from ratio scales.

What is a standard in measurement? In your local area people measure volume, mass and area using different measuring devices. Do these measurements have standards? Discuss in groups.

### Scientific Notation

Scientific notation is a way of writing numbers that are too large or too small to be conveniently written as a decimal. This can be written more easily in scientific notation, in the general form:

d × 10^n

where d is a decimal number between 0 and 10 that is rounded off to a few decimal places; n is known as the exponent and is an integer. If n > 0 it represents how many times the decimal place in d should be moved to the right. If n < 0, then it represents how many times the decimal place in d should be moved to the left. For example, 3.24 × 10³ represents 3240 (the decimal moved three places to the right) and 3.24 × 10^(-3) presents 0.00324 (the decimal moved three places to the left).

[review: "d is a decimal number between 0 and 10" should be "1 ≤ d < 10" (d cannot be 0.5, e.g. 0.5 × 10³ is not scientific notation; write 5 × 10²). The book's own Key Concept on p.17 says "a number between 1 and 10". Also typo "presents" → "represents".]

[added step: worked examples written out — 3.24 × 10³ = 3.24 × 1000 = 3240 (point moves 3 places right: 3.24 → 32.4 → 324. → 3240.); 3.24 × 10^(-3) = 3.24 ÷ 1000 = 0.00324 (point moves 3 places left: 3.24 → 0.324 → 0.0324 → 0.00324).]

### Significant Figures

In a number, each non-zero digit is a significant figure. Zeroes are only counted if they are between two non-zero digits or are at the end of the decimal part. For example, the number 2000 has 1 significant figure (the 2), but 2000.0 has 5 significant figures. You estimate a number like this by removing significant figures from the number (starting from the right) until you have the desired number of significant figures, rounding as you go. For example, 6.827 has 4 significant figures, but if you wish to write it to 3 significant figures it would mean removing the 7 and rounding up, so it would be 6.83.

[added step: rounding 6.827 to 3 s.f. — keep the first three significant digits 6.82; the next digit is 7 ≥ 5, so round the last kept digit up: 2 → 3, giving 6.83. Check: 6.83 − 6.827 = 0.003 < 0.005 ✓.]

[added step: the book's rule leaves out leading zeros — zeros before the first non-zero digit (e.g. the zeros in 0.00324) are never significant; 0.00324 has 3 s.f. Trailing zeros in a whole number without a decimal point (2000, 7800) are treated as not significant by this book; writing 2.0 × 10³ shows 2 s.f. unambiguously.]

### Exercise 2.4

- Write 0.000001256 in scientific notation to 3 decimal places.

**Answer:** 1.256 × 10^(-6)

**Why:**
- Move the decimal point to the right until one non-zero digit is in front of it: 0.000001256 → 1.256.
- Count the moves: 0.000001256 → 0.00001256 (1) → 0.0001256 (2) → 0.001256 (3) → 0.01256 (4) → 0.1256 (5) → 1.256 (6). Six moves to the right → n = −6.
- d = 1.256 already has exactly 3 decimal places, so no rounding is needed.
- Result: 1.256 × 10^(-6).
- Check: 1.256 × 10^(-6) = 1.256 ÷ 1 000 000 = 0.000001256 ✓.

### Key ideas by subsection
- **Introduction:** physical quantities are measured by comparison with a scale/device (metre stick, watch, beam balance); quantities of different kinds (time vs length) cannot be compared.
- **2.1 Scales:** four scales — nominal (labels), ordinal (rank), interval (equal steps, no true zero: °C, °F), ratio (equal steps + true zero: m, kg, s, K); only ratio allows × and ÷. Physics uses ratio scales.

### Common student mistakes
- Calling kg a unit of weight (the book itself does this) — kg is mass; weight is in newtons (W = mg).
- Thinking 20 °C is "twice as hot" as 10 °C (interval scale; in kelvin the ratio is only ≈ 1.035).
- Calling cm, mm, km, g "non-SI" (book wording) — they are SI units with prefixes.
- Writing scientific notation with d outside 1–10 (e.g. 12.5 × 10³ or 0.56 × 10^(-2)).
- Getting the sign of the exponent backwards (small numbers have negative n).
- Counting leading zeros as significant (0.00324 has 3 s.f., not 6) or not knowing that 7800 has 2 s.f. but 7800.0 has 5.
- Rounding in steps (6.8249 → 6.825 → 6.83 is wrong; directly to 3 s.f. it is 6.82).
- Confusing m (milli, prefix) with m (metre, unit), and M (mega) with m (milli); typing "u" or "mu" for µ.
- Leaving a space between prefix and unit ("k m") or writing "Kg" instead of "kg".

**Answer:** A vernier caliper (or a micrometer screw gauge for a very small sphere such as a ball bearing).
**Why:**

3. A farmer wants to know the length of his plots of land in meter but he has only a long rope, a 50 cm ruler and a 6 m long stick. How can he easily measure the length of his plot? Discuss in groups.

**Answer:** Use the 6 m stick to mark off the rope in 6 m sections (or lay the stick end to end along the plot), count the whole sticks, and measure the left-over piece with the 50 cm ruler; length = (number of sticks × 6 m) + leftover.
**Why:**
- The 6 m stick is a known standard length; laying it end to end n times along the edge gives n × 6 m.

- Faster: stretch the rope along the plot, mark the rope's end, then measure the rope with the stick and ruler on flat ground.

Length is one of the fundamental (basic) physical quantities which describes the distance between two points.

Derived quantities are obtained by combining fundamental quantities through multiplication/division (area = length × length, speed = distance ÷ time, density = mass ÷ volume).
**Why:**
- There are only seven fundamental quantities, each with its own base unit (m, kg, s, K, A, mol, cd).
- A derived quantity's unit is built from base units, e.g. area: m × m = m²; speed: m ÷ s = m/s.

2. Some physical quantities have only magnitude. However, other physical quantities have both magnitude and direction. Can you mention some examples of these physical quantities?

**Answer:** Magnitude only (scalars): mass, time, length/distance, speed, temperature, energy, area, volume. Magnitude and direction (vectors): displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum.
**Why:**
- "5 kg" or "20 °C" is complete with a number and unit — no direction makes sense.
- "Push of 10 N" is incomplete: to the left or right gives different results, so force needs a direction (10 N east).
- Pairs to contrast: distance (scalar) vs displacement (vector); speed (scalar) vs velocity (vector).

#### Scalar and Vector Quantities

### Activity 2.13

*(margin box)*

1. Discuss in groups and classify the following physical quantities as scalar or vector quantity: mass, time, area, speed, velocity, acceleration, force, energy, work, pressure, momentum, electric current, current density, displacement, and temperature.

**Answer:**

| Scalar | Vector |
|---|---|
| mass, time, area, speed, energy, work, pressure, electric current, temperature | velocity, acceleration, force, momentum, current density, displacement |

**Why:**
- Scalars are complete with a number and unit: 5 kg, 10 s, 4 m², 20 m/s, 100 J, 50 J, 200 Pa, 2 A, 25 °C.
- Vectors need a direction: 20 m/s north (velocity), 3 m/s² downward (acceleration), 10 N to the left (force), momentum = mass × velocity (takes velocity's direction), 5 m east (displacement).
- Work = force × displacement is a product of two vectors but the result has no direction → scalar.
- Pressure acts equally in all directions at a point in a fluid → scalar.
- Electric current flows "in a direction" along a wire, but currents add like ordinary numbers (not by vector rules), so it is treated as a scalar; current density (current per area at a point) is a vector.

Physical quantities can also be classified as scalar and vector quantities. Some physical quantities are described completely by a number and a unit. A number with a unit is called a magnitude. However, other quantities have a direction attached to the magnitude. They cannot be described by a number and unit only. Thus, physical quantities are grouped into two. These are:

- Scalar quantities
- Vector quantities

A scalar quantity is a physical quantity which has only magnitude but no direction.

Examples are: distance, mass, time, temperature, energy etc.

A vector quantity is a physical quantity which has both magnitude and direction. When expressing that the car moves 50 km/h to east, this gives full information about the velocity of the car that includes magnitude and direction

(p.26)

(50 km/h is the magnitude, and east is the direction). Because of this, velocity is a vector quantity.

Examples are: displacement, acceleration, force, etc.

A vector can be represented either by a single letter in bold face or by a single letter with arrow head on it. For example: displacement can be represented as S⃗ (S with an arrow over it) or **S**.

> **Key Concept:**
> - **Physical quantity:** anything that you can measure and describe by a number and unit.
> - **Fundamental physical quantities:** physical quantities which can be measured directly.
> - **Derived physical quantities:** Physical quantities which depend on one or more fundamental quantities for their measurements.
> - **Scalar quantities:** Physical quantities that are described only by their magnitude.
> - **Vector quantities:** Physical quantities that are described by their magnitude and direction.

## Unit Summary

- Scale is a set of numbers, amounts etc., used to measure or compare the level of something.
- There are four types of measurement scales: nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio.
- In Physics, most of the scales are ratio scales.
- Measurement is the comparison of an unknown quantity with a known one (standard unit).
- Standard units are conventional units which are used to measure physical quantities scientifically.
- Traditional measuring units are not exact and have no a standard.
- Prefixes are used to simplify the description of physical quantities that are very big or very small.
- Quantities that can be measured directly or indirectly are known as physical quantities.
- Physical quantities are characteristics or properties of an object that can be measured or calculated from other measurements.
- Physical quantities are classified as fundamental /or basic physical quantities, and derived physical quantities.
- Length, time, mass, temperature, current, amount of substance and luminous intensity are fundamental quantities in science. All other physical quantities are derived physical quantities.
- Meter, second, kilogram, Kelvin, Ampere, mole and candela are fundamental (basic) units.
- Physical quantities can be categorized as vectors or scalars.

(p.31)

- Meter, kilogram and Second are the SI unit of length, mass, and time respectively.
- SI units can be converted to non SI units and vise versa.

### Part I. Multiple choice

1. Which one of the following scale allows addition, subtraction, multiplication and division?
(a) Nominal scale (b) ratio scale (c) ordinal scale (d) interval scale

**Answer:** (b) ratio scale
**Why:**
- A ratio scale has equal intervals AND a true zero (0 kg means no mass), so 4 kg is twice 2 kg — all four operations are meaningful.
- (a) Nominal: only names/labels (e.g. jersey numbers) — no arithmetic.
- (c) Ordinal: only order (1st, 2nd, 3rd) — gaps are not equal, so no arithmetic.
- (d) Interval: equal gaps allow + and −, but no true zero (0 °C is not "no temperature"), so 20 °C is not "twice as hot" as 10 °C — × and ÷ are not meaningful.

**Why:**

3. The SI standard of time is based on:

- 1 tonne = 1000 kg (Table 2.6) → 2 ton = 2 × 1000 kg = 2000 kg.

- (a) 221 kg takes 2 ton as 120 or 200 kg — wrong conversion; (c) 2011 kg uses 10 kg for B; (d) 2001 kg leaves out B's 100 kg.

[review: The book writes "ton"; Table 2.6 uses "tonne" (metric ton = 1000 kg). Use "tonne" for consistency.]

10. Why are fundamental physical quantities different from derived physical quantities?
(a) Fundamental physical quantities are derived from derived physical quantities.
(b) Derived physical quantities are derived from fundamental physical quantities.
(c) Derived and fundamental physical quantities have no relation.
(d) All are answers

**Answer:** (b) Derived physical quantities are derived from fundamental physical quantities.
**Why:**
- E.g. speed = length ÷ time; density = mass ÷ length³ — derived ones are built from fundamental ones; fundamental ones are not built from anything else.
- (a) is the reverse of the truth.
- (c) is false — derived quantities depend on fundamental ones.
- (d) cannot be right because (a) and (c) are false.

11. Which of the following would describe a length that is 2.0 × 10^(-3) of a meter?
(a) 2.0 km (b) 2.0 cm (c) 2.0 mm (d) 2.0 µm

**Answer:** (c) 2.0 mm
**Why:**
- milli (m) = 10^(-3), so 2.0 × 10^(-3) m = 2.0 mm (= 0.0020 m).
- (a) 2.0 km = 2.0 × 10³ m.
- (b) 2.0 cm = 2.0 × 10^(-2) m.
- (d) 2.0 µm = 2.0 × 10^(-6) m.

12. Which quantity is a vector?
(a) Energy (b) force (c) speed (d) time

**Answer:** (b) force
**Why:**
- A force has a size and a direction (e.g. 10 N downward); pushing the other way gives a different effect.
- (a) energy, (c) speed and (d) time have magnitude only — scalars.

13. Which one of the following lists is a set of scalar quantities?
(a) length, force, time
(b) length, mass, time
(c) length, force, acceleration
(d) length, force, mass

**Answer:** (b) length, mass, time
**Why:**
- Length (m), mass (kg) and time (s) need only a number and a unit.
- (a), (c) and (d) each contain force, a vector; (c) also contains acceleration, a vector.

### Part II: Write true if the statement is correct and false if the statement is wrong.

1. Second is a device used to measure time.

**Answer:** False
**Why:**
- The second (s) is the SI unit of time, not a device.
- Devices that measure time are clocks, watches and stopwatches.
- Corrected statement: "Second is the SI unit of time; a stopwatch is a device used to measure time."

2. Candela is a derived physical quantity.

**Answer:** False
**Why:**
- The candela (cd) is not a quantity at all — it is a unit.
- It is the fundamental (base) unit of luminous intensity, one of the seven base quantities in Table 2.3.
- Corrected statement: "Candela is the fundamental unit of luminous intensity."

(p.33)

3. The unit of force can be derived from the units of mass, length and time.

**Answer:** True
**Why:**
- F = m × a; unit = kg × m/s² = kg·m/s², called the newton (N) (Table 2.4).
- It uses only kg (mass), m (length) and s (time).

4. One kilometer is 100 meter.

**Answer:** False
**Why:**
- kilo = 10³ = 1000, so 1 km = 1000 m (Table 2.5).
- 100 m = 0.1 km (one hectometre).

5. For a very large or very small numbers prefixes are used with SI units.

**Answer:** True
**Why:**
- Prefixes replace powers of ten: e.g. 384 000 000 m = 384 Mm (or 3.84 × 10⁵ km); 0.000 001 s = 1 µs.
- The unit summary says the same: prefixes simplify very big or very small quantities.

6. Scalar quantity can be described by its magnitude and direction.

**Answer:** False
**Why:**
- A scalar has magnitude only (e.g. 5 kg, 20 s).
- A quantity described by magnitude and direction is a vector (e.g. 5 m east).

### Part III: Short answer questions

1. What is the difference between interval scale and ratio scale?

**Answer:** Both have ordered values with equal intervals, but a ratio scale has a true (absolute) zero, while an interval scale has an arbitrary zero. So on a ratio scale you can say "twice as much" (multiply/divide); on an interval scale you can only add and subtract.
**Why:**
- Interval example: temperature in °C — 0 °C does not mean "no temperature"; 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C.
- Ratio examples: length, mass, time, temperature in kelvin — 0 kg means no mass, and 4 kg is exactly twice 2 kg.

2. How many seconds are there in 12 hours?

**Answer:** 43 200 s (4.32 × 10⁴ s)
**Why:**
- 1 h = 60 min × 60 s/min = 3600 s.
- 12 h × 3600 s/h = 43 200 s.
- Check: 12 h = 720 min; 720 × 60 = 43 200 s ✔ (and it is half of 86 400 s in a day ✔).

3. What is measurement?

**Answer:** Measurement is the comparison of an unknown quantity with a known standard quantity of the same kind (the unit). The result is a number and a unit, e.g. 3 m.
**Why:**
- The three key elements: the physical quantity, the measuring tool, and the standard unit.
- The number tells how many units are in the quantity.

4. What is the difference between traditional measuring units and scientific measuring units?

**Answer:** Traditional units (e.g. hand span *sinzir*, cubit *kind*, foot length, *tassa*, *kuna*, shadow position) are based on body parts or local objects, differ from person to person and place to place, are not exact and have no standard. Scientific (standard) units (m, kg, s …) are fixed, internationally agreed, the same everywhere and reproducible, so measurements can be compared and are accurate.
**Why:**
- One person's arm length ≠ another's, so "5 cubits" gives different lengths.
- 1 m is the same in Addis Ababa, Messina or Tokyo, so trade and science can share results.

5. Define the following terms:
a) Meter b) second c) kilogram d) length e) time f) mass g) Physical quantity h) derived physical quantity i) fundamental physical quantity. j) scalar physical quantity k) vector physical quantity

**Answer:**
- a) Meter (m): the SI unit of length; the distance light travels in vacuum in 1/299 792 458 of a second.
- b) Second (s): the SI unit of time; the duration of 9 192 631 770 periods of the radiation from a caesium-133 atom.
- c) Kilogram (kg): the SI unit of mass; originally the mass of the international prototype cylinder kept near Paris (since 2019 defined by fixing the value of the Planck constant).
- d) Length: the fundamental physical quantity that describes the distance between two points.
- e) Time: the fundamental physical quantity that describes the duration between the beginning and end of an event.
- f) Mass: the amount of matter contained in a body.
- g) Physical quantity: anything that can be measured and described by a number and a unit.
- h) Derived physical quantity: a quantity that depends on one or more fundamental quantities for its measurement (e.g. speed, area, density).
- i) Fundamental physical quantity: a quantity that can be measured directly and is not defined from other quantities (the seven in Table 2.3).
- j) Scalar physical quantity: a quantity described by magnitude only (e.g. mass, time).
- k) Vector physical quantity: a quantity described by magnitude and direction (e.g. displacement, force).
**Why:**
- d)–k) use the book's own definitions on pp. 20–26.
- a)–c) are the SI definitions; at Grade 9 level it is enough to say "the SI unit of length/time/mass" plus the standard it is based on.

6. Which SI units would you use for the following measurements?
(a) the length of a swimming pool
(b) the mass of the water in the pool
(c) the time it takes a swimmer to swim a lap

**Answer:** (a) metre (m) (b) kilogram (kg) (c) second (s)
**Why:**
- (a) A pool is about 25 m or 50 m long — a convenient number in metres.
- (b) Mass is always in kg in SI; a 50 m × 25 m × 2 m pool holds 2500 m³ of water ≈ 2 500 000 kg = 2.5 × 10⁶ kg (1 m³ of water ≈ 1000 kg), so it could also be written 2500 tonnes.
- (c) A lap takes tens of seconds (e.g. 30 s), measured with a stopwatch.

7. Which instrument is used to measure the thickness of a sheet metal?

**Answer:** A micrometer screw gauge (a vernier caliper can also be used for thicker sheets).
**Why:**
- Sheet metal is often 0.5–2 mm thick; a ruler (smallest division 1 mm) is not precise enough.
- A micrometer screw gauge reads to 0.01 mm; a vernier caliper to about 0.1 mm.

8. Write some safety rules.

**Answer:** Always wear safety goggles; never work alone in the lab; read about the experiment before entering; do not eat, drink or chew gum in the lab; never taste or touch chemicals; report all accidents to the teacher immediately; be careful with electrical equipment; do only experiments authorized by the teacher; wash hands after lab work.
**Why:**
- These are the rules listed in the Laboratory Safety rules box on pp. 22–23.
- They protect eyes, skin and body from chemicals, heat, sharp glass and electric shock.

9. Give three examples of scalar and vector quantities.

**Answer:** Scalars: mass, time, temperature (also distance, speed, energy). Vectors: displacement, velocity, force (also acceleration, momentum).
**Why:**
- Scalars need only a number and unit: 5 kg, 10 s, 25 °C.
- Vectors also need a direction: 5 m north, 20 m/s east, 10 N downward.

### Part IV: Workout problems

1. In one of the pans of the beam balance the masses 3 kg, 900 g, 90 g and 5 g are placed. What amount of mass should be placed on the other side of the beam balance to make it balanced?

**Answer:** 3995 g = 3.995 kg
**Why:**
- A beam balance is level when the masses on both pans are equal, so the unknown = sum of the masses.
- Convert to one unit: 3 kg × 1000 g/kg = 3000 g.
- Sum: 3000 g + 900 g + 90 g + 5 g = 3995 g.
- In kg: 3995 g ÷ 1000 = 3.995 kg.
- Check in kg: 3 + 0.9 + 0.09 + 0.005 = 3.995 kg ✔.

(p.34)

2. For each of the following symbols, write out the unit in full and write what power of 10 it represents: (a) micro g (b) mg

[review: "micro g" should be printed as the symbol µg (the Greek letter mu); the question asks to write symbols out in full, so the symbol itself should appear.]

**Answer:** (a) µg = microgram = 10^(-6) g (b) mg = milligram = 10^(-3) g
**Why:**
- Prefix micro (µ) = 10^(-6): 1 µg = 0.000 001 g = 10^(-6) g = 10^(-9) kg.
- Prefix milli (m) = 10^(-3): 1 mg = 0.001 g = 10^(-3) g = 10^(-6) kg (Table 2.6: 1 mg = 0.001 g ✔).
- Check: 1 mg = 1000 µg, and 10^(-3) ÷ 10^(-6) = 10³ ✔.

3. The doctor wants to know the age of his patient and asks him how old he is. The patient replies that he is 25 ½ years old. What is the age of the patient in month?

**Answer:** 306 months
**Why:**
- 1 year = 12 months.
- 25 ½ years = 25.5 years.
- 25.5 × 12 = 306 months.
- Check: 25 × 12 = 300 months; ½ year = 6 months; 300 + 6 = 306 ✔.

4. The student wants to measure the length of the classroom using a tape meter. The tape meter reads 8m and 40 cm. What is the length of the classroom in cm?

**Answer:** 840 cm
**Why:**
- 1 m = 100 cm → 8 m = 8 × 100 cm = 800 cm.
- Total = 800 cm + 40 cm = 840 cm.
- Check: 8 m 40 cm = 8.40 m; 8.40 m × 100 cm/m = 840 cm ✔.

5. How many minutes are there in 3 days?

**Answer:** 4320 min
**Why:**
- 1 day = 24 h; 1 h = 60 min → 1 day = 24 × 60 = 1440 min.
- 3 days × 1440 min/day = 4320 min.
- Check: 3 × 24 h = 72 h; 72 × 60 = 4320 min ✔.

6. If the area of a single ceramic is 0.25 m², how many ceramics are used to cover a floor of a classroom whose area is 40 m²?

**Answer:** 160 ceramic tiles
**Why:**
- Number of tiles = floor area ÷ area of one tile.
- N = 40 m² ÷ 0.25 m² = 160 (the units m² cancel — the answer is a pure number).
- 0.25 = ¼, so dividing by 0.25 is multiplying by 4: 40 × 4 = 160.
- Check: 160 × 0.25 m² = 40 m² ✔.

7. The distance between Sun and the Earth is about 1.5 × 10¹¹ m. Express this distance using prefix.

**Answer:** 150 Gm (150 gigametres), equivalently 1.5 × 10⁸ km.
**Why:**
- giga (G) = 10⁹.
- 1.5 × 10¹¹ m = 1.5 × 10² × 10⁹ m = 150 × 10⁹ m = 150 Gm.
- With kilo (10³): 1.5 × 10¹¹ m ÷ 10³ = 1.5 × 10⁸ km = 150 000 000 km.
- Check: 150 × 10⁹ = 1.5 × 10² × 10⁹ = 1.5 × 10¹¹ ✔. (0.15 Tm, with tera = 10¹², is also correct.)

8. The volume of the Earth is on the order of 10²¹ m³. (a) What is this in cubic kilometers (km³)? (c) What is it in cubic centimeters (cm³)?

[review: The parts are labelled (a) and (c); there is no (b). The second part should be (b).]

**Answer:** (a) 10¹² km³ (b/c) 10²⁷ cm³
**Why:**
- (a) 1 km = 10³ m → 1 km³ = (10³ m)³ = 10⁹ m³. So 10²¹ m³ ÷ 10⁹ m³/km³ = 10^(21−9) km³ = 10¹² km³.
- (c) 1 m = 10² cm → 1 m³ = (10² cm)³ = 10⁶ cm³. So 10²¹ m³ × 10⁶ cm³/m³ = 10^(21+6) cm³ = 10²⁷ cm³.
- Common mistake: using 1 km = 10³ m directly for volume (giving 10¹⁸) — the conversion factor must be cubed.
- Check: 1 km³ = 10⁹ m³ × 10⁶ cm³/m³ = 10¹⁵ cm³; 10¹² km³ × 10¹⁵ = 10²⁷ cm³ ✔. (Real value ≈ 1.08 × 10²¹ m³ ≈ 1.08 × 10¹² km³.)

9. For each of the following, write the measurement using the correct symbol for the prefix and the base unit: (a) 101 nanoseconds (b) 10 milligrams (c) 72 gigameters.

**Answer:** (a) 101 ns (b) 10 mg (c) 72 Gm
**Why:**
- (a) nano → n, second → s: 101 ns (= 1.01 × 10^(-7) s).
- (b) milli → m, gram → g: 10 mg (= 10^(-2) g = 10^(-5) kg).
- (c) giga → capital G, metre → m: 72 Gm (= 7.2 × 10¹⁰ m).
- Case matters: "mg" is milligram, "Mg" would be megagram; "gm" is not a valid symbol for gigametre.

## Notes for lesson design

### Key ideas by subsection
- **2.2 Measurement (continued), pp.19–22:** measurement = comparing with a standard unit; result = number + unit. Length: distance between two points, SI unit m, tools: ruler, tape, vernier caliper, micrometer. Mass: amount of matter, SI unit kg, tools: beam balance, electronic balance. Time: duration of an event, SI unit s, tools: stopwatch, clocks; traditional sun/shadow methods are not standard.
- **Laboratory safety rules, pp.22–23:** goggles, no working alone, no eating/tasting, report accidents, care with electricity, only authorised experiments, wash hands.
- **2.3 Classification, pp.23–26:** 7 fundamental quantities and base units (l–m, m–kg, t–s, T–K, I–A, n–mol, I_v–cd); derived quantities/units built from them (Table 2.4: speed m/s, density kg/m³, acceleration m/s², force N = kg·m/s², work J = kg·m²/s², pressure Pa = kg/(m·s²)); scalars (magnitude only) vs vectors (magnitude + direction); vector notation S⃗ or **S**.
- **2.4 Unit conversion, pp.26–30:** multiply by a conversion factor written as a fraction equal to 1 so the old unit cancels (Tables 2.5 length, 2.6 mass, 2.7 time); convert everything to one unit before adding (Example 2.2); 1 h = 3600 s, 1 day = 86 400 s.
- **Unit summary & end-of-unit questions, pp.30–34:** measurement scales (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio), SI prefixes (n, µ, m, k, G), area/volume conversions need the factor squared/cubed.

### Common student mistakes
- Calling cm, mm, km, g "non-SI units" (the book itself does this) — they are SI units with prefixes.
- Dividing instead of multiplying (or vice versa) when converting; not writing units so they cannot see what cancels.
- Adding numbers with different units (1.5 kg + 500 g = 501.5) instead of converting first.
- Treating decimal hours as h:min (3.2 h read as 3 h 20 min instead of 3 h 12 min).
- Using the linear factor for areas/volumes (1 km³ = 10³ m³ instead of 10⁹ m³).
- Confusing a unit with a device or a quantity ("second is a device", "candela is a quantity").
- Mixing prefix case: mg vs Mg, Gm vs gm; reading "µ" as "m".
- Thinking anything that "goes somewhere" is a vector (electric current) or that work is a vector because force and displacement are.
- Saying "3 is the magnitude" (the book's wording) rather than "3 m is the magnitude".
