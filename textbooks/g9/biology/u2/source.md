## 2.1 Characteristics of living things

**Objectives**

At the end of this section, the student will be able to:
- *state the characteristics of living things.*

Living things have variety of shapes and forms. Thus, biologists study life in many different ways. Biologists often live with wildlife, collect fossils, or listen to whales. For example, they count how many times a hummingbird's wings beat per second. What makes something "alive"? Anyone could deduce that a galloping horse is alive and a car is not, but why? We cannot say, "If it moves, it's alive" because a car can move, and gelatin can wiggle in a bowl. They certainly are not alive. Although we cannot define life with a single sentence, we can come up with a series of characteristics shared by living systems.

### Activity 2.1: Reflective Discussion

If somebody places an object in front of you and asks you whether it is alive, what would you do?
Would you poke it to see if it reacts?
Would you watch it closely to see if it moves or breathes?
Would you dissect it to look at its parts?

**Answer:** Check it against several characteristics of life, not just one: does it respond to a stimulus (poke it), does it breathe/use energy, grow, take in food and give out wastes, and is it made of cells (look at a small part under a microscope). Dissecting is not needed and would kill it if it is alive.

**Why:**
- No single test is enough: a car moves and a crystal grows, but neither is alive (book: "We cannot say 'If it moves, it's alive'").
- Poking tests *response to stimuli*; watching tests *movement* and *breathing (respiration)*; looking at parts tests *cellular organisation*.
- Something is judged alive only if it shows all (or most) of the characteristics together — the list in section 2.1.
- Observing gently (watching, using a hand lens or microscope on a small sample) is better than dissecting, which destroys the organism.

*What are the characteristics of living things?*

Some of the properties that are shared by all living things are listed below:

**All living things are made up of one or more cells:** Those made up of one cell, such as bacteria are termed 'unicellular' and those made up of more than one cell, such as plants are termed 'multi-cellular'.

**All living things require energy:** All organisms use a source of energy for their metabolic activities. For example, every muscle in your body is powered by the energy you obtain from your diet. Some organisms use energy from the sunlight to make their foods through the process of photosynthesis. Such organisms, for example plants, are known as **producers or autotrophs**. Other organisms cannot make their own food but consume others. Such organisms are known as consumers or heterotrophs.

**All living organisms respond to stimuli:** organisms can detect or sense stimuli (change) in the internal or external environment and make appropriate responses.

**All living things can grow:** Growth is a permanent increase in size and mass due to an increase in cell number or cell size or both. Even bacteria and single-celled creatures show an increase in size. Multicellular organisms which increase the number of cells in their bodies become more complicated and change their shape and size.

**All living things can reproduce:** Reproduction is the process that makes more of the same kind of organism. Single-celled organisms may simply keep dividing into two. However, multicellular plants and animals may reproduce sexually or asexually.

**All living things can excrete:** Excretion is the removal of the metabolic wastes produced in cells as a result of chemical reactions (**metabolism**). For example, respiration and other chemical reactions in the cells produce waste products such as carbon dioxide. Living organisms expel such substances from their bodies in various ways.

**All living things display ordered complexity:** All living things are both complex and highly ordered. The levels of organization in biological systems begin with atoms and molecules and increase in

(p.21)

complexity. Your body is composed of many different kinds of cells each containing many complex molecular structures. Many nonliving things may also be complex, but they do not exhibit this degree of ordered complexity.

**Most living things maintain homeostasis:** Most organisms maintain relatively constant internal conditions that are different from their environment. Homeostasis is the regulation of an organism's internal conditions to maintain stability. For example, your body temperature remains stable despite changes in outside temperatures.

**All living things possess adaptations that evolve overtime:** All organisms interact with other organisms and their environment in ways that influence their survival, and as a result, organisms evolve adaptations to their environments (Fig.2.1).

1. Is it possible to define life in a simple sentence? If your answer is no, why not? Give a hort explanation.

**Answer:** No. Life cannot be captured in one simple sentence; it is described by a set of characteristics that all living things show together.

**Why:**
- Any single property (movement, growth, using energy) is also shown by some non-living things — a car moves, gelatin wiggles, crystals grow, fire "uses fuel".
- Some living things do not show every property at every moment (a seed is dormant; a mule cannot reproduce), so a one-feature definition would wrongly exclude them.
- So biologists list characteristics: cells, energy/metabolism, response to stimuli, growth, reproduction, excretion, ordered complexity, homeostasis and adaptation.

2. How are living things different from the non-living things?

**Answer:** Living things are made of one or more cells and carry out life processes — they use energy (metabolism), respond to stimuli, grow, reproduce, excrete wastes, show ordered complexity, maintain homeostasis and evolve adaptations. Non-living things are not made of cells and do not show these characteristics together.

**Why:**
- The difference is the *combination* of characteristics (Figure 2.1), not any single one.
- Example: a goat feeds, breathes, grows, gives birth and keeps a steady body temperature; a stone does none of these.
- A non-living object may show one feature by chance (a river moves, a crystal grows) but never the whole set.

**Why:**

3. Discuss the advantages of being organized.

**Answer:** Being organized saves time (things are easy to find), reduces loss and damage, makes it easy to see what you have and what is missing, makes it easy to add new items in the right place, and helps you understand how items are related.

**Why:**
- These are the same advantages classification gives biology: easy identification, easy study of huge numbers of organisms, a place for newly discovered species, and showing relationships between groups.

*Why do biologists classify living things?*

Organizing items not only makes them easier to find but can also make them easier to understand. One tool biologists use to organize and understand living organisms is classification. Classification is the process of grouping things based on their similarities. The science of naming, identifying and classifying organisms is known as taxonomy. Scientists who study taxonomy are called taxonomists. Biologists classify organisms into different categories mostly by judging the degrees of their apparent similarities and differences. These include the external and internal structures of the organism as well as where the organism lives. Taxonomists also consider the genetic makeup of organisms to reveal their evolutionary relationships to other organisms. The assumption is that the greater the degree of physical

(p.22)

similarity between them, the closer their biological relationship is. They try to identify and classify organisms based on a number of features (e.g., morphological, physiological, molecular, behavioural, and/or ecological characters).

### Key Terms

- **Identification**: identifying organisms using characteristic feature
- **Nomenclature**: Aspect of taxonomy that deals specifically with the naming of organisms
- **Taxonomy**: The science of naming and classifying species.

### Activity 2.4: Problem solving

Read a book or search in the internet on biological classification. Why do biologists need to organize living things in hierarchies?

**Answer:** Because there are millions of kinds of organisms; a hierarchy (kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species) puts them into groups within groups so that they are easy to identify, name and study, and so that the levels show how closely organisms are related.

**Why:**
- Each higher level groups several lower ones by broader shared features (e.g. all mammals share hair and milk; all chordates share a notochord/backbone line).
- Knowing an organism's place in the hierarchy tells you many of its features at once (a lion is in class Mammalia → it has hair and feeds young on milk).
- The fewer levels two organisms share, the more distantly related they are; organisms in the same genus are very close relatives.
- A new species can be fitted into an existing group without changing the whole system.

### 2.2.2. Taxonomic hierarchies in biological classification

**Objectives**

At the end of this section, the student will be able to:
- *At the end of this section, the student will be able to:*
- *describe taxonomic hierarchies*

Thousands of years ago, the Greek philosopher **Aristotle** (384-322 BC) developed the first widely accepted biological classification systems. He used simple morphological characters to classify plants into trees, shrubs and herbs. He also divided animals into two groups -those which had red blood and those that did not have. Though it was useful for a while, Aristotle grouped some organisms that had very little in common. For example, he grouped birds, bats, and flying insects because they could fly. Later on in the 1700s, a Swedish Botanist **Carolus Linnaeus** (1707–1778), who is also known as the father of taxonomy, introduced a taxonomic hierarchy of classification. He was the first person to propose an orderly system for classifying organisms.

[review: "Thousands of years ago" — Aristotle lived about 2,300–2,400 years ago, so "more than two thousand years ago" is more precise. The trees/shrubs/herbs grouping of plants is usually credited to Aristotle's student Theophrastus; keep the book's wording but lessons may mention this.]

Taxonomic hierarchy is the process of arranging various organisms into successive levels of the biological classification either in a decreasing or an increasing order. In the Linnaean classification system, all organisms are placed in a ranked hierarchy. The kingdom is ranked the highest followed by Phylum (division), class, order, family, genus, and species (Fig. 2.2). Each rank in a taxonomic hierarchy is termed taxon (plural, taxa). Linnaeus' developed a two Kingdom system of classification. He classified all living organisms under kingdoms Plantae and Animalia that included all plants and animals, respectively. This system did not distinguish between the eukaryotes and prokaryotes, unicellular and multicellular organisms; and photosynthetic (green algae) and non-photosynthetic (fungi) organisms.

[review: strictly, a *taxon* is a named group at any rank (e.g. "Mammalia", "Felidae"), while the level itself (class, family) is a *rank* or taxonomic category; the book uses "taxon" for the rank. Also "Linnaeus' developed" → "Linnaeus developed".]

On the broadest level, biologists divide the diversity of life into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya. Every organism on Earth belongs to one of these three domains. The first two domains, Bacteria and Archaea, identify two very different groups of organisms that have prokaryotic cells, relatively small and simple cells that lack a nucleus or other compartments bounded by internal membranes. Eukaryotes

(p.23)

have relatively large and complex cells that contain a nucleus and other membrane-enclosed compartments. They are grouped into the domain Eukarya. The domaineukarya includes groups such protists, fungi, plants and animals. Domain is the rank above kingdom.

Domain → Kingdom → Phylum → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species

Eukarya → Animalia → Chordata → Mammalia → Pimate → Hominidae → Homo → Homo sapiens

[review: "Pimate" → "Primates" (order Primates); "domaineukarya" → "domain Eukarya"; "groups such protists" → "groups such as protists".]

### Key Terms

- **Archaea**: is a group of single-celled organisms that lack a nucleus but are more closely related to eukaryotes than to bacteria.
- **Bacteria**: is the most diverse and well-known group of single-celled organisms that lack a nucleus.
- **Classes**: is a taxon of similar orders.
- **Eukaryotic cells**: are single-celled organisms with a nucleus.
- **Family**: is a taxon of similar genera.
- **Genera**: is a taxon of similar species.
- **Kingdoms**: is a taxon of similar phyla (plural for phylum).
- **Orders**: is a taxon of similar families.
- **Phylum**: is a taxon of similar classes. (Plant taxonomists use the taxon division instead of phylum).
- **Prokaryotic cells**: are unicellular organisms without a separate nucleus.
- **Species**: unique type of organism
- **Taxon**: is a group of organisms that share a unique setof traits.

**Answer:** Taxonomic hierarchy is the arrangement of organisms into successive ranked levels of classification (in decreasing or increasing order), from the broadest group to the narrowest: domain → kingdom → phylum (division) → class → order → family → genus → species.

### Activity 2.5: Investigating

1. Why and how do human beings classify organisms?

**Answer:** Why — to make the huge variety of living things easy to identify, name, study and talk about, to show how organisms are related, and for practical uses (finding food, medicine, timber; recognising pests, poisonous plants and disease carriers). How — by grouping organisms on shared features (body structure/morphology, internal structure, physiology, behaviour, habitat/ecology and genetic/DNA makeup) into a ranked hierarchy from kingdom to species, and naming each species with a two-part (binomial) Latin name.

**Why:**
- Classification is grouping by similarity (section 2.2.1); the more features shared, the closer the relationship.
- Everyday people also classify (edible vs poisonous plants, wild vs domestic animals); scientists use many characters and a fixed set of ranks so that everyone uses the same system.

*What is the relevance of biological classification?*

Classification gives biologists a framework that allows them to study the relationships between living (extant) and extinct organisms. For example, this framework allows biologists to study the relationship between birds and dinosaurs. Biologists have found that the bones of some dinosaurs have large internal spaces. So do the bones of birds. Because of these findings, some biologists believe that dinosaurs are more closely related to birds than to reptiles.

[review: dinosaurs *are* reptiles, so the last sentence is muddled. The intended idea is: "some (theropod) dinosaurs are more closely related to birds than to other living reptiles such as lizards and crocodiles — birds are thought to have evolved from dinosaurs."]

Taxonomy can be a useful tool for scientists who work in such areas as agriculture, forestry, and medicine. Taxonomy can also help the economy. For example, taxonomists can discover new sources of lumber, foods, medicines, and energy. For example, a taxonomist might know that a certain species of tree contains chemicals that make good disinfectants (e.g., Shiferaw/Moringa). It is possible that a closely related plant species could have the same useful substances. So instead of having one source of chemicals, there may be two or more sources.

### Activity 2.6: Inquiring and researching

[figure: Portrait of Carl Linnaeus — an elderly man in an 18th-century powdered wig and dark blue coat with a medal (decorative).]

Did you know Carl Linnaeus?
List the contribution of Carl Linnaeus to the Science of taxonomy.

**Answer:** Carl Linnaeus (1707–1778), Swedish naturalist and "father of taxonomy": (1) introduced the binomial (two-name) system of naming species, e.g. *Homo sapiens*, *Zea mays*; (2) introduced a ranked taxonomic hierarchy (kingdom, class, order, genus, species); (3) proposed the two-kingdom system (Plantae and Animalia); (4) published *Systema Naturae* and described and named thousands of plant and animal species; (5) used Latin names so that every scientist in the world uses the same name.

**Why:**
- All five points come from sections 2.2.2 and 2.4 of the book.
- His binomial system is still the official starting point for naming organisms today.
- Phylum and family were added to his ranks later, and domain much later.

*How did Carl Linnaeus classify living organisms?*

Linnaeus was a Swedish naturalist who initially graduated in medicine but became interested in plants. He travelled in Scandinavia, England and Eastern Europe, discovering and naming new plant species. In 1735 he published his *Systema Naturae*, in which he accurately described 7700 plant species and classified them. He further grouped species into genera, genera into classes, and classes into orders. He also classified over 4000 animals, but rather less successfully into mammals, birds, insects and worms.

[review: (1) The 1735 first edition of *Systema Naturae* was a short outline; the figures of about 7,700 plant species and 4,400 animal species belong to the 10th edition (1758). (2) The order of grouping is reversed: Linnaeus grouped species into genera, genera into **orders**, and orders into **classes** (class is above order, as in Figure 2.2). (3) Linnaeus' animal classes were six: mammals, birds, amphibians (incl. reptiles), fishes, insects and worms. (4) His travels were mainly in Sweden/Lapland, the Netherlands, England and France rather than Eastern Europe.]

Linnaeus refined and popularized the **binomial system** of naming organisms, in which the first name represents the genus and the second name the specific epithet. This system is still the official starting point for naming or revising the names of organisms.

**Binomial nomenclature**

Species must be named in such a way that the name is recognized all over the world. For example, maize is named *Bekkolo* in Amharic, *Boqqollo* in Afan Oromo and *Baddela* in Sidamu Affo and corn in American English etc. If you are not aware of these names, it could lead to confusion. If the botanical name, *Zea mays*, is used, however, there is no chance of error. As you all might know our country is endowed with cultural and linguistic diversities and each would have different names for the same species. As a result, it would be difficult for a person to know the names given by the 80 plus ethnolinguistic groups in Ethiopia. Similarly, there are over thousands of languages in the African continent and beyond.

Therefore, the Latin form of the name allows it to be used in all over the world irrespective of language barriers. The similarity in size, shape, behavior and colormakes it difficult to differentiate one species from the other. Again, the use of the scientific name avoids such confusion.

The **binomial system of** naming species (introduced by Linnaeus) is an internationally agreed system in which the scientific name of an organism is made up of two parts showing the genus and the species. Binomial means 'two names'; the first name gives the **genus** and the

(p.26)

second gives the specific epithet. For example, Enset, false banana, *Ensete ventricosum* and Banana, *Musa acuminate*, belong to the same family (Musaceae).In writing a scientific name, the first letter of name of the genus is a capitalized letter and the specific epithet always starts with a small letter, for example, the scientific name of human beings is *Homo sapiens.* The scientific names are underlined when handwritten or italicized when printed.

[review: "*Musa acuminate*" → "*Musa acuminata*" (correct spelling of the wild banana species).]

### Self Assessment

1. What is a binomial nomenclature?

**Answer:** Binomial nomenclature is the internationally agreed system (introduced by Linnaeus) of giving every species a two-part Latin scientific name: the first part is the genus (written with a capital letter) and the second is the specific epithet (written with a small letter), e.g. *Homo sapiens*, *Zea mays*. The name is italicized when printed and underlined when handwritten.

**Why:**
- "Binomial" means "two names".
- One universal name avoids confusion from many local names (maize = Bekkolo, Boqqollo, Baddela, corn).
- Two species in the same genus share the first name (e.g. *Felis catus*, *Felis silvestris*), so the name also shows relationship.

2. Write the scientific names of the following organisms: house fly, mouse, dogs, cat & goat.

**Answer:**
- House fly — *Musca domestica*
- Mouse (house mouse) — *Mus musculus*
- Dog — *Canis familiaris* (also written *Canis lupus familiaris*)
- Cat (domestic cat) — *Felis catus*
- Goat — *Capra hircus*

**Why:**
- Each name has genus (capital letter) + specific epithet (small letter), italicized (underlined when handwritten).
- *Musca*, *Mus*, *Canis*, *Felis*, *Capra* are the genera; *domestica*, *musculus*, *familiaris*, *catus*, *hircus* are the epithets.
- The dog is treated by many biologists as a subspecies of the wolf, giving the three-part (trinomial) name *Canis lupus familiaris*.

**Dichotomous keys**

Dichotomous keysare used to identify unfamiliar organisms. They simplify the process of identification. Each key is made up of pairs of contrasting features (dichotomous means two branches), starting with quite general characteristics and progressing to more specific ones. By following the key and making appropriate choices it is possible to identify the organism correctly. Figure 2.3 shows an example of a dichotomous key that could be used to place an unknown vertebrate and unknown invertebrate in the correct class. Item 1 gives you a choice between two alternatives. If the animal is **poikilothermic (cold-blooded),** you move to item 2 and make a further choice. If it is a **homoiothermic (warm-blooded),** you move to item 4 for your next choice. The same technique may be used for assigning an organism to its class, genus or species. However, the important features may not always be easy to see and you have to make use of less fundamental characteristics.

### Activity 2.9: Cooperative Learning

What is the largest group of organisms recognized by biologists? How many such groups should there be? Most biologists used to favor the adoption of two groups. What are these two groups?

**Answer:** The largest group is the **kingdom** (today the domain is above it). The number has changed over time: Linnaeus used two kingdoms; Whittaker (1969) proposed five (Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia), which this book uses; many biologists now also use three domains. The two groups biologists used to favour were the **plant kingdom (Plantae)** and the **animal kingdom (Animalia)**.

**Why:**
- In the Linnaean hierarchy the kingdom is the highest rank; domain (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) was added above it later (section 2.2.2).
- The two-kingdom system failed because bacteria, fungi and many unicellular organisms do not fit well into either plants or animals — the reason for the five-kingdom system.

*Why did we come up with the five kingdoms?*

Classification of organisms into plants and animals was easily done and understood, but a large number of organisms did not fall into either category. Hence the two kingdom classification used for a long time was found inadequate. Besides, gross morphology a need was also felt for including other characteristics like cell structure, nature of cell wall, mode of nutrition, habitat, methods of reproduction, evolutionary relationships, etc.

Whittaker (1969) proposed a five kingdom classification to solve the pitfalls of the two kingdom system of classification. The main criteria for classification used by him include cell structure, body organization, mode of nutrition, reproduction and phylogenetic relationships. Whittaker's five-kingdom scheme consists of animals, plants, fungi, monera and protists(Fig.2.4). It is still not easy to fit all

(p.29)

organisms into the five-kingdom scheme. For example, many protista with chlorophyll (the protophyta) show important resemblances to some members of the algae, but the algae are classified into the plant kingdom.

Above it, a pale-blue zone **Protista** with Rhizopoda, Ciliophora, Mastigophora (flagellates), Bacillariophyta (diatoms), Oomycete, Myxomycete, Dictyostelidae (slime moulds). All lines branch from a single trunk starting in Monera.]

### Did You Know?

Viruses are not included in any kingdom – they are not considered to be living organisms because they lack key characteristics of living things. Viruses are particles that are not alive. They cause diseases and infections. Viruses are made up of nucleic acids, either DNA or RNA, surrounded by a protein coat. They are smaller than the tiniest bacterium. Most biologists agree that viruses are not alive because they don't grow, develop, or carry out respiration. All viruses replicate, or make copies of themselves. However, viruses need the help of living cells to copy themselves. In order to copy itself, a virus must enter a living cell. The cell in which a virus replicates is called the host cell.

### Did You Know?

Most people tend to think of bacteria in terms of illness and disease. However, there are actually only a few disease-causing bacteria compared to the number of harmless and beneficial bacteria. Still, other protists seem to be like fungi except that they do not have the same kind of cell walls that fungi have.

### Activity 2.15: Peer conferencing

Think about places you have seen mushrooms growing. What do those places have in common? Were they hot, dry, cool, or damp? Did the mushrooms appear suddenly or grow slowly over time?

**Answer:** Mushrooms are usually seen in damp, cool, shaded places rich in dead organic matter — on rotting logs and tree stumps, in leaf litter, on animal dung, in moist soil after rain, under trees. They seem to appear suddenly (overnight, especially in the rainy season), but the hidden network of hyphae (mycelium) has been growing slowly in the soil or wood for a long time; the mushroom is only the reproductive structure that pushes up quickly when conditions are right.

**Why:**
- Fungi are heterotrophs that absorb food from dead material (saprophytes), so they grow where dead matter is plentiful.
- They need moisture for growth and spore release, and most avoid strong direct sun, so places are damp, cool and shady rather than hot and dry.
- Fast appearance comes from cells expanding with water, not from new growth starting from zero.

*What are fungi?*

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms that include microorganisms such as yeasts, moulds and mushrooms.

Except for unicellular yeasts, fungi are filamentous multicellular organisms.

Their bodies consist of long, slender thread-like structures called hyphae. Hyphae play an important role in how they obtain food.

Fungi possess a cell wall that is made up of chitin and polysaccharides.

Like animals, fungi are heterotrophic in nutrition. But unlike animals, fungi do not ingest (eat) their food. Instead, a fungus absorbs nutrients from the environment outside of its body. Many fungi accomplish this task by secreting powerful enzymes into their surroundings, digest compounds from a wide range of sources, living or dead. These enzymes break down complex molecules into smaller organic compounds that the fungi can absorb into their bodies and use (Figs 2.9 and 2.10).

[figure: Figure 2.9. Examples of fungi — photo of small white umbrella-shaped mushrooms with gills growing from moss on the forest floor, and a cluster of yellow-orange mushrooms growing on a mossy tree trunk.]

Most fungi absorb soluble organic matter from dead substrates and hence are called saprophytes (decomposers). Decomposer fungi break down and absorb nutrients from nonliving organic material, such as fallen logs, animal corpses, and the wastes of living organisms.

[review: "saprophytes" is an older term (fungi are not plants, "-phyte"); "saprotrophs" is the modern term. Keep both in lessons.]

(p.34)

Fungi cannot make their own food. They are heterotrophs. Fungi use a process called extracellular digestion to obtain nutrients. This means food is digested outside a fungus's cells and then the digested food is absorbed. For example, some hyphae of a fungus will grow into an orange. They release digestive enzymes into the orange that break down the large organic molecules into smaller molecules. These small molecules are absorbed into the hyphae and move into the flowing cytoplasm.

Fungi can also live with different living as parasites or mutualists. Parasitic fungi absorb nutrients from the cells of living hosts. Some parasitic fungi are pathogenic, causing diseases in humans (Example: *Candida albicans* cause rush and *Tinea pedis* cause athlete's foot), animals and plants. Mutualistic fungi also absorb nutrients from a host organism, but they reciprocate with actions that benefit the host. For example, mutualistic fungi that live inside certain termite species use their enzymes to break down wood, making food available for termites. Fungi can also live as symbionts in association with algae as lichens and with roots of higher plants as mycorrhiza.

[review: (1) "Fungi can also live with different living as parasites…" is garbled → "Fungi can also live with other living organisms as parasites or mutualists." Fungi can reproduce either by vegetative means (fragmentation, fission and budding), asexual reproduction through spores formation and sexually. The various spores are produced in distinct structures called fruiting bodies

Kingdom Animalia includes all multicellular, heterotrophic, eukaryotic animals. Constructing a good definition of an animal is

## Unit review

- The characteristics of living things are movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition, etc.
- Classification is a way of sorting organisms into a meaningful order, traditionally using morphology and anatomy, but recently also using DNA (molecular).
- The science of naming, identifying and classifying organisms is known as **taxonomy**.
- **Carolus Linnaeus** (1707–1778), who is also known as the father of taxonomy, introduced a taxonomic hierarchy of classification.
- The taxonomic hierarchies are domains, kingdom, Phylum (division), class, order, family, genus, and species
- The three domains are archaea, bacteria and eukarya (i.e. protists, fungi, plants and animals)
- Classification gives biologists a framework that allows them to study the relationships between living and extinct organisms.
- Taxonomy can be a useful tool for scientists who work in such areas as agriculture, forestry, and medicine.
- The binomial system is an internationally agreed system in which the scientific name of an organism is made up of twoparts showing the genus and the specific epithet.
- In writing a scientific name, the first letter of name of the genus is a capitalized letter and the specific name always starts with a small letter, for example, the scientific name of human beings is *Homo sapiens.* The scientific names are underlined when handwritten or italicized when printed.

**Part One (True or False items):** Say true if the statement is correct false if the statement is wrong

1. Archaea have very different cell walls from bacteria.

**Answer:** True

**Why:**
- The book (p.30): "Archaebacteria (Archaea) have very different cell walls than bacteria."

2. Classification is the process of grouping things based on their similarities.

**Answer:** True

**Why:**

3. Ethiopia is a primary center for *Eragrostis tef* and *Guizotia abyssinica*.

**Answer:** True

**Why:**
- Section 2.5: Ethiopia is a primary centre of diversity for noug (*Guizotia abyssinica*), tef (*Eragrostis tef*) and Ethiopian mustard (*Brassica carinata*).
- [review: the statement should say "primary centre **of diversity** for…" to match the text.]

4. Gymnosperm are seed plant that does not make flowers or fruits

**Answer:** True

**Why:**
- Key terms (p.36): "Gymnosperm: Seed plant that does not make flowers or fruits; for example, a conifer."
- Their seeds are "naked" (not enclosed in an ovary/fruit).

5. Linnaeus' developed a four Kingdom system of classification.

**Answer:** False

**Why:**
- Linnaeus developed a **two**-kingdom system (Plantae and Animalia) — p.22.
- Whittaker (1969) later proposed five kingdoms.

6. Some autotrophic bacteria make their own food the way plants do and they are chemosynthetic.

**Answer:** False

**Why:**
- Bacteria that make food "the way plants do" use light — they are **photosynthetic** (e.g. cyanobacteria).
- Chemosynthetic bacteria make food using energy from chemical reactions, not like plants (p.30).
- Corrected statement: "Some autotrophic bacteria make their own food the way plants do; they are photosynthetic."

7. The Greek philosopher Aristotle (384-322 BC) developed the first widely accepted biological classification systems.

**Answer:** True

**Why:**
- This is stated word for word in section 2.2.2 (he grouped animals by red blood/no red blood and plants into trees, shrubs and herbs).

8. Fungi can reproduce either by vegetative, asexual reproduction through spores formation and sexually.

**Answer:** True

**Why:**
- p.34: fungi reproduce by vegetative means (fragmentation, fission, budding), asexually by spores, and sexually; spores form in fruiting bodies.

9. Many animals have two types of specialized cells not seen in other multicellular organisms.

**Answer:** True

**Why:**
- p.37: muscle cells and nerve cells are found in animals but not in plants or fungi.

10. Unicellular algae are the basis of aquatic food chains and produce much of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere.

**Answer:** True

**Why:**
- Stated in the protists text (p.31): unicellular algae (phytoplankton) are the producers in water and release oxygen by photosynthesis.

**Part Two (Multiple Choices items):** Choose the best answer among the give alternatives.

1. A process by which an organism produces offspring is called........................
   A. reproduction  B. homeostasis  C. development  D. inheritance

**Answer:** A. reproduction

**Why:**
- A is correct: "Reproduction is the process that makes more of the same kind of organism" (p.20).
- B is wrong: homeostasis is keeping internal conditions stable.
- C is wrong: development is the changes an organism goes through as it grows.
- D is wrong: inheritance is passing DNA (traits) to offspring, not producing the offspring.

2. Bacteria that serve as decomposers are
   A. photoautotrophs  B. chemoautotrophs.  C. photoheterotrophs  D. chemoheterotrophs

**Answer:** D. chemoheterotrophs

**Why:**
- D is correct: decomposers get both energy (chemical energy) and carbon from dead organic matter — "chemo" (energy from chemicals) + "heterotroph" (food from other organisms).
- A is wrong: photoautotrophs (e.g. cyanobacteria) make their own food from light and CO₂.
- B is wrong: chemoautotrophs (e.g. sulphur bacteria) make their own food using energy from inorganic chemicals.
- C is wrong: photoheterotrophs take energy from light, so they do not depend on breaking down dead matter for energy.
- [review: the terms photoheterotroph and chemoheterotroph are not explained in the unit (only heterotrophs, photoautotrophs and chemoautotrophs in Figure 2.6); students can reason from "chemo"/"photo" + "auto"/"hetero".]

3. All fungi..........................................
   A. are multicelled  B. are heterotrophs  C. form flagellated spores  D. all of the above

**Answer:** B. are heterotrophs

**Why:**
- B is correct: "Fungi cannot make their own food. They are heterotrophs" (p.34) — this is true of every fungus.
- A is wrong: yeasts are unicellular.
- C is wrong: most fungi have spores without flagella (only a small group, the chytrids, has flagellated spores).
- D is wrong because A and C are not true of all fungi.

4. Most fungi obtain nutrients from ................................................
   A. nonliving organic matter  B. living animals  C. living plants  D. photosynthesis

**Answer:** A. nonliving organic matter

**Why:**
- A is correct: "Most fungi absorb soluble organic matter from dead substrates and hence are called saprophytes (decomposers)" (p.33).
- B and C are wrong: only some fungi are parasites on living animals or plants — not most.
- D is wrong: fungi have no chlorophyll and cannot photosynthesise.

5. Fungal infections are most common in ......................................................
   A. plants  B. mammals  C. insects  D. birds

**Answer:** A. plants

**Why:**
- A is correct: fungi are the main cause of plant diseases (e.g. rusts and smuts of wheat and teff, coffee berry disease, potato late blight-type moulds); far more fungal diseases affect plants than animals.
- B is wrong: mammals do get fungal infections (thrush, athlete's foot) but these are fewer; a warm body temperature and immune system protect mammals.
- C is wrong: some fungi infect insects, but this is less common than in plants.
- D is wrong: fungal infections in birds occur but are relatively uncommon.

6. .............................................. is the transmission of DNA to offspring.

- A is wrong: reproduction produces the offspring; inheritance is what is passed on in it.

**Part Three**: Critical thinking questions

1. How do viruses affect human health?

**Answer:** Viruses are non-living particles that can only copy themselves inside living host cells; when they enter human cells they cause diseases and infections (e.g. common cold, influenza, measles, polio, HIV/AIDS, COVID-19). They are prevented by vaccination, hygiene (hand washing, covering coughs) and avoiding contact with infected people. Some viruses are also useful in medicine and research.

**Why:**
- The Did You Know box (p.29) states that viruses cause diseases and infections and need a host cell to replicate.
- Prevention points are school-level public-health measures.

2. What are the major groups of vertebrates?

**Answer:** Fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

**Why:**
- These are the five classes in Figures 2.13 and 2.15.
- Fish, amphibians and reptiles are cold-blooded; birds and mammals are warm-blooded.

3. What ecological roles do bacteria play?

**Answer:** (1) Decomposers — break down dead organisms and wastes and recycle nutrients; (2) nitrogen fixation — make nitrogen available to plants (e.g. in legume root nodules); (3) producers — photosynthetic cyanobacteria make food and release oxygen; chemosynthetic bacteria are producers in places like deep-sea vents; (4) living in other organisms — help digestion in humans and in cattle (cellulose); (5) some are parasites that cause diseases in plants and animals, which controls population size.

**Why:**
- Points 1–4 come from pp.30–31 (helpful bacteria, nitrogen, oxygen, digestion) and the Did You Know on p.34 (recycling by fungi, bacteria and protists).

4. What ecological roles do fungi play?

**Answer:** (1) Decomposers (saprophytes) — break down dead logs, leaves, animal bodies and wastes and recycle nutrients; without them the world would be overrun with waste; (2) mutualists — in lichens with algae, in mycorrhiza with plant roots (help the plant absorb water and minerals), in termites (digest wood); (3) parasites — cause diseases in plants, animals and humans; (4) food for other organisms.

**Why:**
- All points come from pp.33–34 and the Did You Know box.

5. Write the ecological and economic importance of insects

**Answer:** Ecological: pollinate flowering plants (bees, butterflies); food for birds, reptiles, fish and other animals; decomposers and recyclers (termites, dung beetles, fly larvae); natural pest control (ladybirds eat aphids). Economic: honey and beeswax (honey bee), silk (silkworm); pollination of crops (coffee, fruit, oil seeds); but also pests that destroy crops and stored grain (locusts, armyworms, weevils) and vectors that carry diseases of people and livestock (mosquitoes — malaria; tsetse fly — sleeping sickness/nagana), causing losses.

**Why:**
- These follow the hint list of Activity 2.21 (economic, pollination, pest, vector, etc.).
- A full answer gives both helpful and harmful importance.

6. What structural and functional features do bacteria and archaea share?

**Answer:** Both are prokaryotes: single-celled, small and simple cells; no nucleus (DNA free in the cytoplasm) and no membrane-bound organelles (no endomembrane system); both usually have a cell wall (but of different chemistry); both reproduce asexually by dividing into two, not sexually; both include autotrophs (chemosynthetic) and heterotrophs; both were placed in kingdom Monera.

**Why:**
- The unit review (p.41): "Monera (bacteria and archaea) do not typically have a nucleus or endomembrane system, and they do not reproduce sexually. Members of both groups typically have cell walls."
- Their difference: archaea have very different cell walls and often live in extreme environments.

7. How are plants important?

**Answer:** Plants are producers — they make food by photosynthesis, which feeds almost all other organisms; they release oxygen and take in carbon dioxide; they give food (teff, maize, wheat, enset), medicine, timber, fuel, fibres, oils, coffee and spices; they protect soil from erosion, keep water in catchments and provide habitats; they have ornamental, cultural and spiritual value (e.g. adey abeba at Meskel, church forests).

**Why:**
- Activity 2.16 lists food, oxygen and medicine; Activity 2.18 lists economic, medicinal, ornamental, cultural, spiritual and aesthetic uses.
- The book's key point is that plants are autotrophs (producers) at the base of food chains.

## Notes for lesson design

### Key ideas by subsection
- **2.1 Characteristics of living things:** no one-sentence definition of life; living things show a *set* of features together — made of cells (unicellular/multicellular), need energy (autotrophs/producers vs heterotrophs/consumers), respond to stimuli, grow (permanent increase in size/mass), reproduce (sexually/asexually), excrete metabolic wastes, ordered complexity, homeostasis, adaptations that evolve (Figure 2.1). The unit review uses the MRS GREN list instead.
- **2.2.1 Principles of classification:** classification = grouping by similarity; taxonomy = naming, identifying, classifying; characters used — morphological, anatomical, physiological, molecular (DNA), behavioural, ecological; more similarity → closer relationship.
- **2.2.2 Taxonomic hierarchy:** Aristotle (red blood/no red blood; trees, shrubs, herbs) → Linnaeus (father of taxonomy, two kingdoms, ranked hierarchy). Ranks: domain → kingdom → phylum (division in plants) → class → order → family → genus → species; similarity increases downward (Figure 2.2, human example). Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea (prokaryotes) and Eukarya.
- **2.3 Relevance of classification:** studies relationships of living and extinct organisms (birds–dinosaurs); practical use in agriculture, forestry, medicine (related plants may hold the same useful chemicals, e.g. Moringa); biological species = interbreed to give fertile offspring.
- **2.4 Linnaean nomenclature:** binomial = Genus + specific epithet, Latin, genus capitalised, epithet small letter, italic in print / underlined by hand; one name worldwide solves the problem of many local names (maize: Bekkolo, Boqqollo, Baddela, corn → *Zea mays*).
- **2.5 Ethiopian plants and animals:** high biodiversity and endemism; primary centre of diversity for teff, noug, Ethiopian mustard; Table 1 full classifications (elephant, Ethiopian wolf, gelada, lion, walia, ostrich, wattled ibis, enset, maize, noug, teff, wheat); dichotomous keys — pairs of contrasting features, general → specific (Figure 2.3).
- **2.6 Five kingdoms (Whittaker 1969):** criteria — cell structure, body organisation, nutrition, reproduction, phylogeny. Monera (prokaryotes; eubacteria and archaebacteria; cocci/bacilli/spirilla; heterotrophs, photo- and chemoautotrophs; halophiles, thermoacidophiles, methanogens); Protista (only common feature = eukaryotic; protozoa, algae, fungus-like; Amoeba/pseudopod, Paramecium/cilia, Euglena/flagellum); Fungi (hyphae, chitin walls, heterotrophs with extracellular digestion, saprophytes/parasites/mutualists, lichens, mycorrhiza, yeast for injera and tej/tella); Plantae (multicellular, photosynthetic, cellulose walls, stationary; bryophytes → pteridophytes → gymnosperms → angiosperms (monocots/dicots), Figure 2.12); Animalia (multicellular heterotrophs that ingest food, no cell walls, collagen, muscle and nerve cells; invertebrate phyla and five vertebrate classes, Figures 2.13–2.15). Viruses are in no kingdom.
- **2.7 Ethiopian taxonomists:** Dr Mesfin Tadesse, Prof. Sebsebe Demissew, Prof. Ensermu Kelbessa, Prof. Silesh Nemomissa (plants, *Flora of Ethiopia*), Prof. Abebe Getahun (animals, fishes); few taxonomists compared with Ethiopia's rich biodiversity.

### Common student mistakes
- Thinking one feature (movement, growth) proves something is alive; forgetting that dead/once-living things (dry wood) are not "non-living" in the same sense as stones.
- Mixing up the order of ranks (class vs order — the book itself reverses them on p.25); forgetting domain is above kingdom; using "Genera" as a singular.
- Writing binomials wrongly: small letter for genus, capital for epithet, forgetting italics/underline, or writing only the epithet ("sapiens"); spelling *Eragrostis tef*, *Canis simensis*, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* wrongly (the book has several such misspellings).
- Believing "eukaryotic = single-celled" (book key term error) or that all prokaryotes/Monera are harmful germs.
- Thinking blue-green "algae" are protists or plants — they are cyanobacteria (Monera); confusion over whether algae are plants or protists (book is inconsistent).
- Saying archaea are photosynthetic like plants; confusing photosynthetic and chemosynthetic bacteria (Review Part One Q6).
- Saying fungi are plants (no chlorophyll, chitin walls, absorb food) or that fungi "eat" food like animals.
- Reading couplets of a dichotomous key that do not contrast (Figure 2.3 couplet 3) and the "Reptile" error in the invertebrate key.
- Calling echinoderm skeletons external; claiming every mammal gives live birth and every fish/reptile lays eggs.
- Swapping vertebrates and invertebrates (the unit review itself does so).

### Ideas for interactive practice and interactive pictures
- **Alive or not? sorter:** drag cards (goat, stone, seed, fire, car, crystal, mushroom, dry log, virus, bacterium) into Living / Non-living / Once-living; each drop checks against the characteristics wheel (Figure 2.1) and shows which features are present. Random card sets can be generated from a pool.
- **Hierarchy ladder:** drag rank labels into the correct order (domain → species), then fill a ladder for a random organism from Table 1 (12 organisms × 8 ranks = generated questions such as "What is the order of the gelada?" / "Which two Table 1 organisms share the same family?").
- **Relatedness counter:** pick two random organisms from Table 1; student counts how many ranks they share (e.g. lion and Ethiopian wolf share kingdom, phylum, class, order = 4) → "more shared ranks = closer relatives".
- **Binomial name fixer:** show a randomly mis-formatted name (e.g. "zea Mays", "Homo Sapiens", "eragrostis tef") and ask the student to correct capitalisation/italics; or match local names (Amharic/Afan Oromo/Sidamu Affo) to scientific names.
- **Interactive dichotomous key:** step-through key for the vertebrate and corrected invertebrate keys (Figure 2.3); generate an unknown animal with random features and let students walk the key; extension — students build their own 2-couplet key for 4 school-compound objects.
- **Five-kingdom classifier:** random organism card with 3 clues (nucleus? cell wall material? nutrition? cells?) → choose the kingdom; feedback names the deciding clue. Include tricky cards: Euglena, yeast, mushroom, cyanobacterium, seaweed, sponge.
- **Clickable pictures:** Figure 2.7 protists with hotspots (cilia, pseudopod, flagellum, chloroplasts, nucleus, contractile vacuole); Figure 2.5 bacterial shapes (cocci/bacilli/spirilla) drag-to-label; Figure 2.12 plant-division flowchart where students click yes/no (vascular? seeds? flowers? one or two cotyledons?) to place moss, fern, juniper, teff, coffee; Figure 2.14/2.15 invertebrate and vertebrate cards that flip to show corrected features.
- **Monera/Protista/Fungi/Plantae/Animalia comparison table:** fill-in grid (cell type, cell number, cell wall, nutrition, example) with randomised blanks.
- **True/False and MCQ generator:** reuse the review statements with one detail randomly swapped (e.g. "two"/"four" kingdoms, "photosynthetic"/"chemosynthetic", "chitin"/"cellulose") to generate new items.
- **Ethiopian biodiversity map:** clickable map pins for endemic species (walia ibex — Simien; Ethiopian wolf — Bale; gelada — highlands; teff and noug — crop centres) showing each species' classification.
