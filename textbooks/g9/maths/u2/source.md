## INTRODUCTION

Equations are like a balance weighing machine or scale. If you have seen a weighing machine, you might have thought that an equal amount of weight has to be placed on either side for the scale to be considered "balanced". If you add some weight to one side, the scale will point on one side and the two sides are no more in balance. Equations abide by the same reasonable judgment. Whatever is on one side of the equal sign must have exactly the same value on the other side; otherwise it becomes an inequality.

In the earlier grades, you learned about linear equations in one variable and the methods to solve them. In the present unit, you will further discuss systems of linear equations in two variables, equations involving non-linear equations such as absolute values, exponents and radicals and quadratic equations.

### Definition 2.1

When an equation in one variable has exponent equal to 1, it is said to be a linear equation in one variable. It is of the form ax + b = 0, where x is the variable, and a and b are real coefficients and a ≠ 0. This equation has only one solution.

**For solving a linear equation in one variable, the following steps are followed:**

- Step 1: Use LCM (Least Common Multiple) to clear the fractions if any.
- Step 2: Simplify both sides of the equation.
- Step 3: Isolate the variable.
- Step 4: Verify your answer.

### Example 1

Solve for x, if

a. x + 2 = 0.  b. 3x + 4 = 10

x + 2 + (−2) = 0 + (−2)

x = −2.

b. Similarly, 3x + 4 − 4 = 10 − 4

3x = 6

x = 6/3 = 2 Hence, x = 2

[added step: check a: −2 + 2 = 0 ✓; check b: 3(2) + 4 = 6 + 4 = 10 ✓.]

## 2.2 Systems of linear equations in two variables

### Activity 2.2

Two pencils and one eraser cost Birr 5 and three pencils and two erasers cost Birr 8. Let the price of pencil and eraser be x and y respectively. Express the statements with x and y.

**Answer:** 2x + y = 5 and 3x + 2y = 8 (solving them gives x = 2, y = 1: a pencil costs Birr 2 and an eraser Birr 1).

**Why:**
- 2 pencils cost 2x and 1 eraser costs y, total Birr 5 → 2x + y = 5.
- 3 pencils cost 3x and 2 erasers cost 2y, total Birr 8 → 3x + 2y = 8.
- (Extra) Multiply the first by 2: 4x + 2y = 10. Subtract the second: (4x + 2y) − (3x + 2y) = 10 − 8 → x = 2. Then y = 5 − 2(2) = 1.
- Check: 2(2) + 1 = 5 ✓; 3(2) + 2(1) = 8 ✓.

A system of linear equations consists of two or more linear equations made up of two or more variables such that all equations in the system are considered simultaneously. To find the unique solution to a system of linear equations, we must find a numerical value for each variable in the system that will satisfy all equations in the system at the same time. Some linear systems may not have a solution and others may have an infinite number of solutions. In order for a linear system to have a unique solution, there must be at least as many equations as there are variables. This even does not guarantee a unique solution.

In this section, we will discuss systems of linear equations in two variables, which consist of two equations that contain two different variables.

To solve linear equations with two variables, there are three methods. These are solving using tables, solving by substitution method and elimination method.

(p.92)

### 2.2.2 Solving systems of linear equations in two variables by substitution

One of the methods of solving a system of linear equations is substitution method. The method involves solving one of the equations in terms of the other variable and then substitutes the result into the other equation to solve the second variable.

**Steps in solving systems of linear equations in two variables by substitution**

1. Solve one of the two equations for one of the variables in terms of the other.
2. Substitute the expression for this variable into the second equation, then solve for the remaining variable.

(p.94)

3. Substitute that solution into either of the original equations to find the value of the first variable. If possible, write the solution as an ordered pair.
4. Check the solution in both equations.

### Example 1

Solve the following system of linear equations by substitution method.

{ −x + y = −5
{ 2x − 5y = 1

**Solution:**

First, solve the first equation for y (or solve the second equation for y)

y = x − 5 (Substitute this into the second equation).

2x − 5(x − 5) = 1

[added step: 2x − 5x + 25 = 1]

−3x + 25 = 1

−3x = −24

x = 8

Now, we substitute x = 8 into the first equation or second equation and solve for y.

(−8) + y = −5

y = −5 + 8 = 3.

Therefore, the solution of the above system of linear equations is (x, y) = (8, 3).

[added step: check in the second equation: 2(8) − 5(3) = 16 − 15 = 1 ✓.]

### 2.2.3 Solving systems of linear equations in two variables by Elimination method

Another method of solving systems of linear equations is the addition method, also called the elimination method. In this method, we add two terms with the same variable, but opposite coefficients, so that the sum is zero. Of course, not all systems are set up with the two terms of one variable having opposite coefficients. Often, we must adjust by multiplying one or both of the equations so that one of the variables will be eliminated by addition.

**The following are steps to solve system of equations using the addition (elimination) method.**

1. Write both equations with x and y variables on the left side of the equal sign and constants on the right.
2. Write one equation above the other, lining up corresponding variables. If one of the variables in the top equation has the opposite coefficient of the same variable in the bottom equation, add the equations together, eliminating one variable. If not, use multiplication by a nonzero number so that one of the variables in the top equation has the opposite coefficient of the same variable in the bottom equation, and then add the equations to eliminate the variable.
3. Solve the resulting equation for the remaining variable.
4. Substitute that value into one of the original equations and solve for the second variable.
5. Check the solution by substituting the values into the other equation.

(p.97)

### Example 1

Solve the following using addition (elimination) method.

{ x + 2y = 1

### 2.3.1 Equations involving absolute value

**Review of absolute value**

In the previous sections, you worked with equations having variables x or y that can assume any value. But sometimes it becomes necessary to consider only non-negative values.

If you consider distance, it is always non-negative. The distance of a number x is located on the real line from the origin is either positive or zero. If x is at the origin distance is 0.

**Factorization method 1**

### Activity 2.4

Multiply the left side of each of the following. What do you get? What is the difference between these equations and linear equations?

1. (x + 3)(x − 2) = 0
2. (5x + 1)(2x + 4) = 2
3. ((1/2)x − 3)(x + 5) = 0

**Answer:**
1. x² + x − 6 = 0
2. 10x² + 22x + 4 = 2, i.e. 10x² + 22x + 2 = 0 (or 5x² + 11x + 1 = 0)
3. (1/2)x² − (1/2)x − 15 = 0 (or x² − x − 30 = 0)

Difference: each contains an x² term (highest power of x is 2), so they are quadratic, not linear; a linear equation has highest power 1 and one solution, while these can have up to two solutions.

**Why:**
- 1. (x + 3)(x − 2) = x² − 2x + 3x − 6 = x² + x − 6.
- 2. (5x + 1)(2x + 4) = 10x² + 20x + 2x + 4 = 10x² + 22x + 4; set equal to 2 and subtract 2: 10x² + 22x + 2 = 0; divide by 2: 5x² + 11x + 1 = 0.
- 3. ((1/2)x − 3)(x + 5) = (1/2)x² + (5/2)x − 3x − 15 = (1/2)x² − (1/2)x − 15; multiply by 2: x² − x − 30 = 0.
- Check 1 by solving: x = −3 or x = 2 both make (x + 3)(x − 2) = 0 — two solutions, unlike a linear equation.

### Definition 2.3

An equation of the form ax² + bx + c = 0 where a, b, c ∈ R and a ≠ 0 is a quadratic equation. Here, a is called the leading coefficient, b is the middle term and c is the constant term.

[review: strictly, b is the coefficient of the middle term (the middle term is bx); c is the constant term.]

There are three basic methods for solving quadratic equations: factorization (if possible), completing the square and using the quadratic formula method.

## Notes for lesson design

### Key ideas by subsection
- **2.1 Linear equations in one variable:** form ax + b = 0 (a ≠ 0), exactly one solution; four steps — clear fractions with the LCM, simplify both sides, isolate the variable, verify by substitution; balance-scale model (do the same to both sides); word problems → set up one equation (isosceles triangle perimeter, consecutive numbers).
- **2.2 Systems in two variables:** a solution is an ordered pair satisfying BOTH equations; three methods in the book — tables (find the common (x, y) pair), substitution (solve one equation for one variable, plug into the other), elimination/addition (multiply so one variable has opposite coefficients, add); three outcomes — one solution (lines cross), no solution (false statement like −9 = 4; parallel lines), infinitely many (true statement like 0 = 0; same line); coefficient-ratio test (2x − 3y = a, 4x − 6y = b type); word problems (pens/books, ages, digit problems, "write 3 to the left").
- **2.3.1 Absolute value equations:** |q| = distance from 0; properties |p| = |−p|, |p| ≥ 0, |x| = p (p ≥ 0) ⇔ x = p or x = −p; isolate the absolute value first (e.g. 2|2x − 1| − 1 = 5 → |2x − 1| = 3); |expr| = negative → no solution; |expr| = 0 → one solution.
- **2.3.2 Quadratic equations:** ax² + bx + c = 0 (a ≠ 0); zero-product rule ab = 0 ⇔ a = 0 or b = 0; factorization — common factor, sum–product pairs, perfect squares (one repeated root), difference of squares, splitting the middle term when a ≠ 1; completing the square — divide by a, add and subtract (b/(2a))², write (x + b/(2a))² = (b² − 4ac)/(4a²), take ± square root.

### Common student mistakes
- Forgetting to multiply EVERY term by the LCM (e.g. 4·(1/2) but not 4·(5/4)x).
- Sign slips when moving terms or expanding −5(x − 5) or −3(x − 2y).
- Calling x + 2y = 1 or y = x "linear in one variable"; calling x² + x = 2 linear.
- Stopping after finding one variable in a system; checking only one equation (e.g. (8, 5) satisfies only the first equation of Exercise 2.2 Q2).
- Misreading 0 = 0 as "no solution" or "x = 0", and a false statement as "an error in my work".
- Not rewriting both equations as ax + by = c before eliminating (e.g. 4x − 10 = 6y, x = 9 + y).
- Absolute value: writing only the positive case; not isolating |…| first (e.g. splitting 2|2x − 1| − 1 = 5 directly); "solving" |x + 2| = −6; giving two answers for |x − 2| = 0.
- Quadratics: dividing both sides by x in x² + 3x = 0 and losing x = 0; sign errors in sum–product pairs; writing x = 3 only for x² = 9; in completing the square forgetting to divide by a first or to subtract the added square; forgetting ± when taking square roots.
- Digit problems: confusing the number 10t + u with t + u.

There are quadratic equations that cannot be solved by factorization method. This is generally true when the roots are not rational numbers. Consider the quadratic equation ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0.

Solving for x, (x + b/(2a))² = (b² − 4ac)/(4a²)

x + b/(2a) = ±√((b² − 4ac)/(4a²))

x = −b/(2a) ± √(b² − 4ac)/(2a)

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a)

[added step: how the first line comes from completing the square — divide ax² + bx + c = 0 by a: x² + (b/a)x + c/a = 0; add and subtract (b/(2a))² = b²/(4a²): (x + b/(2a))² − b²/(4a²) + c/a = 0; and −b²/(4a²) + c/a = (4ac − b²)/(4a²). > For quadratic equation ax² + bx + c = 0, a ≠ 0,

#### Discriminant

> **Remark:** When using the quadratic formula, you should be aware of three possibilities. These three possibilities are distinguished by a part of the formula called the **discriminant**. The discriminant is the value under the radical sign which is b² − 4ac. A quadratic equation with real numbers as coefficients can have the following:
>
> The roots of the quadratic equation are: x = (−b ± √D)/(2a), where D = b² − 4ac.
> - If D > 0, then the roots are real and distinct (unequal); the quadratic equation has two distinct roots.
> - If D = 0, then the roots are real and equal (coincident); the quadratic equation has exactly one real root.
> - If D < 0, then there are no real roots.

#### Equations involving radicals

### Definition 2.4

Radical equations are equations that contain variables in the radicand (the expression under a radical symbol)

For example, √(3x + 18) = x, √(x + 3) = x − 3 and √(x + 5) − √(x − 3) = 2 are radical equations.

> **In solving radical equations, the following are steps to follow.**
> 1. Positive radical number and radicand (the expression under a radical symbol) should be positive. Thus, at first, specify the domain of x. That is, √a > 0, a > 0
> 2. Isolate the radical expression on one side of the equal sign. Put all remaining terms on the other side.
> 3. If the radical is a square root, then square both sides of the equation.
> 4. Solve the resulting equation.
> 5. If a radical term still remains, repeat steps 1–2.
> 6. Check solutions by substituting them into the original equation.

[review: step 1 should use "≥", not ">": the radicand and the square root must be non-negative, √a ≥ 0 and a ≥ 0 (the book's own Example 1 uses ≥, and √0 = 0 is allowed). In step 5, "repeat steps 1–2" should really be "repeat steps 2–4" (isolate, square, solve again).]

**For questions 1-5, write true if the statement is correct and false if the statement is not correct**

1. For any rational numbers a, b, and c, then a + b = a + c implies b = c

**Answer:** True.
**Why:**
- Add −a to both sides: (−a) + a + b = (−a) + a + c ⇒ b = c (cancellation law for addition).
- Example: 2 + b = 2 + 5 forces b = 5.

2. For any rational numbers a > 0, the solution set of ax + b ≥ 0 is {x: x ≥ −a/b}.

**Answer:** False.
**Why:**
- ax + b ≥ 0 ⇒ ax ≥ −b ⇒ x ≥ −b/a (dividing by a > 0 keeps the sign).
- The correct solution set is {x : x ≥ −b/a}, not −a/b.
- Counter-example: a = 1, b = 2: x + 2 ≥ 0 ⇔ x ≥ −2, but −a/b = −1/2; x = −1 satisfies x + 2 ≥ 0 yet is not ≥ −1/2.

[review: if the book intended "−b/a" and the answer key says True, the printed −a/b is a typo; as printed the statement is false.]

3. For any rational numbers x & y and a < 0, if x > y, then ax < ay.

**Answer:** True.
**Why:**
- Multiplying both sides of an inequality by a negative number reverses the sign.
- x − y > 0 and a < 0 ⇒ a(x − y) < 0 ⇒ ax < ay.
- Example: 3 > 1, a = −2: −6 < −2 ✓.

4. The equation of the line y = −3x passes through the origin.

**Answer:** True.
**Why:**
- At x = 0, y = −3(0) = 0, so (0, 0) is on the line.

5. The graph of y = c (c < 0) is above the x-axis.

**Answer:** False.
**Why:**
- y = c is a horizontal line whose every point has y-coordinate c.
- c < 0, so the line lies below the x-axis (e.g. y = −2).

## Notes for lesson design

### Key ideas by subsection
- **2.3.2 Completing the square (Ex 2.10):** make the coefficient of x² equal 1, add and subtract (b/2)², write (x + b/2)² = k, then take ±√k; k < 0 means no real roots.
- **Quadratic formula:** x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a), derived by completing the square on ax² + bx + c = 0. Always rewrite the equation in standard form first (Ex 2.13 k, n, o, s, t, w, x).
- **Discriminant D = b² − 4ac:** D > 0 two distinct real roots; D = 0 one repeated root x = −b/(2a); D < 0 no real roots.
- **Roots and coefficients:** r₁ + r₂ = −b/a, r₁r₂ = c/a; build an equation from its roots as x² − (sum)x + product = 0; irrational roots of rational-coefficient equations come in conjugate pairs (Ex 2.14 Q4).
- **Harder quadratic ideas (Ex 2.14):** common root of two quadratics, integral roots via perfect-square discriminant, symmetric systems (x² = 17x + y, y² = x + 17y), absolute value of a quadratic.
- **2.3.3 Exponential equations:** write both sides with the same base and equate exponents (a > 0, a ≠ 1); use the exponent laws (a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ, (aᵐ)ⁿ = a^(mn), etc.).
- **Rational exponents:** x^(p/q) = k — raise to q/p; when p is even there are two answers ± (Example 2 p.117, Ex 2.18 e, Review 29); when the denominator is 2 the base must be ≥ 0.
- **Radical equations:** state the domain (radicand ≥ 0, the side equal to a square root ≥ 0), isolate the radical, square, solve, and ALWAYS check — squaring creates extraneous roots (Examples 1–3, Ex 2.18 l, m).
- **2.4 Applications:** two-variable linear systems for tickets/prices/interest/ages; work-rate problems add rates (1/T) not times; projectile/free-fall models h = −16t² + v₀t + h₀ are solved with factoring or the formula; maximum at t = −b/(2a).
- **Summary/Review:** consistency of 2×2 systems by ratios a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂, c₁/c₂ (dependent, inconsistent, independent).

### Common student mistakes
- Forgetting to divide by a before completing the square (Ex 2.10 e, f), or adding (b/2)² without also subtracting it.
- Taking only the positive square root (losing the ± ), or writing x = −3 ± √2 as one number.
- Using the formula on an equation not in standard form (e.g. 3x² = 6x − 1 with c = −1 instead of +1); sign errors with negative a or b (−b when b is negative).
- Dividing only the √ part by 2a: (6 ± √24)/2 must divide both 6 and √24 — and simplifying √24 = 2√6.
- Saying "no solution" when D < 0 but forgetting to state "no real solution"; miscomputing D when c is negative (−4ac becomes +).
- Mixing up sum −b/a with b/a.
- Exponents: equating bases instead of exponents; writing 1/8 as 2³ instead of 2⁻³; forgetting the bracket in 9^(x+4) = 3^(2x+8).
- x^(2/5) = 4: giving only x = 32 (missing −32); x^(3/2) = 27: inventing x = −9.
- Radical equations: not checking and keeping extraneous roots (x = −5 in Example 1, x = 83 in Example 3, x = −1 in Ex 2.18 m); squaring √a + √b as a + b.
- Work problems: adding times (6 + 10) instead of rates; mixing hours and days (the book itself does this in Examples 3–5).
- Absolute value: forgetting the negative case, or accepting |x² − 1| = −3-type cases.
- Treating "infinitely many solutions" (dependent) as "no solution".

### Ideas for interactive practice and interactive pictures
- **Completing-the-square generator:** pick integers h, k (k may be negative) and build x² − 2hx + (h² − k) = 0; student fills (x − h)² = k, then the roots h ± √k. Extension: multiply by a ∈ {2, 3}.
- **Discriminant classifier:** random a ∈ {±1, ±2, ±3}, b ∈ [−12, 12], c ∈ [−20, 20]; student computes D and taps "2 roots / 1 root / no real roots"; reveal roots.
- **Formula step-builder:** drag a, b, c into slots of (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a); auto-simplify radicals (√72 = 6√2).
- **Build-a-quadratic from roots:** give r₁, r₂ (integers, fractions or p ± √q) → student enters x² − (sum)x + product; also the reverse ("sum is 10, product is 16 — find b, c").
- **Same-base exponent drill:** generate bases 2, 3, 5 with exponents −4…6 (e.g. 4^(ax+b) = 8^(cx+d)); student chooses common base, writes the linear equation, solves.
- **Radical equation with check:** generate √(px + q) = x + r with one valid and one extraneous root; student must mark which root survives.
- **Interactive pictures:**
  - Parabola y = ax² + bx + c with sliders for a, b, c showing x-intercepts appearing/merging/vanishing as D changes sign; display D live.
  - Area model for completing the square: an x-by-x square plus two (b/2)-by-x strips, with the missing (b/2)² corner square.
  - Graphs of y = √(15 − 2x) and y = x (Example 1) — one intersection at x = 3, showing why −5 is extraneous; same for y = √(x + 3) and y = 3x − 1.
  - Projectile graph h = −16t² + 48t + 64 with draggable t showing height, vertex (1.5, 100) and landing time 4 s.
  - Work-rate bar: tanks filling at 1/7 and 1/12 per hour, combined bar fills at 19/84 per hour.
  - Two lines for 2×2 systems with sliders for coefficients: intersecting / parallel / coincident, with the ratio test shown.
  - Number line for |ax + b| = c showing the two points at distance c from −b/a.
